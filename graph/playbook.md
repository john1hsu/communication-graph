## barriers-and-psychology

### [Considering others' contributions first](../articles/barriers-and-psychology/considering-others-contributions-first.md) — Technique

1. Before anyone states or estimates their own share of a piece of team work, write down the name of every person who contributed, including people outside the core team.
2. Next to each name, note one or two concrete things that person did, such as drafts written, analysis run, approvals chased or customers handled.
3. Only after the list is done, write your own contributions in the same concrete terms and estimate your share.
4. If people give percentages, add them up; a total well above 100% is a signal that someone's work is being overlooked, not that someone is lying.
5. In large teams, split the review by sub-team or workstream and make assignments explicit, so each person has fewer colleagues' contributions to recall.
6. When the list shows an uneven split, talk about how work will be divided next time rather than letting the person who carried more quietly resent it.

*A project lead is drafting the end-of-quarter update to a director about a launch that five people worked on.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I drove the launch end to end: I built the rollout plan, fixed the pricing page and got legal sign-off. Shipped on time. | The launch shipped on time. Ana built the analytics dashboard, Raj rewrote the pricing page, Mei ran the legal review, and Tom handled the support handoff. I owned the rollout plan and coordinated the go/no-go calls. |

**Why:** Listing colleagues first surfaces work the lead had half forgotten, gives the director an accurate picture of who to recognise, and still states the lead's real role plainly.

Ask yourself:
- Before I describe what I did on this project, have I named everyone else and what they did?
- If each person on the team wrote down their share, would our numbers add up to more than 100%?
- Whose work am I least likely to remember because it happened out of my sight?
- If the list shows I carried more than others, am I raising the workload split for next time instead of keeping score?

### [Curse of knowledge](../articles/barriers-and-psychology/curse-of-knowledge.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- Which terms, acronyms or steps in this message did I learn on the job rather than expect my reader to know?
- If my reader had only this message and none of my background, what would they do next?
- Have I asked someone outside the work to restate my point in their own words, or am I only judging clarity myself?

### [Egocentric bias](../articles/barriers-and-psychology/egocentric-bias.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- If someone outside my team read this message cold, what would they need that I am carrying only in my head?
- Have I asked the other person what they think, or am I just imagining their view and trusting my guess?
- Before I say how much I did on this project, have I listed what each other person contributed?
- Am I assuming my tone is obvious in this email because I can hear it in my own head?

### [Illusion of transparency](../articles/barriers-and-psychology/illusion-of-transparency.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- Have I actually said the thing I am assuming they already picked up from my tone or face?
- In this email, would a reader who could not hear my voice know whether I was joking, annoyed or serious?
- Did I check what they understood, or did I take their nod as proof?
- Before this presentation, am I treating my racing heart as something the audience can see?

### [Information overload](../articles/barriers-and-psychology/information-overload.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- Does every person on this distribution list need to act on or know this, or am I copying them to cover myself?
- Could the reader act on my first two lines without opening the attachment?
- Am I adding a fifth channel or thread for something that already has a home?
- Have I told my team when I do and do not expect replies, so nobody feels they must watch the inbox all day?
- When I feel swamped, is it the volume of messages, or a lack of clear priorities behind them?

### [Self-distancing strategies](../articles/barriers-and-psychology/self-distancing.md) — Technique

1. Before the meeting or talk, write or say two or three sentences of self-talk using your own name or "you" instead of "I" (for example, "Sam, you know this material; your job is to explain it").
2. Picture the room from the back row or from a colleague's seat, not through your own eyes, and ask what that observer would actually notice.
3. Notice what the observer is busy with - their own agenda, notes and next point - and adjust your estimate of how much attention you will get.
4. Turn the distanced view into one concrete action, such as the opening line, the one question you will ask or the one-sentence correction you will use if you slip.
5. Afterwards, review the moment the same way ("What did Sam do well? What would Sam change?") instead of replaying it in the first person.

*An analyst is about to raise a concern about a vendor's numbers in a cross-team review where she is the most junior person.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'm going to sound naive. Everyone will remember that I'm the one who didn't get the model. I'll just keep quiet and email the lead later. | Priya, picture this from the back of the room: eight people focused on their own slides. You spotted a real gap. Ask one short question about the churn assumption, then let the lead answer. |

**Why:** Naming herself and taking an observer's view shrinks the imagined spotlight and turns vague dread into one specific, low-cost contribution, which is the pattern lab studies link to less distress and better performance.

Ask yourself:
- If I described this moment to myself by name, what would I tell me to do next?
- From a colleague's seat, how much of this meeting is actually about me?
- Am I replaying this slip in the first person, and what would an observer say mattered instead?
- Have I turned the distanced view into one concrete next action?

### [Spotlight effect](../articles/barriers-and-psychology/spotlight-effect.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- Am I staying quiet because I think everyone will remember this one comment, when they are mostly thinking about their own contributions?
- If a colleague had made the slip I just made, would I still be thinking about it tomorrow?
- Am I about to spend the first minute of my talk apologising for something the audience has not noticed?
- If I picture this meeting from the back of the room instead of through my own eyes, how big does my moment really look?

## cross-cultural-communication

### [Describe-analyze-evaluate](../articles/cross-cultural-communication/describe-analyze-evaluate.md) — Technique

1. When a colleague's behaviour surprises or irritates you, pause before replying and write down only what you saw or heard, in words a camera could confirm.
2. List at least two or three different explanations for that behaviour, including at least one that would make it reasonable in the other person's context.
3. Name your own evaluation and its source - what you feel about the behaviour and which norm you are measuring it against.
4. Check the most important explanation with the person or a cultural informant using a neutral question built on your description.
5. Only then decide how to respond, and if you give feedback, lead with the description rather than the evaluation.

*A team lead in a global project notices that a newly transferred engineer never raises objections in design reviews and later sends concerns privately by email.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| You're not engaging in reviews. If you disagree, say it in the room - sending emails afterwards looks like you're going around the team. | In the last three reviews you didn't comment during the meeting, then emailed me concerns afterwards - and they were good ones. I can think of a few reasons that might happen. How do design disagreements usually get raised on teams you've worked on? |

**Why:** The first version presents an evaluation ("not engaging", "going around the team") as fact; the second states the observable pattern, admits several possible explanations and asks before judging, so the engineer can explain a norm the lead may not share.

Ask yourself:
- Could someone who disagrees with me still accept my description of what happened as accurate?
- Have I written down more than one explanation, including one that would make this behaviour sensible where they come from?
- Which of my own norms am I using to judge this, and where did I learn it?
- Am I about to give feedback that starts with my verdict instead of what I observed?

### [Direct vs. indirect feedback](../articles/cross-cultural-communication/direct-vs-indirect-feedback.md) — Concept

Ask yourself:
- If I strip out the softeners or intensifiers, what is the core message I am sending - and would this listener decode it the same way?
- Am I about to deliver criticism in front of people who do not need to hear it?
- Have I checked what the person understood, rather than assuming my politeness or bluntness landed as intended?

### [Ethnocentrism](../articles/cross-cultural-communication/ethnocentrism.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- When I called a colleague's message rude, vague or odd, was I measuring it against how people communicate where I learned to work?
- Would I rate this candidate's or manager's competence the same if their accent, style and background matched mine?
- Have I asked what this behaviour means in the other person's context before deciding what it means?
- Who on this team have I quietly been avoiding or routing around, and is the real reason a difference in style?

## difficult-conversations-and-conflict

### [Apology](../articles/difficult-conversations-and-conflict/apology.md) — Genre

1. Apologize as soon as you know you caused the problem; do not wait until legal, political or face-saving worries have shaped the message.
2. Name the specific thing you did and say plainly that it was your responsibility, without "if anyone was offended" or a passive "mistakes were made".
3. State the concrete effect on the other person (lost time, rework, an embarrassing moment with their client) so they can see you understand the cost to them.
4. Give a short explanation of what went wrong only if it helps them trust the fix, and stop before it turns into an excuse.
5. Offer a specific repair for this instance and a change that prevents a repeat, with an owner and a date.
6. Leave out the demand for forgiveness; then listen to their reaction and follow through visibly on what you promised.

*A project analyst sent a client the wrong version of a cost model, and the client's finance director presented outdated numbers to her own leadership team.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Hi Priya, apologies for any confusion around the model - there were a few version-control issues on our side this week. Hope you can forgive the mix-up! Updated file attached. | Priya, I sent you last month's cost model instead of the final one, and that's on me - it meant you presented figures to your leadership that were about 8% too low. The correct file is attached with the changes highlighted, and I can join a 15-minute call with your CFO tomorrow to walk through the difference. From now on every client file will go out only from the release folder after a second-person check, starting today. |

**Why:** The rewrite names the specific error, takes responsibility in the first person and acknowledges the client's cost, then offers an immediate repair and a preventive change - the two components rated most effective - and drops the request for forgiveness, rated least effective.

Ask yourself:
- Have I said exactly what I did, in the first person, without "if", "but" or a passive construction?
- Does my apology show that I understand what this cost the other person, not just how bad I feel?
- Is my explanation short enough that it cannot be read as an excuse?
- Have I offered a concrete repair and a change with a date, and will I actually follow through?
- Is this a lapse of skill, where apologizing helps, or an accusation about my honesty that I believe is false, where admitting it could backfire?

### [Defensiveness](../articles/difficult-conversations-and-conflict/defensiveness.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- Am I about to describe what happened, or to deliver a verdict on the person who did it?
- Have I signalled that the outcome is still open, or does my message sound like the decision is already made?
- When I felt criticised just now, did I restate their point before I explained or justified myself?
- Is the other person reacting to my content, or to a tone of superiority, certainty or hidden agenda they are picking up?

### [I-statements](../articles/difficult-conversations-and-conflict/i-statements.md) — Technique

1. Before speaking, name one specific, observable behaviour (what was said or done, and when) instead of a trait or a pattern word like "always" or "never".
2. Work out the concrete, tangible effect that behaviour has on you or your work - time lost, rework, a missed commitment.
3. Identify the feeling underneath, and check whether anger is masking something more precise such as worry or frustration.
4. Say it in the first person in roughly this order - behaviour, effect, feeling - and, if useful, a request for what you would like instead.
5. Add one sentence that shows you see the other person's side (their constraints, intentions or pressures) before or after your own view.
6. Stop and listen; expect some defensiveness, reflect back what you hear, and send a clearer second I-statement only if the first one was not understood.

*A project lead raises a recurring problem with a colleague who sends status updates late on the morning of the weekly client call.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| You never get your updates in on time. You're making the whole team look unprepared in front of the client. | I know Mondays are packed for you with the support queue. When the status update reaches me after 9 a.m. on call days, I can't check the numbers before we present them, and I get anxious that we'll give the client something wrong. Could we agree on Friday afternoon as the cut-off? |

**Why:** The rewrite replaces a character verdict ("never", "you're making") with one observable behaviour, its concrete effect and the speaker's own feeling, acknowledges the colleague's constraint, and ends with a specific request - the combination the 2018 rating study found least likely to provoke defensiveness.

Ask yourself:
- Have I described a specific behaviour I could point to, or slipped in a label, a motive or an "always"?
- Is my "I feel…" a real feeling, or a disguised accusation such as "I feel that you don't care"?
- Have I said anything that shows I understand the other person's side of the situation?
- After I speak, am I ready to listen to their reaction rather than repeat my point louder?

### [Role reversal](../articles/difficult-conversations-and-conflict/role-reversal.md) — Technique

1. Agree the ground rule before the discussion starts - each person summarises the other's position, and the other confirms or corrects it, before anyone argues back.
2. Let each side state its own position once, briefly and without interruption.
3. Swap - each person presents the other's position in the first person ("As the ops lead, my concern is...") covering their goal, their reasons and what worries them.
4. Ask the original owner to rate the summary and fill in anything missing or distorted; repeat until they say it is fair.
5. Only then argue your own case, starting from the points you now know you share and naming the points that still genuinely conflict.
6. When preparing alone for a hard negotiation, write or rehearse the other party's brief in their voice and test which of your own arguments would actually move them.

*A product manager and an operations lead are stuck on whether to launch a feature before the quarter-end freeze.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| PM - We have to ship on the 28th; sales has promised it. Ops - You always do this, and my team pays for it on call. PM - It's a small change, you're overreacting. | PM - Let me try your side first. You're worried that launching two days before the freeze leaves no window to roll back, and your on-call team absorbed the last two late launches. Is that right? Ops - Mostly; the bigger issue is we have no runbook yet. Now let me do yours: sales made a commitment to a named customer and slipping costs you credibility. PM - Yes. So can we ship to that one customer behind a flag and do the full launch after the freeze? |

**Why:** Each side states the other's concern until the owner agrees it is fair, which surfaces the real blocker (no runbook) and a shared fact (one customer matters most), and opens an option neither raised while they were only defending their own positions.

Ask yourself:
- Could the other person hear my summary of their position and say "yes, that's it" without correcting me?
- Did I describe their reasons and worries, or only their demand?
- Are our positions actually incompatible, or have we just misunderstood each other - and has the swap made that clearer?
- Am I using the swap to understand them, or to rehearse a rebuttal?
- If I argued their side to someone who shares their values, would it sound persuasive or like a caricature?

### [Social accounts](../articles/difficult-conversations-and-conflict/social-accounts.md) — Technique

1. Give the explanation at the same moment as the bad news or the admission, before people fill the silence with their own story.
2. State the real reason - the constraint, trade-off or goal that drove the outcome - in concrete terms rather than "business reasons".
3. Separate honestly what was outside your control from what was your call, and own your part; never invent a mitigating factor.
4. Say how the decision will be carried out and how the people affected will be treated, not only why it was made.
5. Make the detail proportional to the loss, then ask what still does not make sense and answer it.
6. If you caused the harm, put the account inside an apology - responsibility and repair first, explanation kept short.

*A team lead has to tell five analysts that the conference places she had promised them this year have been cancelled.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Hi all, due to business reasons the conference budget has been cut for this year. Thanks for understanding. | I have to take back the conference places I promised you. Q3 revenue came in 12% under plan, and finance has frozen all non-customer travel across the division until March - I argued to keep two places and lost that argument. Registrations will be refunded by Friday, so none of you are out of pocket, and I've booked 15 minutes with each of you this week to agree what we do instead for your development goals. If any part of that still doesn't add up, ask me directly. |

**Why:** The vague original gives no reason anyone could judge, so it invites cynicism; the rewrite names a specific, checkable cause outside the lead's control, owns the lead's own role, explains how people will be treated, and invites the questions that test whether the account was adequate.

Ask yourself:
- Could the people affected repeat the actual reason for this outcome after hearing my explanation?
- Have I named only causes that are true and that I would be comfortable having checked?
- Did I explain how they will be treated from here, not only why it happened?
- Is my explanation a short context for an apology, or has it become an excuse that replaces one?

## digital-and-async-communication

### [Camera-optional norm](../articles/digital-and-async-communication/camera-optional-norm.md) — Technique

1. List your recurring video meetings and decide for each whether faces add value - small working sessions, sensitive one-to-ones and first meetings with new people usually benefit; large updates, webinars and long all-hands rarely do.
2. Agree the camera expectation for each meeting type with the team, write it in the invite or team charter, and say plainly that cameras-off is fine wherever video is not needed.
3. Tell newer colleagues directly, in person or one-to-one, that turning the camera off will not count against them.
4. Model it as the organiser - switch your own camera off for part of a long meeting or for audio-only segments, because people follow what they see others doing.
5. Keep participation visible without video - ask people by name, use chat, reactions or a shared document, and agree that cameras-off still means no multitasking.
6. When video is used, suggest hiding self-view once framing is set, and build in short audio-only breaks in meetings longer than about 45 minutes.

*A team lead runs a daily 30-minute stand-up and two weekly hour-long planning calls for eight people, several of whom joined in the last three months.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Invite text - 'Cameras on please, it helps us stay connected.' The lead keeps a full gallery view, comments when a tile is dark, and the newest analysts never switch off even on their fourth call of the day. | Team charter line - 'Stand-up: cameras optional. Planning: cameras on for the first 10 minutes of check-in, optional after. 1:1s: your call.' The lead turns their own camera off after the check-in, calls on people by name, and tells new joiners in their first 1:1 that cameras-off is normal here. |

**Why:** The expectation is explicit and tied to the purpose of each meeting, the lead's behaviour shows the norm is real, and the people under the most self-presentation pressure hear it said to them directly, while the check-in keeps the group connection that buffers fatigue.

Ask yourself:
- Have I said out loud, or written in the invite, whether cameras are expected in this meeting and why?
- Would a new team member feel safe turning their camera off here, and have I told them so?
- Am I modelling the norm, or does my own always-on camera quietly signal that video is required?
- When cameras are off, do I still give everyone a clear way to contribute?

### [Channel selection](../articles/digital-and-async-communication/channel-selection.md) — Technique

1. Name the job of the message in one line - is it mainly to pass on information the receiver can absorb alone (conveyance), or to agree on meaning, resolve disagreement or decide something together (convergence)?
2. For conveyance - updates, data, instructions, reference material - default to a written, asynchronous channel the receiver can reread and process in their own time.
3. For convergence - an ambiguous problem, a disagreement, a sensitive or emotional message, or a request you need someone to say yes to - default to a live channel with voice and fast feedback, such as a call, video or a face-to-face conversation.
4. If tone, sarcasm or humour carries part of the meaning, either move to voice or rewrite the text so the meaning survives without your tone of voice; read it aloud in a deliberately flat or opposite tone before sending.
5. Combine channels when the task needs both - send the facts in writing first, discuss live, then confirm the decision and owners in writing.
6. Check the receiver's side - their familiarity with you and the topic, time zone, norms and access - and adjust the default; a lean channel works better between people who already share context.

*A project manager needs a client's sign-off to cut a feature so the release can stay on date, and the client sponsor is known to be attached to that feature.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Email, sent at 6 pm - Subject: Scope change. Hi Priya, given the integration issues we will need to drop the reporting module from the March release. Let me know if that works. Thanks. | Short email first - Subject: Decision needed Thursday - reporting module vs. March date. Priya, I'd like 20 minutes Wednesday to talk through a trade-off: keep the March date by moving the reporting module to April, or keep the module and slip to mid-April. One-page options summary attached. Then a 20-minute call to discuss, followed the same day by a written recap of the decision, owner and new dates. |

**Why:** The facts travel in writing where they can be reread, the contested decision happens live where questions and concerns surface immediately, and the written recap locks in the shared understanding the call produced.

Ask yourself:
- Am I mainly passing information on, or do we need to reach agreement - and does my channel fit that job?
- If my reader misread the tone of this text, what is the worst plausible interpretation, and would a call prevent it?
- Am I choosing email or chat because it suits the message, or because it lets me avoid an awkward conversation?
- After a live discussion, have I put the decision, owners and dates in writing?

### [Scheduled inbox checks](../articles/digital-and-async-communication/scheduled-inbox-checks.md) — Technique

1. Look at how often you check messages today and what your role genuinely needs; if you are on call or in a live-support role, agree an exception path before you change anything.
2. Pick a small number of fixed check-in times (three a day is the most-tested dose) and block them in your calendar, sized to your usual volume.
3. Between check-ins, close the email tab or app and switch off desktop and phone alerts for email and non-urgent chat.
4. During each check-in, triage in one pass - reply, delegate, schedule or archive - rather than reading messages and leaving them for later.
5. Tell your team and key stakeholders when you check messages and how to reach you for anything genuinely urgent (a phone call or a named chat channel).
6. Review after two weeks - stress, missed items, response complaints - and adjust the number of check-ins rather than abandoning the routine.

*A finance analyst gets 120 emails and a steady flow of chat pings a day and is preparing a quarterly forecast that needs long stretches of concentration.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Outlook open all day with pop-up alerts on; the analyst answers each message within minutes and does forecast work in the gaps between replies. | Email and chat alerts off; inbox processed at 9:00, 13:00 and 16:30. The analyst's status and email signature say: 'I check messages three times a day - for anything urgent on the forecast, call my desk line.' |

**Why:** Fewer checks cut the interruptions that fragment the forecast work, and the stated urgent path stops colleagues guessing or escalating, which addresses the co-worker expectations the original study could not control.

Ask yourself:
- Do the people who depend on me know when I will see their message and how to reach me if it cannot wait?
- Did I actually close the inbox and turn off alerts between check-ins, or just check less while staying distracted?
- In this check-in, did I decide what to do with each message, or did I just read and leave them?
- Is my message volume high enough that batching helps, or am I adding rigidity for little gain?
- Has turning alerts off made me more anxious about missing something, and if so would hourly checks suit me better?

### [Setting reply-time expectations](../articles/digital-and-async-communication/setting-reply-time-expectations.md) — Technique

1. Before sending, decide when you truly need an answer - a date and time, or "no deadline" - and whether you need a reply at all or the message is for information only.
2. For real deadlines, state the reply-by time and the reason in the first lines or the subject line (for example "[Action by Thu 3pm]"), not only at the end.
3. For anything non-urgent, add one plain sentence saying so, especially when you send outside normal working hours or across time zones.
4. Reserve urgent labels for messages that genuinely need fast action, and use a phone call or chat ping for true emergencies instead of a late-night email.
5. If you work odd hours, schedule-send to the recipient's working hours or add a standing signature line saying you do not expect replies outside them.
6. Agree default reply windows for each channel with your team (for example chat same day, email within two working days) and write them in the team charter so individual messages only need to flag exceptions.

*A team lead reviewing a draft report on Saturday morning emails comments to an analyst; the comments are needed for a client meeting the following Thursday.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Hi Sam - a few comments on the report below. Can you take a look? | Hi Sam - comments on the report below. No need to look at this before Monday; I need the revised version by Wednesday noon for Thursday's client meeting. Enjoy your weekend. |

**Why:** The original leaves Sam to guess whether a Saturday email means 'reply today'; the rewrite gives the real deadline and its reason and says the weekend is free, the kind of sender-side note that removed receivers' overestimate of urgency in experiments.

Ask yourself:
- Does this message say when I need a reply, or would the reader have to guess?
- Am I sending outside the recipient's working hours, and if so have I said it can wait or scheduled it?
- Is the urgency I am signalling the urgency I actually have?
- Would my team know our default reply window for this channel without asking?

### [Video call communication](../articles/digital-and-async-communication/video-call-communication.md) — Channel

Ask yourself:
- Does this conversation need faces and live back-and-forth, or would a phone call, a shared document or a short written update do?
- If the goal is to generate ideas, have I given people a way to think away from the screen, or should we meet in person?
- Is my audio clear enough that a stranger would take me seriously, and have I fixed it before worrying about my background?
- Did I tell people at the start how to ask to speak and in what order I will take contributions?
- After I asked a question, did I leave enough pause for the lag before filling the silence or moving on?

### [Zoom fatigue](../articles/digital-and-async-communication/zoom-fatigue.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- Does this conversation need video at all, or would a phone call, a shared document or a written update do the job?
- Have I booked video meetings back to back with no break to stand up, move or look away from the screen?
- Am I watching my own tile more than the people I am talking to, and could I hide self-view?
- Did I make it clear that cameras are optional for this meeting, especially for newer team members?
- Did people have a moment to connect as a group, or did we go straight into a wall of faces and slides?

## executive-and-client-communication

### [Communicating uncertainty to decision makers](../articles/executive-and-client-communication/communicating-uncertainty.md) — Skill

*A product lead tells the leadership team how many customers a pricing change will cost.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Churn should be fine - we don't expect much impact, though obviously nobody can be sure. | Our best estimate is that 3% of accounts cancel in the first quarter, with a plausible range of 1-6%. The estimate rests on a four-week pilot in one region, so confidence is moderate. We will know much more after the second region's renewals on 15 March; until then I'd budget for 6%. |

**Why:** The before version mixes false reassurance with a blanket hedge, so leaders can neither plan nor challenge it; the after version gives a point estimate, a range, the quality of the evidence and when it will improve, which is what a decision needs.

Ask yourself:
- Have I given a number or range where I have one, instead of a word like "probably" or "should be fine"?
- Have I said separately how good the evidence behind my estimate is?
- Have I told them what we do not yet know, what we are doing to find out and when we will know?
- Am I presenting real disagreement among experts or data as such, rather than smoothing it over or overplaying it?
- Is the uncertainty in the sentence that carries the estimate, not in a footnote or a later slide?

### [Managing up](../articles/executive-and-client-communication/managing-up.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- Can I name my manager's top two goals and the pressure they are under this quarter, from what they have told me rather than my guess?
- Do I know whether my manager would rather read this in writing first or hear it live, and am I using that format?
- Have we explicitly agreed what "done" looks like and when they want to hear from me, or am I assuming?
- Is there a problem or slipping date I have not told them about yet, and why am I waiting?
- Am I spending their time and goodwill on something that truly needs them, and have I brought a proposed solution?
- Would I be comfortable if my manager saw exactly how I prepared for this conversation, or does it rely on flattery or spin?

### [Project status reporting](../articles/executive-and-client-communication/status-reporting.md) — Genre

1. Agree with the sponsor, in writing, what each status rating means (for example what turns a project amber or red) before the first report goes out.
2. Open with the overall rating, the one-line reason for it and any decision you need, with a date.
3. Report against the approved baseline for time, cost, scope and benefits, and show the change since the last report rather than only the current position.
4. For each significant issue, state the cause, what you have already done, the options and your recommendation.
5. Raise a slipping milestone in the first report where you believe it is at risk, not the one where it is missed.
6. After each review, ask the reader whether the report gave them what they needed and adjust the format, not the facts.

*A team lead on a six-month CRM migration sends a fortnightly status report to the steering group; a data-quality problem has put the go-live date at risk.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Status: GREEN. Good progress this sprint - 14 of 18 integration tests passing. Some data-quality items being worked through with the vendor; we remain confident about the timeline and will update next report. | Status: AMBER (was GREEN). Go-live on 2 March is at risk: 22% of migrated customer records fail validation, and at the current fix rate cleanup finishes about three weeks late. Done so far: vendor engaged, root cause traced to duplicate legacy IDs. Options: (a) add two analysts for four weeks (about $18K) and hold the date; (b) move go-live to 23 March at no extra cost. Recommendation: (a). Decision needed from the steering group by Friday. |

**Why:** The rewrite applies the agreed rating honestly, quantifies the problem instead of hiding it in reassurance, shows what has already been done, and turns the bad news into a decision the readers can make while there is still time to act.

Ask yourself:
- Would someone who knew everything I know give this project the same rating I have?
- Is there a risk I am privately worried about that does not appear in this report, and why have I left it out?
- Does the first line tell a busy sponsor whether they need to act, and by when?
- Am I reporting change against the approved baseline, or only selecting the numbers that look good this week?

### [Risk numbers as natural frequencies](../articles/executive-and-client-communication/risk-numbers-as-natural-frequencies.md) — Technique

1. Pick one reference group large enough that every count comes out as a whole number (1,000 or 10,000 is usual) and name it in the first sentence.
2. Split that group by the base rate first - how many have the condition, defect or event and how many do not.
3. Apply the detection or hit rate inside the first subgroup and the false-alarm rate inside the second, and write each result as a count.
4. Answer the decision question as "X out of Y" - for example, how many of all flagged cases are real - instead of quoting the test's accuracy.
5. Keep the same denominator for every option you compare, and give absolute changes ("from 20 to 10 in 1,000") rather than relative ones ("halved").
6. Tie every number to its group in the wording ("of the 9,980 legitimate orders, about 500 are flagged") and, for a live audience, sketch the counts as a simple frequency tree.

*A risk analyst briefs the head of operations on whether to auto-cancel every order a new fraud model flags.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The model catches 90% of fraud with only a 5% false-positive rate, so a flagged order is very likely fraudulent - I'd auto-cancel them. | Out of every 10,000 orders, about 20 are fraudulent and the model flags 18 of them. Of the other 9,980 legitimate orders it also flags about 500. So of roughly 518 flagged orders, only 18 - about 1 in 30 - are fraud. Auto-cancelling would block about 500 good customers to stop 18 bad ones; I suggest manual review instead. |

**Why:** The before version quotes the model's accuracy and invites the listener to ignore how rare fraud is; the counts make the base rate and the false alarms visible, so the leader can see that most flags are wrong and decide on that basis.

Ask yourself:
- Have I stated the reference group ("out of every 1,000 ...") before giving any count?
- Can my listener see how many cases are false alarms, not just how many real cases are caught?
- Am I comparing options with the same denominator and in absolute terms rather than "doubled" or "halved"?
- Does every number in my sentence clearly belong to one named group?
- If someone asked "so of the ones flagged, how many are real?", have I already answered it?

### [Trusted advisor](../articles/executive-and-client-communication/trusted-advisor.md) — Framework

*A consultant opens a steering-committee call with a client sponsor whose project is behind schedule.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Thanks for the time. Let me walk you through our 30-slide progress deck, and at the end I'd like to talk about extending our team for phase two. | Before the deck: last week you said the board is worried about the March date. Is that still the main concern? I'll focus on what protects March, including one option that needs less of our time, not more. |

**Why:** The rewrite shows reliability (it remembers what the sponsor said), intimacy (it names the real worry) and low self-orientation (it puts the client's problem ahead of selling more work).

Ask yourself:
- Before this meeting, can I state the client's real worry in their words rather than my summary of the scope?
- Have I delivered every small promise I made since we last spoke, and told them early about any I will miss?
- Is any part of what I plan to say mainly about my firm's revenue, my reputation or being right?
- Have I listened long enough to earn the right to frame the problem, or am I jumping to my solution?

### [Words of estimative probability](../articles/executive-and-client-communication/words-of-estimative-probability.md) — Technique

1. Agree a short likelihood scale with your team or client before you need it - for example remote (under 5%), unlikely (20-45%), roughly even (45-55%), likely (55-80%), very likely (80-95%) - with ranges that do not overlap.
2. Before writing a forecast, decide your own number or range first, then pick the one word on the scale whose range contains it.
3. Put the numeric range in brackets right after the word in the text itself ("likely (55-80%)"), not in a footnote, appendix or hover text.
4. Keep likelihood (how probable the event is) separate from confidence (how solid your evidence is), and say each in its own sentence.
5. Reserve "possible", "could" and "may" for cases where you genuinely cannot estimate the odds, and say so rather than using them as softeners.
6. Use the same words with the same meanings in every report, and add a short key whenever a new reader joins the distribution.

*A consultant updates a client steering committee on whether a system migration will hit its go-live date.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| There is a real possibility we could miss the March 1 go-live, although the team is working hard and it may well still be achievable. | We judge it unlikely (20-45%) that we hit the March 1 go-live; very likely (80-95%) by March 22. We have moderate confidence: the estimate rests on two weeks of test-defect data. Decision needed by Friday: hold the date with reduced scope, or move it. |

**Why:** The before version could be read anywhere from a small risk to a probable slip; the after version gives one shared range, separates odds from confidence, and turns the forecast into a decision the committee can make.

Ask yourself:
- If three readers each wrote down the percentage they think my likelihood word means, would their numbers land in the same range as mine?
- Have I put the number next to the word, or am I relying on readers to look up a key they will probably skip?
- Am I using "could", "may" or "possible" to hedge when I actually have an estimate I am reluctant to commit to?
- Have I kept my confidence in the evidence separate from the likelihood of the event?

## feedback-and-coaching

### [Feedback sandwich](../articles/feedback-and-coaching/feedback-sandwich.md) — Technique

1. Decide whether a sandwich is needed at all - routine corrections in a healthy working relationship can be given on their own, and praise can be given separately when it is earned.
2. Pick one specific, important correction; if there are several, the issue is bigger than a sandwich and needs a fuller conversation.
3. Open with sincere praise for a specific behaviour you want the person to keep, ideally related to the same piece of work.
4. State the correction plainly as an observable behaviour, not a judgement of the person, and offer a concrete alternative.
5. Close with genuine, specific encouragement - not a generic compliment - and invite the person to respond, then paraphrase what they say.
6. Afterwards, check that the correction was heard by asking what they will do differently next time.

*A team lead reviewing an analyst's first client slide deck, which is well researched but hides the recommendation on slide 14.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Great job overall, really nice work! Just one tiny thing, maybe think about the order a bit? But honestly it's great, the client will love it. | Your market-sizing section is the strongest I've seen from a new analyst - the sources are solid. The main fix: the recommendation is on slide 14, so the client won't see it until the end; move it to slide 2 and let the rest support it. Your data work makes that recommendation easy to defend. What do you think of moving it? |

**Why:** The first version wraps a vague correction in generic praise, so the analyst may hear only "great job"; the rewrite keeps the positive-corrective-positive shape but makes every part specific, states the fix as an action, and checks it landed.

Ask yourself:
- Is my praise specific and sincere, or am I using it only to cushion the criticism?
- If the person repeated back only one sentence from this conversation, would it be the correction?
- Am I sandwiching because the feedback needs it, or because I am uncomfortable saying it?
- Do the people I manage now brace for a "but" whenever I praise them?

### [Feedforward](../articles/feedback-and-coaching/feedforward.md) — Technique

1. Pick one specific behaviour you want to get better at, phrased as a future goal ("run tighter project kick-offs"), not a past failure.
2. Describe that goal briefly to a colleague who has seen you work or who knows the subject - they do not need to be your manager.
3. Ask for two concrete suggestions for the future, and make clear you are not asking for a review of anything that already happened.
4. Listen and write the suggestions down without judging, explaining or defending - the only permitted reply is "thank you".
5. Offer to return the favour - ask what they want to improve and give them two forward-looking suggestions in turn.
6. Repeat with a few different people, then choose the suggestions you will actually try and tell those colleagues what you changed.

*A project manager whose kick-off meetings regularly overrun wants to improve before the next quarter's projects start.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Can you give me some feedback on last week's kick-off? I know it ran 40 minutes over, but the client kept raising scope questions, so honestly I'm not sure what I could have done differently. | I'm working on running kick-offs that finish on time. Could you give me two ideas I could try at the next one? I'll just note them down - no need to go back over last week. |

**Why:** The first version invites a debate about a past meeting and opens with a defence, so the colleague has to argue to be helpful; the rewrite names a future goal, asks for a bounded number of ideas and promises not to argue, which makes it easy to offer useful suggestions.

Ask yourself:
- Have I named one behaviour I want to improve in the future, rather than asking for a verdict on the past?
- When a suggestion arrived, did I say "thank you" - or did I explain why it would not work?
- Am I using feedforward to avoid a conversation about a real problem that still needs to be discussed?
- Which of the suggestions will I actually try, and have I told the people who gave them?

### [Receiving feedback](../articles/feedback-and-coaching/receiving-feedback.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- Before I judged this feedback right or wrong, could I say back exactly what they observed and what they want me to do differently?
- Am I reacting to what was said, to who said it, or to what it seems to say about who I am?
- Did I ask for one concrete, forward-looking suggestion instead of a general request for any feedback?
- Have I separated the useful coaching from the parts I disagree with, instead of rejecting the whole message?
- Did I close by saying what I will try, or that I want to think it over and come back?

### [Self-affirmation before feedback](../articles/feedback-and-coaching/self-affirmation-before-feedback.md) — Technique

1. Before a review, debrief or round of comments, set aside five minutes somewhere you will not be interrupted.
2. Pick one personal value that has nothing to do with the work being judged, such as family, friendship, craft outside work or community.
3. Write for about five minutes about why that value matters to you and a recent time you acted on it; do not write about the project.
4. Go into the conversation and, when the critical point lands, ask what they observed and what they suggest before you defend or explain.
5. Afterwards, list the one or two changes you will try, separating them from the points you still disagree with.

*A product manager is about to hear a director's comments on a launch that slipped two weeks.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| She walks in braced, interrupts the first critical point to explain the vendor delay, and leaves having heard only that the director thinks she planned badly. | Beforehand she writes for five minutes about why mentoring two junior colleagues matters to her. When the director says the risk log was stale, she asks which risks were missed and agrees to a weekly review, noting the vendor issue separately. |

**Why:** Reflecting on a value outside the project keeps one critical comment from feeling like a verdict on her whole worth, so she can hear the specific fix instead of defending her identity.

Ask yourself:
- Did I affirm a value unrelated to the work being judged, rather than reassuring myself that the work is fine?
- Did I do the reflection before the feedback, not as damage control afterwards?
- When the hardest comment came, did I ask a question before I explained or justified?
- Am I treating this as a small boost to openness, not a substitute for clear, fair feedback from the giver?

### [Situation-Behavior-Impact](../articles/feedback-and-coaching/situation-behavior-impact.md) — Technique

1. Pin down the situation - the specific meeting, document or moment, with a time and place ("in Tuesday's 10 a.m. client call"), not "lately" or "last week".
2. Describe the behaviour as something a camera could have recorded - words said, actions taken - and strip out labels such as "rude", "lazy" or "great attitude".
3. State the impact the behaviour had on you, the team or the work, in your own voice, naming a concrete consequence and, if useful, your reaction (frustrated, relieved, proud).
4. For redirecting feedback, stop and ask about intent ("What were you aiming for there?") and listen before proposing any fix.
5. Agree together on what to keep doing or what to try next time, and give the feedback close to the event while it is still fresh.
6. Use the same three parts for positive feedback, so the person knows exactly which behaviour to repeat.

*A team lead gives feedback to an analyst who presented the quarterly numbers at a leadership review.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| You came across as really unprepared today and kind of defensive. You need to step up your game before the next one. | In this morning's quarterly review with the VPs, when Priya asked where the churn figure came from, you said 'I'd have to check' and moved on. The VPs then questioned two other numbers and we lost ten minutes of the agenda, and I was worried they'd discount the whole forecast. What was going on for you at that point? |

**Why:** The rewrite swaps verdicts ("unprepared", "defensive") for a time-stamped situation, an observable behaviour and a concrete impact, then asks about intent - so the analyst can see exactly what to change and has room to explain instead of defending their character.

Ask yourself:
- Could the other person picture the exact moment I mean, or did I say something vague like "lately"?
- Is my behaviour description something a camera could record, or did I slip in a judgement or guess at a motive?
- Have I said what the impact actually was, rather than assuming they already know?
- Did I leave room to ask about their intent and listen before deciding what should change?

## foundations

### [Audience analysis](../articles/foundations/audience-analysis.md) — Technique

1. List every audience, not just the addressee - the decision maker, technical reviewers, people who are copied, people the message may be forwarded to and people affected by the outcome.
2. For the primary audience, write down what they already know, what they care about, what attitude they are likely to bring and what they must decide or do after reading.
3. Check your assumptions against evidence - the questions people actually ask you, past replies, usage data or a short conversation with a typical reader.
4. Choose content, order, vocabulary, tone and channel to fit the primary audience, putting what matters to them first.
5. Give secondary audiences their own clearly labelled section instead of mixing material for different readers.
6. Reread the draft as each audience in turn - including someone it might be forwarded to - and fix what would confuse or alienate them.

*An engineering lead emails a delay notice about a data-platform migration to a list that includes the client's finance director.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Heads up - the ETL refactor slipped because the CDC connector kept OOMing on the backfill. New ETA is sprint 14, assuming infra gets us the bigger nodes. | Subject: Data migration finishing three weeks later than planned (no change to cost). The move to the new reporting system will finish on 4 December instead of 13 November. Your month-end reports will run on the old system as usual until then. Technical details for your IT team are in the section below. |

**Why:** The finance director is the primary reader and needs the date, the impact on her work and the cost; the jargon and team shorthand move to a separate section for the technical secondary audience.

Ask yourself:
- Who will read or hear this - including people copied or forwarded to - and which of them is the one who must act?
- What does my primary audience already know, and which of my terms or steps would they not recognise?
- What is in this for them, and do they see it in the first few lines?
- Have I checked my picture of the audience against something they actually said or did, or am I only guessing?

### [Grice's cooperative principle](../articles/foundations/grices-maxims.md) — Principle

Ask yourself:
- If my reader took every word literally and also read between the lines, what would they conclude that I did not intend?
- Did I use a weaker word such as "some", "most" or "should" where a stronger one was true, and so imply the stronger one is false?
- Does every sentence I am about to send answer the question that was actually asked?
- Have I stated anything I believe but cannot back up, without flagging it as a belief?
- Is there detail here the reader does not need to act, and detail they need that I left out?

### [Rapport](../articles/foundations/rapport.md) — Concept

Ask yourself:
- Did I give this person my full attention, or was part of me on my screen or my next point?
- Did I learn one thing about them as a person before we got into the business?
- Before a hard negotiation or tense thread, have we ever spoken live, even for five minutes?
- Did the setting and their cues invite small talk, or did they signal they wanted to get straight to work?
- Am I trying to connect, or performing techniques such as copying their posture?

## listening

### [Active listening](../articles/listening/active-listening.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- Could I state their point, and how they feel about it, well enough that they would say "yes, exactly"?
- Did I hold back advice, reassurance and my own counter-argument until they confirmed I understood?
- Was my attention on them, or was I rehearsing my reply or glancing at a screen?
- Did I ask at least one question that drew out something they had not yet said?
- Would they leave this conversation feeling heard, even if I did not agree with them?

### [Interrupting](../articles/listening/interrupting.md) — AntiPattern

*In a weekly project meeting, a junior analyst starts explaining why the vendor data looks wrong, and the team lead jumps in.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Analyst: 'The Q3 numbers don't reconcile because the vendor changed—' Lead: 'Yeah, vendors always do that, let's just use last quarter's file. Next item.' | Analyst: 'The Q3 numbers don't reconcile because the vendor changed—' Lead: [waits until she stops] 'So the vendor changed something in the feed. What changed, and what does it mean for the forecast?' |

**Why:** In the first version the lead takes the floor mid-sentence and closes the topic, so the analyst loses both the point and the standing to raise it again; in the second he lets her finish, summarises, and hands the floor back, so the actual cause surfaces.

Ask yourself:
- Did I start talking before the other person had finished their sentence or their point?
- When I came in over someone, was it to agree and build on what they said, or to change the subject or win the argument?
- In this meeting, who got cut off early, and did anyone, including me, hand the floor back to them?
- If I was interrupted, did I calmly reclaim the turn ("Let me finish this point, then I'd like your view")?

### [Open-ended questions](../articles/listening/open-ended-questions.md) — Technique

1. Decide what you actually need to learn - their view, the obstacles, the options - before you ask anything.
2. Open with "what" or "how" ("What's getting in the way?", "How did the client react?") instead of "Is", "Do" or "Did".
3. Stop talking and let them answer fully; resist offering a suggested answer if they pause.
4. Build your next question on what they just said ("You mentioned the handover - what happened there?") rather than switching topic.
5. Reflect back what you heard before asking another question, so the exchange does not become an interrogation.
6. Switch to a closed or specific question when you need a commitment, a number, or a straight answer to a known risk ("Will this slip past the 30th?").

*A team lead checks in with an engineer on a database migration that has gone quiet for a week.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Is the migration still on track for the end of the month? | What's the part of the migration you're least confident about right now? |

**Why:** The closed question invites a reflexive "yes"; the open one asks for an explanation and presumes there is some uncertainty, which makes it easier to surface a problem early.

Ask yourself:
- Could the other person have answered my question with one word, and was that what I really wanted?
- Did my next question build on their answer, or did I jump to my own agenda?
- Did I ask a "why" question where a "what" or "how" would have felt less like a demand for justification?
- When I needed a firm answer on a specific risk, did I ask a direct, specific question instead of a vague one?

### [Paraphrasing](../articles/listening/paraphrasing.md) — Technique

1. Listen to the whole point without planning your reply; note the facts, what the speaker seems to feel and what seems most important to them.
2. Wait for a natural pause, then restate the core of their message in one or two sentences, using your own words rather than echoing theirs.
3. Where feelings are clearly present, name the feeling and its cause tentatively ("it sounds like the late change left you frustrated").
4. End with a check question such as "Have I got that right?" or "What did I miss?" and accept corrections without arguing.
5. If they correct you, paraphrase the corrected version; follow up with an open question if something is still unclear.
6. Only once they confirm you have understood, give your own perspective, answer or proposal.

*An operations lead tells a project manager that the new vendor's delivery schedule will not work for the warehouse, and the PM's instinct is to defend the schedule.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The schedule was signed off by procurement last month, so we really can't change it now. Let's just make it work. | So if I've understood you, the Tuesday deliveries land on the same morning as the weekly stock count, which means your team either delays the count or leaves trucks waiting - and you're worried we'll start paying demurrage. Is that the main problem, or is there more to it? |

**Why:** The rewrite restates the specific facts and the concern behind them in the PM's own words and invites correction, so the operations lead can confirm or fix the picture before anyone argues for a solution.

Ask yourself:
- Did I restate their point in my own words, or did I just repeat their phrases back like a parrot?
- Did I capture what mattered to them and how they felt, not only the facts?
- Did I end with a genuine check ("Have I got that right?") and accept a correction without defending myself?
- Did I hold back my own view until they agreed I had understood them?

### [Perspective-taking](../articles/listening/perspective-taking.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- Can I state what the other person is trying to achieve and what constrains them, without using my own goals?
- Which of my beliefs about their view did I actually hear from them, and which did I only imagine?
- What one question would confirm or overturn my best guess about their position?
- Am I trying to understand their reasoning, or just feeling sorry for them in a way that will make me give ground I need?
- Has imagining their side made me more suspicious or more competitive than the facts warrant?

### [Wait time](../articles/listening/wait-time.md) — Technique

1. Ask one question, then stop talking - do not rephrase it, add a second question or offer a sample answer.
2. Count silently to at least three (longer for a hard or open question, or when a group is involved) before saying anything else.
3. If the silence runs on, re-invite rather than rescue - "Take a moment; I'd rather hear a considered answer" - and keep waiting.
4. When the person finishes answering, pause again for a few seconds before you reply, so they can add the second thought that often matters most.
5. In a meeting, say up front that you will wait before taking answers, so the first fast voice does not set the direction.
6. When you are asked a hard question, give yourself the same pause - say you want a moment to think - instead of filling the gap with the first answer that comes to mind.

*A product manager runs a project retrospective with six engineers and wants to hear what really slowed the release.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| So, what slowed us down? ... Was it the test environment? Or maybe the late spec changes? OK, Priya, the environment, right? Great - next item. | What slowed us down most on this release? I'll give everyone a few seconds to think before we start. [silent count to five] ... [after Priya answers, another three-second pause] ... Anything else behind that? |

**Why:** In the first version the manager fills the silence with guesses and accepts the first answer, so the group responds to his options; the pauses in the second version give quieter people time to form a view and give the first speaker room to add the underlying cause.

Ask yourself:
- After my last question, did I actually stay silent for at least three seconds, or did I rephrase, add options or answer it myself?
- When someone finished speaking, did I pause before replying, or did I jump straight to my next point?
- Did the answers I got come mostly from the fastest speakers, and would more think time have widened who spoke?
- When I was asked something difficult, did I take a moment to think, or did I answer to escape the silence?

## meetings-and-facilitation

### [1-2-4-All](../articles/meetings-and-facilitation/1-2-4-all.md) — Technique

1. Write one open, specific invitation the group can act on, such as "What opportunities do you see to cut our release lead time?", and show it to everyone.
2. Give one minute of silent, individual reflection in which each person writes their own ideas; protect the silence and do not talk over it.
3. Have people pair up for two minutes to share and build on each other's ideas, keeping different ideas distinct rather than forcing agreement.
4. Merge pairs into groups of four for about five minutes to compare ideas and pick out the ones that stand out.
5. Bring everyone back and ask each group for one important idea that is not yet on the board, recording each one, until the strongest ideas are visible.
6. Close by naming what the group will do with the ideas - prioritise them, assign owners or feed them into a decision - and debrief briefly with a co-host on how the process went.

*A team lead runs a 30-minute retrospective with ten engineers, two of whom are senior and usually speak first, to find ways to reduce late-night production incidents.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| So, what should we change to cut down on incidents? ... Good point, Priya [senior engineer], more alerting makes sense. Anyone else? No? Let's take that as the action. | Take one minute on your own and jot down every change you think would cut late-night incidents. Now share with the person next to you for two minutes, then join another pair for five. Each group of four, give me the one idea we haven't heard yet. |

**Why:** The open question lets the first senior voice set the answer and the quiet engineers never speak; reflecting alone and then in pairs means everyone has an idea and a low-risk audience before the whole room hears anything, so more and more varied options surface.

Ask yourself:
- Was my invitation specific enough that someone could write concrete answers to it in one minute?
- Did I protect the silent reflection, or did someone (including me) start talking before everyone had written?
- In the all-group share, did I ask for new ideas from each group rather than letting the most confident group report everything?
- Did I steer the content of the small-group conversations, or did I limit myself to running time and process?

### [Facilitation](../articles/meetings-and-facilitation/facilitation.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- Before we started, did the group agree the goal, the agenda and how we would take turns, or did I assume everyone knew?
- Who got cut off or stayed silent, and did I hand the floor back to them by name?
- Did I summarise where we had landed before moving to the next item, and check that the summary was right?
- Did I keep my own opinions out of the content, or did I steer the group toward the answer I wanted?
- Did the meeting end with named owners and next steps for each decision?

### [Meeting agendas](../articles/meetings-and-facilitation/meeting-agendas.md) — Technique

1. Ask the people who will attend what they need the meeting to settle, and include only items that need this whole group.
2. Write each item as a question the group must answer, not a topic label.
3. Mark each item as share, seek input or decide, and name who leads it and how many minutes it gets.
4. Use the questions to set the invite list - invite only the people needed to answer them - and cancel if you cannot write any questions.
5. Circulate the written agenda, with any pre-reading, far enough ahead for people to prepare.
6. Open the meeting by restating its purpose, follow the agenda, and end when the questions are answered.

*A team lead schedules a 45-minute meeting with six colleagues about next quarter's office move.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Agenda: 1. Office move update 2. Desk allocation 3. AOB | Purpose: agree the move plan. 1. (Decide, Priya, 15 min) Which two weeks in March should we move? 2. (Seek input, Tom, 20 min) Under what conditions should we reallocate desks to hybrid staff? 3. (Share, me, 5 min) What changes for parking? Please read the floor plan before Tuesday. |

**Why:** Questions show what counts as done, the labels tell people whether they are deciding or just listening, and the named owners and pre-reading let them arrive prepared instead of reacting cold.

Ask yourself:
- Could each attendee tell from the agenda alone what the meeting must decide and what they should prepare?
- Is every item phrased as a question that needs this whole group, or could some be handled by email?
- Did the agenda reach people early enough for them to actually prepare?
- At the end, can I point to an answer for each question on the agenda?

### [Nominal group technique](../articles/meetings-and-facilitation/nominal-group-technique.md) — Technique

1. Write one specific, open question on the board or shared screen and read it aloud, for example "What are the biggest obstacles to closing month-end two days faster?"
2. Give everyone 10-20 minutes of silent, individual writing to list as many answers as they can, with no talking.
3. Run a round robin - each person reads out one idea per turn, which you record verbatim and number - and keep going until the lists are exhausted; allow no debate yet.
4. Walk through the numbered list for clarification only, letting anyone explain an item, and merge true duplicates with the agreement of the people who raised them.
5. Ask each person to privately pick and rank their top five items (5 points for the most important down to 1), then tally the scores in front of the group.
6. Discuss the top-ranked items and the surprises in the tally, and, if the scores are close, run a second private vote before you commit to next steps.

*An operations manager runs a one-hour meeting with eight team members, including two senior leads, to decide which process problems to fix first next quarter.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Okay, open floor - what should we fix first? ... Great point, Sam [senior lead], the ticketing tool is a mess. Anyone disagree? No? Let's go with that. | Take ten minutes and write down, on your own, every process problem that slows you down. Then we'll go round the table one idea at a time, I'll number them, and everyone ranks their top five privately before we talk priorities. |

**Why:** The open floor lets the first senior voice anchor the room and blocks quieter people from offering ideas; silent writing and private ranking let every member contribute and weigh in equally before status and conversation shape the outcome.

Ask yourself:
- Is my question specific enough that people could write concrete answers to it on their own?
- Did I protect the silent-writing and round-robin phases from early debate or evaluation, including my own?
- Did each person rank privately, so seniors' preferences were not visible before others voted?
- Is this really a single-question, prioritise-and-decide meeting, or does the topic need open exploration first?

### [RACI matrix](../articles/meetings-and-facilitation/raci-matrix.md) — Technique

1. List the recurring tasks and decisions that cause confusion as rows, and the roles involved as columns.
2. For each row, name exactly one Accountable owner - the person who signs off or makes the call.
3. Mark who is Responsible for doing the work, keeping it to as few people as possible.
4. Mark Consulted only for people whose input must be heard before the work is finished, and Informed for people who need the outcome afterwards.
5. Scan the finished grid for rows with no A, several As, many Rs or a long list of Cs, and fix them.
6. Share the grid with everyone named, agree it together, and use the C and I columns as the contact list for updates.

*A product team keeps arguing about who signs off on customer-facing release notes; marketing, support and engineering all edit them and the notes ship late.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Release notes - owners: Eng, Marketing, Support (everyone please review before we publish). | Release notes - R: tech writer drafts. A: product manager approves and publishes. C: lead engineer checks accuracy by Wednesday. I: support team gets the final text on release day. |

**Why:** One named approver ends the circular edits, the single consulted reviewer has a deadline, and support knows it will be told rather than needing to chase or review.

Ask yourself:
- For this decision, can everyone involved name the one person who makes the final call?
- Is each person I have marked Consulted someone whose input could actually change the outcome?
- Do the people marked Informed know when and how they will hear the result?

## negotiation

### [BATNA](../articles/negotiation/batna.md) — Technique

1. Before the first meeting, list every realistic thing you could do if no deal is reached - other vendors, other candidates, building it in-house, waiting, or doing nothing.
2. Translate each alternative into the same terms as the deal on the table (total cost, scope, risk, timing, effort to switch) so you are comparing like with like.
3. Pick the single best alternative - that is your BATNA - and from it set your reservation value, the worst deal you would still accept over walking away.
4. Spend some preparation time improving the BATNA itself (get a real competing quote, line up a second option) rather than only describing it.
5. Estimate the other side's BATNA too, and ask about their alternatives, so you know how much they need this deal.
6. Anchor your opening ask on your goal, not on your BATNA; keep a weak BATNA to yourself and never invent an alternative you do not have.
7. Re-check the BATNA whenever new information arrives during the talks, and walk away only when an offer is genuinely worse than it.

*An operations manager is renegotiating an annual software contract after the vendor proposes a 12% price rise.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| We really can't move away from you, so please do what you can on the price. What's the best you can offer? | We've priced a comparable tool that would cost us about 8% less in year one, including migration. We'd rather stay - the team knows your product - so we're looking for a renewal at last year's rate with the new reporting module included. What would make that work on your side? |

**Why:** The manager did the BATNA homework first (a real, like-for-like competing quote), so the ask is anchored on a goal rather than on desperation, the alternative is mentioned truthfully without a threat, and the question invites the vendor to solve the problem with them.

Ask yourself:
- Do I know concretely what I will do tomorrow if this negotiation ends without a deal?
- Have I compared my alternative with the offer on equivalent terms, or only on the headline price?
- Is my opening ask set by my goal, or have I let a weak alternative drag it down?
- Is everything I am about to say about my alternatives true?

### [Concession strategy](../articles/negotiation/concession-strategy.md) — Technique

1. Before the meeting, write down your opening position, your target and your walk-away point, and the total amount you are prepared to concede between them.
2. List what you want back for each possible concession - a longer term, faster payment, a reference, reduced scope - so you never give without a matching ask ready.
3. Plan the moves as a sequence of real steps that get smaller as you approach your limit, rather than one large jump, and hold some of the concession budget back.
4. When you concede, name it - say what it costs you and what it gives them - and state exactly what you need in return, or make it explicitly conditional ("we can do X if you can do Y").
5. Answer the other side's concessions in kind and on time; acknowledge them and move a proportionate amount, rather than pocketing them in silence.
6. Keep every statement about your costs and limits true; stop conceding when the next step would cross your walk-away point.

*A software vendor's account manager is renewing a contract with a client who has asked for a 15% discount.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| OK, we can do 15% off. Hopefully that works for your budget - shall I send the paperwork? | I can't get to 15%, but I can offer 6% off the renewal, which comes out of our services margin. To make that work, we'd need a two-year term instead of one. If the two-year term is a problem, tell me what matters most and we can look at the payment schedule instead. |

**Why:** The first reply gives away the whole concession budget at once, labels nothing and asks for nothing, so the client learns the price was soft and has no reason to give back. The second makes a smaller, named move tied to a specific return, leaves room for later steps, and invites the client to reveal what they value.

Ask yourself:
- Do I know my total concession budget and walk-away point before I make the first move?
- For the concession I am about to make, what exactly am I asking for in return?
- Have I said clearly what this move costs me, without exaggerating it?
- Are my steps getting smaller as I get closer to my limit, or am I making one big jump under pressure?
- Have I acknowledged and reciprocated the concessions the other side has already made?

### [First offer](../articles/negotiation/first-offer.md) — Technique

1. Before the conversation, research the market and estimate the zone of possible agreement - your walk-away point, the other side's likely walk-away point, and what comparable deals settled at.
2. Decide whether to move first - do so when you know the range at least as well as the other side; if they clearly know more, ask questions and let them open.
3. Set the offer from your ambitious target, not from your walk-away point, and make sure it is one you can justify with a real reason.
4. Limit the opening to the issues where your interests conflict; hold back terms you suspect both sides want anyway until you have learned their preferences.
5. State the number precisely and confidently, give the reason in one or two sentences, then stop talking and listen.
6. If they open first, do not respond to their number straight away - restate your own target, their likely limits and your alternatives to yourself, then counter from your plan rather than from their anchor.

*A marketing team lead is buying a six-month analytics retainer from a small agency she has researched; similar retainers she found ranged from $8K to $11K a month.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| We don't have a fixed budget yet - what would you normally charge for something like this? | Based on the three proposals we reviewed for similar scope, we'd like to propose $8,500 a month for six months, with the weekly dashboard and quarterly review included. That reflects the market and a six-month commitment from us. How does that sit with you? |

**Why:** She knows the market range at least as well as the agency, so moving first lets her set the reference point near the low end with a stated rationale, instead of inviting the agency to anchor at the top of the range.

Ask yourself:
- Do I know the likely range of agreement better than, as well as, or worse than the other side?
- Is my opening based on my target and a reason I can say out loud, rather than on my walk-away point?
- Is it ambitious enough to leave room to move but not so extreme that it could offend or end the talks?
- Am I about to reveal a preference we probably share, which they could trade back to me as a concession?
- If they opened first, have I re-anchored on my own numbers before replying?

### [Integrative negotiation](../articles/negotiation/integrative-negotiation.md) — Technique

1. Before the conversation, list every issue that could be on the table - not only price, but timing, scope, payment terms, staffing, risk sharing, duration - and add issues if there is only one.
2. Rank those issues by how much each matters to you, and note your BATNA so you know the point below which no package is worth taking.
3. Open by asking why, not what - ask what the other side needs to achieve, what constraints they face and which terms matter most to them - and listen for differences in priority.
4. Share some of your own priorities in return, a piece at a time, watching whether the other side reciprocates before you disclose more.
5. Propose trades across issues - give ground on what you value less in exchange for movement on what you value more - instead of meeting in the middle on each issue separately.
6. Bundle the trades into complete packages and test them with "what if" questions, using objective criteria such as market rates to settle the remaining distributive pieces.
7. Before closing, ask whether any other arrangement would leave both sides better off than the one on the table.

*An IT manager is negotiating a software renewal with a vendor account executive who has proposed an 8% price increase.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| An 8% increase isn't going to work for us. Can you meet us halfway at 4%? | Help me understand what's driving the 8% - is it a revenue target for this year, or the cost of supporting us? On our side, budget certainty matters more than anything, and we're flexible on contract length. If we signed for three years instead of one, and agreed to be a reference customer, could you hold this year's price flat with a capped increase after that? |

**Why:** Splitting the difference treats the renewal as a single fixed pie; asking about the vendor's interests and offering terms that are cheap for the buyer but valuable to the vendor (a longer commitment, a reference) lets both sides gain more than a 4% compromise would give them.

Ask yourself:
- Have I put more than one issue on the table, or am I haggling over a single number?
- Do I know which issues matter most to the other side, or am I assuming their interests are the opposite of mine?
- Am I trading low-priority terms for high-priority ones, rather than conceding issue by issue?
- Am I sharing information step by step and checking that it is reciprocated?
- Is there a package that would leave both of us better off than the one we are about to sign?

### [Multiple equivalent simultaneous offers](../articles/negotiation/multiple-equivalent-simultaneous-offers.md) — Technique

1. List every issue that is genuinely negotiable (price, scope, timing, payment terms, support, duration) and give each a weight that reflects how much it matters to you.
2. For each issue, write the realistic range of options and score them so that options you prefer earn more points.
3. Build two or three complete packages that score about the same in total for you but trade the issues off differently - for example, higher price with faster delivery versus lower price with a longer commitment.
4. Check every package against your BATNA and make all of them more ambitious than your target, so there is still room to move.
5. Present the packages together, say you would be comfortable with any of them, and ask which one fits the other side best - or, if none does, which is closest and why.
6. Treat their choice as information about their priorities; if they combine the best parts of different packages, reply with a fresh set of equivalent packages rather than conceding piece by piece.

*A consulting manager is proposing terms for a follow-on analytics engagement to a client's procurement lead.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Our proposal is a 12-week engagement at $240K, paid monthly, with two analysts on site. Let us know if that works. | We've put together three options we'd be glad to do. A: 12 weeks, $240K, two analysts on site, monthly billing. B: 16 weeks, $255K, one analyst on site plus remote support, quarterly billing. C: 10 weeks, $215K, two analysts, with your team owning the dashboard build. Which of these fits your budget cycle and team best - or which is closest? |

**Why:** Each package is worth roughly the same to the firm, so any choice is acceptable; the client gets a real choice instead of a take-it-or-counter offer, and their pick reveals whether they care most about price, duration or internal capacity.

Ask yourself:
- Are my packages really equivalent in value to me, or have I hidden a preferred option among decoys?
- Is every package above my walk-away point and more ambitious than my target?
- Have I kept it to two or three options so the other side can compare them?
- Am I ready to respond to cherry-picking with new packages instead of single concessions?
- Is everything I say about the packages - including that I would accept any of them - true?

## nonverbal-and-voice

### [Camera-level gaze on video calls](../articles/nonverbal-and-voice/camera-level-gaze.md) — Technique

1. Raise the camera to your eye level or slightly above, propping a laptop on books if you need to.
2. Sit about an arm's length back so you are framed from the chest up with your eyes roughly a third of the way down the frame.
3. Drag the video window of the person you are talking to up to the top centre of your screen, directly under the lens, rather than off to one side.
4. Look into the lens when you state your main point, make a request or ask a question, using a small sticker beside the lens as a reminder.
5. While the other person is talking, watch their face in the window under the lens so you can read their reaction.
6. If you need to glance at notes, keep them just below the camera so you look down briefly rather than sideways.

*A project manager presents a schedule change to a client on a video call.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The laptop sits flat on the desk, the client's video is on a second monitor to the right, and the manager reads from notes on that monitor; the client sees someone looking down and away while asking for a two-week extension. | The laptop is raised to eye level, the client's window sits just under the lens with brief notes beneath it, and the manager looks into the lens to say: we need two more weeks, and here is what you get for it. Then the manager watches the client's face while the client responds. |

**Why:** Gaze below the lens is barely noticed while sideways gaze reads as looking away, and looking into the camera makes a speaker seem more sociable, so the request lands as direct and attentive.

Ask yourself:
- Is my camera at or slightly above eye level, and is the other person's window right under it?
- When I made my main point or my request, was I looking into the lens?
- Was I watching the other person's face while they spoke, rather than my own self-view or another screen?
- Am I forcing myself to stare at the lens so hard that I am losing track of what people are saying?

### [Gaze and eye contact](../articles/nonverbal-and-voice/eye-contact.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- Was I looking at the other person mostly while they were speaking, and letting my gaze move while I was thinking?
- Did I hold anyone's gaze long enough that it might have felt like a stare or a challenge?
- Did I share my eye contact around the room, or did I keep looking only at the most senior person?
- Is this person's comfort with direct gaze likely to differ from mine, and did I adjust to what they did?

### [Gesturing while speaking](../articles/nonverbal-and-voice/gestures.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- Did my hands show something my words did not, such as a size, a sequence or a comparison?
- When I watch a recording, do I see fidgeting with my pen, hair or laptop that could read as nerves?
- Did I use any hand sign whose meaning might differ for someone from another culture in the room?
- Was I relying on gestures to carry a point that people on audio or reading the slides afterwards would miss?

## persuasion-and-influence

### [Credibility](../articles/persuasion-and-influence/credibility.md) — Concept

Ask yourself:
- If I asked two trusted colleagues how credible I am on this topic, would their answer match mine?
- Does this audience doubt my expertise, my motives, or whether I care about their interests, and which one am I addressing?
- Where I lack a track record, have I borrowed one through data, a pilot or a respected expert?
- Have I met the key people one-on-one to listen before asking them for anything?
- Am I stating limits and uncertainty plainly, or overselling in a way that will cost me trust later?

### [Influence without authority](../articles/persuasion-and-influence/influence-without-authority.md) — Technique

1. Treat the person whose help you need as a potential ally, even if past dealings were tense, and drop any story that they are simply difficult.
2. Write down exactly what you need from them, which parts are essential and which are nice to have, and check that the request serves the work rather than only your own ambitions.
3. Diagnose their world - how they are measured and rewarded, what their boss expects, what their workload and pressures are this quarter.
4. List the currencies they are likely to value (resources, information, help, recognition, visibility, a link to a larger purpose) and the ones you can actually offer, including cheap ones you control yourself.
5. Assess the relationship; if trust is low, repair it first, and adapt to how they prefer to work (data or story, email or a quick call).
6. Make a concrete, honest offer that trades something they value for what you need, then deliver on it and keep in touch afterwards so the relationship exists before you need it again.

*A product analyst needs an engineer on another team to add tracking events before a launch, but has no authority over the engineer's priorities.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Hi, I need these six tracking events added before Thursday's release. It's important for the launch, so please prioritise it. Thanks! | Hi - I know your team is measured on the release shipping cleanly, so I don't want to add risk. Of the six events, two are essential for the launch review (sign-up and first purchase); the rest can wait a sprint. I've written the event specs and test cases so it should be about an hour of work, and I'll credit your team in the launch metrics readout to the VP. Would Wednesday morning work, or is there a better slot? |

**Why:** The rewrite shows an understanding of the engineer's goals, cuts the ask to what is essential, lowers the cost by doing the preparatory work, and offers currencies the engineer may value (less effort, visibility with leadership) instead of borrowing urgency the analyst has no standing to impose.

Ask yourself:
- Do I know what this person is measured on and worried about right now, or am I guessing?
- What can I offer that they would value, and is it something I can genuinely deliver?
- Have I separated what I must have from what would merely be nice?
- Would I be comfortable if they saw exactly how I planned this request, or does it rely on spin?

### [Narrative persuasion](../articles/persuasion-and-influence/narrative-persuasion.md) — Technique

1. Decide what you want the listener to think, feel or do at the end, and where they start from today.
2. Pick one true case that is typical of the problem, not the most dramatic one you can find, and check you may share it (anonymise people and clients).
3. Build it around a specific character with a goal, an obstacle, what happened and why, and the stakes - concrete enough that the listener can picture it.
4. Open with the story or a hook from it, keep it short, and cut details that do not move the cause-and-effect chain forward.
5. Put the numbers right after it to show the story is representative, and say so explicitly ("this happened to roughly one in five new customers last quarter").
6. Close by linking the story to your specific request, then test it on a colleague and ask what stuck and what felt unconvincing.

*A product manager asks the leadership team to fund a redesign of the customer onboarding flow.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Onboarding completion is 61%, down 7 points year on year. Support tickets in the first 30 days are up 18%. I'd like two sprints to redesign the flow. | Last month a 40-person logistics firm signed with us on a Monday. By Thursday their admin had opened four tickets, still couldn't import their driver list, and asked about cancelling. That isn't unusual: completion is down to 61% and early tickets are up 18%. Two sprints on the import and setup steps would fix the exact place she got stuck. Can we add it to Q1? |

**Why:** The rewrite gives leaders one concrete, typical customer to picture and follow from cause to consequence, then uses the figures to show the case is representative and ends with the ask - pairing the story's pull on intentions with the statistics' pull on beliefs.

Ask yourself:
- Is this story true, and is it typical of the problem rather than the most extreme case I could find?
- Does the story lead directly to my request, or is it just a warm-up anecdote?
- Have I given the numbers that show how often this happens, so the audience can judge how far the story generalises?
- Would I be comfortable if the person in the story, or a sceptical colleague, heard how I told it?

### [Persuasion](../articles/persuasion-and-influence/persuasion.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- Can I say, in the other person's terms, what they gain if they agree with me?
- Have I earned credibility on this topic with this audience, or am I assuming it?
- Am I giving reasons and evidence, or leaning on pressure, rank or urgency I have not justified?
- What would change my own mind, and am I willing to adjust the proposal if I hear it?
- Would I be comfortable if they saw exactly how I prepared this pitch?

### [Selling issues to leadership](../articles/persuasion-and-influence/issue-selling.md) — Technique

1. Write the issue in one sentence and link it to a goal, metric or priority your leaders already own, framed as an opportunity where honestly possible.
2. Find out how the decision maker likes to receive proposals - data or story, a written pre-read or a conversation - and what evidence counts in your organisation.
3. Build a short business case - cost, benefit, risk and what happens if nothing changes - and bring at least one concrete proposed solution, not only the problem.
4. Test the idea with peers and one or two respected senior people first; fold in their objections and ask who should be involved or informed.
5. Read the context before you raise it - is leadership listening, under pressure, or recently burned by a similar idea? - and pick a moment and channel that fit.
6. Raise it calmly, acknowledge any past decision you are revisiting without blaming whoever made it, and ask for one specific next step.
7. Follow up, report what happened, and decide deliberately whether to persist, re-bundle the issue with another priority, or let it go for now.

*A support team lead wants leadership to fund a fix for a confusing billing page that drives a large share of tickets.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Hi all, I wanted to flag (again) that the billing page is a mess. My team is drowning in tickets and nobody seems to care. Can we please prioritise this? | Proposal for Thursday's ops review: about a third of last month's support tickets trace to the billing page. A two-sprint redesign, already scoped with product, would cut handling costs and supports this year's customer-retention goal. I'd like a decision on adding it to Q1. One-page case attached; finance has checked the numbers. |

**Why:** The rewrite ties the issue to a goal leaders own, brings data and a solution, shows peers are already involved, and uses a scheduled forum - the packaging, involvement and process moves the research links to success - instead of venting frustration.

Ask yourself:
- Can I state in one sentence which of leadership's existing goals this issue helps, and is that link honest?
- Am I bringing a proposed solution and the evidence my leaders usually ask for, or only a complaint?
- Who else have I involved, and would any of them be surprised to hear me raise it?
- Is this the right moment and forum, or am I raising it because I am frustrated right now?
- If they say no, what will I do next - and am I prepared to accept that answer?

### [Stakeholder mapping](../articles/persuasion-and-influence/stakeholder-mapping.md) — Technique

1. Write down the decision or change in one sentence and set the boundary - which teams, customers, regulators and time frame are in scope.
2. List everyone who can affect it or will be affected by it, then ask two or three colleagues outside your team who is missing, including people with little voice.
3. For each stakeholder, note their main interest or concern, whether they currently support, oppose or are undecided, and what power they hold (budget, sign-off, expertise, reputation, ability to block).
4. Place each one on a power-versus-interest grid and pick an engagement level - partner closely, keep satisfied, consult, or keep informed.
5. For each group, decide who will talk to them, the one message that speaks to their interest, the channel and how often - then put that in a communication plan.
6. Test the map by talking to the stakeholders themselves, and revisit it at each milestone, because power, interest and stance shift.

*An operations manager is replacing the team's expense-claims tool and has a go-live date in six weeks.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Plan: send one all-staff email two weeks before go-live announcing the new tool, with a link to the user guide. | Map: Finance director (high power, high interest, undecided) - weekly check-in, co-own the cut-over date. Payroll lead (high power, low interest) - one briefing on the export file, sign-off by week 3. Frequent travellers in sales (low power, high interest, worried about slower refunds) - invite two to test in week 2, answer the refund-time question in every message. Everyone else - short email at go-live and a two-minute video. |

**Why:** The single email treated everyone as one audience; the map shows who can block the launch, who will feel it most and what each needs to hear, so the manager spends attention where a surprise would be costly.

Ask yourself:
- Who could stop or slow this, and have I spoken to them directly rather than assuming their view?
- Who will be most affected but has the least say, and how will they hear about it before it lands?
- Does each group's message answer their concern, or is it the same message I would send everyone?
- When did I last update this map, and has anyone's stance or influence changed since?

## presenting-and-public-speaking

### [Anxiety reappraisal](../articles/presenting-and-public-speaking/anxiety-reappraisal.md) — Technique

1. Expect your heart rate, breathing and alertness to rise before the talk, and stop treating that as proof that something is wrong.
2. Remind yourself, in a sentence, what the arousal is for - it moves oxygen and attention to where you need them for the task.
3. Just before you start, say "I am excited" (aloud or under your breath) instead of telling yourself to calm down.
4. Turn the excitement toward the opportunity - name one thing that could go well, such as the decision you want the audience to make.
5. Pair the reframe with preparation and rehearsal; it changes how the nerves feel, not whether you know your material.
6. Afterwards, judge the talk by what the audience understood or decided, not by how anxious you felt.

*A finance analyst is about to present a quarterly variance review to the leadership team for the first time and notices her hands shaking in the corridor.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Calm down, calm down. They'll see I'm nervous. Just get through the slides without messing up. | My heart's racing because this matters - that's energy I can use. I'm excited to show them why the logistics overspend is a one-off and get the forecast approved. |

**Why:** The first version fights the arousal, which rarely works, and keeps attention on what could go wrong. The second accepts the arousal, labels it as excitement and points it at a concrete opportunity - the shift the reappraisal studies tested.

Ask yourself:
- Am I trying to force myself to feel calm, or accepting that being keyed up is normal before something that matters?
- What is one thing that could go well in this talk, and have I pointed my energy at it?
- Am I using the reframe as a substitute for preparing and rehearsing out loud?
- After the talk, am I judging it by what the audience did, or by how nervous I felt?

### [Assertion-evidence slides](../articles/presenting-and-public-speaking/assertion-evidence-slides.md) — Technique

1. Before opening the slide software, write the talk's main message, then list the three to six points the audience must accept to believe it.
2. Turn each point into one slide whose headline is a complete sentence of no more than two lines that states the point, not the topic.
3. Under each headline, place the visual that best proves the sentence - a chart, diagram, photo, table or equation - and delete decorative images.
4. Strip the body text down to short labels on the visual; move anything you would have bulleted into what you say aloud or into a handout.
5. Read only the headlines in order and check that they tell the whole argument; fix any slide whose headline could not stand on its own.
6. Rehearse explaining each visual in your own words rather than reading the headline back to the audience.

*An operations analyst is presenting the cause of a rise in late deliveries to the regional leadership team.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Headline: 'Q3 delivery performance'. Bullets: 'On-time rate down', 'Carrier changes in July', 'Warehouse B staffing', 'Weather events', 'Next steps'. | Headline: 'Late deliveries doubled after July because Warehouse B lost its night shift'. Body: a line chart of weekly on-time rate for each warehouse, with Warehouse B's drop marked at the week the night shift ended. |

**Why:** The first slide names a topic and leaves the audience to work out which bullet matters. The second states the conclusion in the headline and shows the one chart that proves it, so leaders who only glance at the slide still take away the cause.

Ask yourself:
- If someone read only my slide headlines, would they get my argument and my conclusion?
- Does each slide make exactly one point, and does the visual actually prove that point rather than decorate it?
- Am I planning to explain each visual, or am I going to read the headline and bullets aloud?
- Is the material complex enough to need visual evidence, or would a simple spoken point serve the audience better?

### [Filled pauses](../articles/presenting-and-public-speaking/filled-pauses.md) — AntiPattern

*A product manager gives a two-minute status update to a leadership meeting and is asked why the launch slipped.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| So, um, basically the, uh, launch is, like, moving out a bit, um, because of, uh, the vendor thing, so, um, yeah, we're, uh, looking at maybe the 20th. | The launch moves to the 20th. [pause] The cause is a late security review from our vendor. [pause] It finished yesterday, so the 20th is firm. |

**Why:** The speaker replaces each filler with a short silent pause and plans one sentence at a time, so the answer is shorter, the date comes first, and the hesitation that suggested doubt is gone.

Ask yourself:
- In my last recorded talk, how many "ums" and "uhs" did I use per minute - closer to two or to twelve?
- Do my fillers cluster at the start of answers to questions, where a planned first sentence would remove them?
- When I lose my place, can I let two seconds of silence pass instead of filling it?
- Am I trying to remove every single filler, when a few are normal and cost me little?

### [Habit reversal training](../articles/presenting-and-public-speaking/habit-reversal-training.md) — Technique

1. Record two or three short practice talks and count your fillers per minute to get a baseline.
2. Write a one-line description of the habit you are targeting, such as "um" or "uh" at the start of a sentence.
3. Replay a recording and mark every filler the moment you hear it, until you catch nearly all of them.
4. Practise the competing response - close your mouth and take a brief silent pause - each time you feel a filler coming.
5. Give a new practice talk, ideally with a colleague tapping the table when you miss one, and recount your rate.
6. Repeat a short booster session before high-stakes talks if your rate drifts back up.

*A team lead must present quarterly results to a department of 40 and has noticed she says "um" constantly in rehearsals.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Um, so revenue was, uh, up this quarter, um, about 8%, and, uh, the main driver was, um, the renewals. | Revenue was up 8% this quarter. [brief pause] The main driver was renewals. |

**Why:** After three 15-minute sessions of spotting her fillers on recordings and practising a silent pause instead, she catches the urge before it becomes a sound, so each sentence starts clean and the number lands first.

Ask yourself:
- Do I know my actual filler rate from a recording, or am I guessing?
- Can I name the exact sound or word I am trying to replace, and where it usually appears?
- When I felt a filler coming in that talk, did I pause silently instead?
- Have I tested the new habit in front of a real audience, not just alone with a recorder?

### [Presentation rehearsal](../articles/presenting-and-public-speaking/presentation-rehearsal.md) — Technique

1. Start at least several days ahead and split practice into short sessions (opening, body, close) rather than one late-night cram.
2. Rehearse the whole talk out loud, standing, at full volume, with the slides or notes you will actually use.
3. Time every full run-through and cut material until you finish comfortably inside the slot.
4. Record one run on your phone, watch it back, and note one or two specific delivery habits to fix.
5. Give at least one run-through to a small live audience - three or four colleagues - and ask each for one thing that was unclear and one thing to keep.
6. Do a final rehearsal in the real room or on the real video setup, then stop practising and sleep.

*A product manager has a 10-minute slot to propose a pricing change to the leadership team on Thursday.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| She builds the deck on Wednesday night, clicks through it silently twice at her desk, and on Thursday runs four minutes over, loses her place on slide 6 and has no answer when the CFO asks about churn. | On Monday she talks the deck through aloud and finds it runs 14 minutes, so she cuts two slides. On Tuesday she records a run, notices she reads the slides with her back to the camera, and fixes it. On Wednesday three peers sit in; one asks about churn, so she adds a backup slide. Thursday's talk ends at nine minutes. |

**Why:** Rehearsing aloud exposed the timing problem, the recording exposed a delivery habit she could not feel, and a live audience surfaced the question leadership was likely to ask - none of which silent clicking would reveal.

Ask yourself:
- Have I said the whole talk out loud, standing, at least once from start to finish?
- Do I know how long it actually runs, from a timed run-through rather than a guess?
- Has anyone other than me heard it and told me what was unclear?
- Did I rehearse with the same slides, notes and setup I will use on the day?

### [Public speaking anxiety](../articles/presenting-and-public-speaking/speaking-anxiety.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- Am I declining this presentation because it is not my job, or because I am afraid of it?
- Have I rehearsed this out loud, standing, at least once - or only read my notes silently?
- Am I assuming the audience can see my nerves as clearly as I can feel them?
- What is the one point I need them to leave with, if everything else goes badly?
- Afterwards, am I judging the talk by how I felt or by what the audience actually did with it?

### [Self-recording playback](../articles/presenting-and-public-speaking/self-recording-playback.md) — Technique

1. Before you record, write a three-item checklist of delivery behaviours to look for, such as hand movement, eye contact and pace.
2. Prop your phone at audience eye level and record one full, standing run-through with the slides or notes you will really use.
3. Before you press play, jot down how you think you came across, then watch as if the speaker were a colleague you are coaching.
4. Watch once with the sound off for gestures, posture and fidgets, and once with sound for pace, pauses and filler words.
5. Note what to keep and pick no more than two habits to change, writing a concrete replacement for each.
6. Record a second run, check whether those two habits changed, and ask a colleague to watch one clip if you can.

*A finance analyst is preparing a 15-minute budget review for department heads and has rehearsed it twice in her head.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| She runs the talk silently on her laptop, feels it went fine, and on the day grips the clicker with both hands, rocks on her feet and looks at the screen for most of the talk. | She films one standing run-through, watches it muted, and sees the clicker grip and the rocking. She sets the clicker down between slides, plants her feet, uses one hand to show the three-way budget split, and records again to confirm the change. |

**Why:** Playback shows habits the speaker cannot feel from the inside, and checking against a short list turns a vague impression into two specific fixes.

Ask yourself:
- Did I decide what to look for before I watched, or did I just react to how I looked and sounded?
- Have I picked no more than two changes, each with a specific replacement behaviour?
- Is my verdict on the recording closer to what a neutral colleague would say than to how nervous I felt while speaking?
- Did I record a second run to check that the change actually happened?

## questioning-and-conversation

### [Face-to-face conversation](../articles/questioning-and-conversation/face-to-face-conversation.md) — Channel

Ask yourself:
- Is this a request, a disagreement, an ambiguous problem or sensitive news - the kinds of message that land better in person?
- Am I choosing email because it suits the message, or because it spares me an awkward conversation?
- Have I picked a private place and enough time, rather than catching the person in a corridor or open-plan area?
- Before we part, have we said the decision and next steps out loud, and will I confirm them in writing?

### [Humble inquiry](../articles/questioning-and-conversation/humble-inquiry.md) — Technique

1. Before the conversation, write down what you are assuming about the situation and the person, and mark which parts you have not actually checked.
2. Open with a question you do not know the answer to ("Walk me through how you approached this") instead of your verdict or fix.
3. Stop after the question and let the other person talk; resist filling the first silence with your own view.
4. Ask one genuine follow-up that builds on what they just said, rather than steering back to your agenda.
5. Acknowledge what you heard in your own words before you add any opinion, request or decision.
6. Only then tell - share your view or ask for what you need - and invite them to correct or add to it.

*A team lead notices a direct report delivered a client analysis in an unexpected format, a day before the client review.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This isn't the template we use. Please redo it in the standard deck format tonight so we're ready for tomorrow. | You built this differently from our usual deck - I'd like to understand why. Can you walk me through what you were going for and what you know about this client that I might not? |

**Why:** The first version assumes the lead already knows everything relevant and shuts the report down; the second surfaces their reasoning (perhaps the client asked for it), keeps them engaged, and still leaves time to agree a format together.

Ask yourself:
- Did I ask at least one question I did not already know the answer to?
- Was my question a real question, or a suggestion or a test dressed up as one?
- Who talked more in that conversation, and did I learn anything that changed my view?
- Did I acknowledge what they said before telling them what I think?

### [Perspective-getting](../articles/questioning-and-conversation/perspective-getting.md) — Technique

1. Before a decision that depends on someone else's view, write down your best guess of what they think and how confident you are.
2. Turn each guess into a short, neutral question you can put to them directly, covering every point that matters rather than one or two.
3. Ask, then listen to the whole answer without arguing, and note anything that contradicts your guess.
4. Restate what you heard in your own words and ask whether you have it right.
5. Make an honest answer safe - say why you are asking, show you can hear an unwelcome reply, and never penalise one.
6. Where you cannot ask, get the information another way - a survey, their written priorities, or someone who has been in their position - and treat any remaining guess as a hypothesis.

*A team lead is planning next quarter's on-call rota and assumes the senior engineers want fewer weekend shifts in exchange for more weekday nights.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Team lead (to their manager) - I've redone the rota so seniors get fewer weekends. I know they'd rather take extra weeknights; they've got families. | Team lead (to each engineer, in one-on-ones) - I'm redrawing the on-call rota. Which matters more to you, fewer weekends or fewer weeknights, and is there anything about the current set-up I'm not seeing? So, if I've got it right, weekends are fine but back-to-back nights are the real problem? |

**Why:** The lead swaps a confident guess for a quick question about each point that matters and a check of the answer, and finds that the actual complaint (consecutive nights) is not the one they had planned to fix.

Ask yourself:
- Which of my beliefs about this person's view did I hear from them, and which did I only imagine?
- Have I asked about every point my decision depends on, or only the one I was least sure of?
- Did I restate their answer and let them correct it?
- Would they feel safe telling me something I don't want to hear?
- Am I skipping the question because I feel sure - and is that certainty based on anything they told me?

### [Self-disclosure reciprocity](../articles/questioning-and-conversation/reciprocal-self-disclosure.md) — Technique

1. Open with a light question tied to the shared situation, then listen for what the other person chooses to reveal.
2. Answer their disclosure with one of your own at a similar level of personal detail - not a bigger story, not a deflection.
3. Hand the turn back with a question that builds on what they just said, so speaking and listening alternate in short exchanges.
4. Deepen one notch at a time (facts, then preferences, then experiences) only after the other person has matched the previous level.
5. If you are senior to the other person, share interests, experiences or lessons learned rather than current weaknesses or anxieties, and keep task-relevant shortcomings for settings that call for them.
6. Close the exchange while it is still balanced, and note one thing they told you to follow up on next time.

*A team lead's first coffee chat with a contractor who has just joined the project.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The lead asks eight questions in a row - background, tools, location, weekend plans - and shares nothing. Or, in the opposite version, spends fifteen minutes on their own career story and a stressful family week. | Lead: 'How's the first week landing?' Contractor: 'Good - still learning the codebase, it's huge.' Lead: 'It took me a month to stop getting lost in it; the billing module was my nemesis. Which part has been hardest so far?' The contractor names one, adds that they're new to the city, and the lead mentions a lunch spot the team likes before heading to the stand-up. |

**Why:** Each person shares a little and then gets the floor back, the lead matches the contractor's level of detail, and the lead's disclosure is a past struggle that is now solved rather than a current weakness.

Ask yourself:
- Did we take roughly equal turns, or did one of us do almost all the talking or all the asking?
- When they shared something, did I answer at about the same depth before asking my next question?
- Did I go deeper only after they matched the last level, or did I jump ahead to something too personal?
- Given my status relative to them, was what I shared likely to build connection rather than cost me credibility?

### [Small talk at work](../articles/questioning-and-conversation/small-talk.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- Did I ask at least one follow-up question that built on something they actually said?
- Did I share something about myself too, or did the exchange feel like an interview?
- Did I go one step deeper than the weather or the commute when the moment allowed it?
- Was this a good time for them, or did I pull a focused colleague out of deep work?
- Did I end the chat cleanly, with a warm close or a link back to the work?

### [Teach-back](../articles/questioning-and-conversation/teach-back.md) — Technique

1. Pick the one to three points the other person must get right to act - a deadline, a decision, a sequence of steps - rather than checking everything.
2. Explain each point in plain, specific language, one point at a time.
3. Take ownership of the check out loud, for example "I want to make sure I explained that clearly."
4. Ask an open request to restate or demonstrate ("How will you walk the client through this?"), never a yes/no question such as "Does that make sense?"
5. Listen for gaps or errors; if you hear one, re-explain that part differently and ask again until their version is accurate.
6. Close by confirming the agreed version in writing where it matters (a recap message or ticket).

*A team lead hands over an urgent client escalation to a colleague before going on leave.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| So that's the background - escalate to Priya's team if the patch fails, and keep the client posted. Does that all make sense? | I've thrown a lot at you, so let me check I explained it well. If the patch fails on Thursday, what are your next two moves, and who hears from you first? |

**Why:** The yes/no check invites a polite "yes"; the open request surfaces the missing piece (who to call first) while the lead can still fix it, and frames any gap as the explainer's problem.

Ask yourself:
- Did I ask them to say it in their own words, or did I accept a nod or a "makes sense"?
- Did I frame the check as testing my explanation, not their competence?
- Which one or two points would cause real damage if misunderstood, and did I check those?
- When their restatement was off, did I re-explain differently rather than repeat myself louder?

## writing

### [Active voice](../articles/writing/active-voice.md) — Technique

1. Find each sentence that has a form of "to be" plus a past participle (was sent, will be reviewed, has been approved).
2. Ask who does the action; if the sentence does not say, decide who should, and name them.
3. Rewrite with that actor as the subject and a strong verb right after it ("Finance will review the budget").
4. Address the reader as "you" when they are the one who must act.
5. Keep a passive only when the actor is unknown, unimportant or obvious, or when the thing acted on is what the paragraph is about.

*A project manager tells a client team what happens before go-live.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The test results will be reviewed and any outstanding issues must be resolved before the release can be approved. | Our QA lead will review the test results by Tuesday. Your team needs to fix any open issues by Thursday, and then I will approve the release. |

**Why:** The original names no one, so each side can assume the other owns the work; the rewrite assigns every action to a person and a date.

Ask yourself:
- Can the reader tell, sentence by sentence, who is responsible for each action?
- Where I kept a passive, is the actor unknown, unimportant or obvious, or am I hiding who decided?
- Did I mislabel any sentence as passive just because it uses "was" or describes the past?

### [Bottom line up front](../articles/writing/bottom-line-up-front.md) — Technique

1. Before drafting, write one sentence that states your conclusion, recommendation or request, including any decision and deadline you need.
2. Make that sentence (after a short purpose statement if the format calls for one) the opening of the message, and echo it in the subject line or title.
3. Add only the background the reader needs to act on it, most important first, and move history and method to the end or an attachment.
4. Put any secondary requests or audiences in clearly labelled sections below the main point.
5. Reread only the first two lines and ask whether a busy reader could act on them alone; if not, rewrite them.
6. Decide deliberately whether this message is an exception - serious bad news or a reader who expects context first - and if so use an indirect structure on purpose rather than by habit.

*A project analyst emails her director about a vendor problem that needs a decision.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Hi Sam, I wanted to give you some background on the reporting vendor. Over the last two months we have seen several late deliveries, and after a call with their account team on Tuesday it looks like their new platform will not support our quarterly export. I have looked at a few options... | Subject: Decision needed by Thursday - switch reporting vendor for Q1. Sam, I recommend we move the Q1 reports to our backup vendor; it adds about two weeks of setup but keeps the March board deadline. Please approve or decline by Thursday. Background: our current vendor's new platform cannot produce the quarterly export (confirmed on Tuesday's call), and deliveries have been late twice since September. |

**Why:** The recommendation, the decision asked for and the deadline appear in the subject line and first two sentences, so the director can act without reading the history, which is still there for anyone who wants it.

Ask yourself:
- If my reader read only the subject line and first two sentences, would they know what I need from them and by when?
- Have I stated my conclusion or recommendation, or only described the situation and left the reader to infer it?
- Is this message an exception - serious bad news, or a reader who expects context first - and have I chosen the structure on purpose?

### [Concise writing](../articles/writing/concise-writing.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- If my reader stopped after the first two sentences, would they know what I need and by when?
- Which sentence, paragraph or side point could I delete without the reader losing anything they need to act?
- Did my last edit make this shorter, or did I only add caveats and background?
- Have I cut so far that a reader new to the topic would now be missing context or find the tone curt?

### [Paramedic method for revising prose](../articles/writing/paramedic-method.md) — Technique

1. Finish the draft first, then pick one long or important sentence to revise at a time.
2. Circle the prepositions (of, in, about, for, by, on) and box every form of "to be" (is, are, was, be, been).
3. Ask where the action is; look for it hidden in a noun such as decision, recommendation or review.
4. Turn that action into a simple verb and make the person or team doing it the subject.
5. Delete slow wind-ups ("It is important to note that", "The purpose of this email is to") so the sentence starts with its subject.
6. Remove redundancies and leftover words, then count words removed divided by the original count (the lard factor).
7. Read the revised passage aloud and restore any context, courtesy or emphasis the reader still needs.

*A project lead's status email asks the steering committee to choose a vendor.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| It is the recommendation of the project team that a decision on the selection of a vendor be made by the steering committee by the end of the month. | The project team recommends that the steering committee choose a vendor by month-end. |

**Why:** The actions buried in "recommendation", "decision" and "selection" become two verbs with named actors, the wind-up disappears, and 29 words drop to 13 (a lard factor of about 55%) without losing the request or deadline.

Ask yourself:
- In each key sentence, can I point to who does what, as a subject followed by a verb?
- Which nouns ending in -tion, -ment or -ance am I using where a verb would carry the action?
- Does my first sentence start with its subject, or with a wind-up I could delete?
- After cutting, does the message still carry the context and tone this particular reader needs?

### [Plain language](../articles/writing/plain-language.md) — Principle

Ask yourself:
- Who exactly is reading this, and what do they already know about the subject?
- Which terms here did I learn inside my team or field, and have I replaced or defined each one where it first appears?
- Could a reader say in their own words what I want them to do next?

### [Pyramid principle](../articles/writing/pyramid-principle.md) — Framework

1. Before drafting, write the single sentence you want the reader to accept or act on - your answer, not your topic.
2. Write the question that sentence answers, and the situation and complication that make the reader ask it.
3. List every supporting point, then sort them into two to four groups that each answer "why?" or "how?" about the top sentence.
4. Check each group - its points should be the same kind of idea, not overlap, and together cover the claim - and put them in a deliberate order (time, structure or importance).
5. Turn each group into a summary sentence and a heading, place the evidence under it, and reread the top sentence and headings alone to see whether they tell the whole story.

*An operations analyst writes up a three-week investigation into rising machine-tooling costs for the plant manager.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Report on tooling costs. Week 1: we collected cost data from all six machines. Week 2: we ran tests on feed rates and coolant. Week 3: we analysed the results and found several interesting patterns, which are described below, followed by some options we considered... | Recommendation: standardise coolant pressure on all six machines; we expect tooling costs to return to last year's level within a quarter. Situation: the machines ran within budget until spring. Complication: tooling costs have since risen sharply. Question: what should we change? Three findings support the answer - 1) the cost rise tracks the machines with low coolant pressure, 2) a two-week trial at higher pressure cut tool wear, 3) the change needs no new equipment. Data for each is in the sections below. |

**Why:** The rewrite moves from the order the analyst worked in to the order the reader thinks in - answer, then grouped reasons, then evidence - so the manager can decide from the first paragraph and check any reason in its own section.

Ask yourself:
- Could my reader state my conclusion after reading only the first paragraph and the headings?
- Does every point under a heading actually support that heading, and are my groups free of overlaps and gaps?
- Am I presenting the order in which I did the work, or the order in which my reader needs the answer?

### [Readability formulas](../articles/writing/readability-formulas.md) — Technique

1. Decide who the readers are and what they must find, understand and do before you look at any score.
2. Prepare the text for scoring by removing headings, bullet fragments, tables, URLs and other non-prose that calculators miscount.
3. Run one named formula in one tool and record which you used, so later scores are comparable.
4. Treat a high score only as a pointer to passages worth rereading for long sentences and unfamiliar terms; fix those for the reader, not for the number.
5. Never chop sentences or swap in short but vague words just to lower the score.
6. Check the revised draft with a few real or representative readers (ask them to find an answer or explain the next step) and let their results, not the score, decide whether it is ready.

*A policy team is told every staff notice must score at or below grade 8 before it goes out.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Leave. Approval needed. Submit form. Use portal. Manager signs. HR checks. Then booked. | To book leave, submit the leave form in the HR portal at least two weeks ahead. Your manager approves it, HR checks your balance, and you get an email when it is booked. |

**Why:** The choppy version scores as very easy but leaves readers guessing about order, deadline and who acts; the revised version scores slightly harder yet answers the questions readers actually have.

Ask yourself:
- Did I change any sentence only to move the score rather than to help a reader?
- Which formula and tool produced this number, and did I strip headings and lists before scoring?
- Has anyone from the intended audience tried to use this document, and what did they get wrong?
