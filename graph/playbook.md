# Practice playbook

Every before/after rewrite, step-by-step procedure and self-check question in the graph, grouped by domain. Generated by `scripts/kg.py build`.

## barriers-and-psychology

### [Curse of knowledge](../articles/barriers-and-psychology/curse-of-knowledge.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- Which terms, acronyms or steps in this message did I learn on the job rather than expect my reader to know?
- If my reader had only this message and none of my background, what would they do next?
- Have I asked someone outside the work to restate my point in their own words, or am I only judging clarity myself?

### [Illusion of transparency](../articles/barriers-and-psychology/illusion-of-transparency.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- Have I actually said the thing I am assuming they already picked up from my tone or face?
- In this email, would a reader who could not hear my voice know whether I was joking, annoyed or serious?
- Did I check what they understood, or did I take their nod as proof?
- Before this presentation, am I treating my racing heart as something the audience can see?

## cross-cultural-communication

### [Direct vs. indirect feedback](../articles/cross-cultural-communication/direct-vs-indirect-feedback.md) — Concept

Ask yourself:
- If I strip out the softeners or intensifiers, what is the core message I am sending - and would this listener decode it the same way?
- Am I about to deliver criticism in front of people who do not need to hear it?
- Have I checked what the person understood, rather than assuming my politeness or bluntness landed as intended?

## difficult-conversations-and-conflict

### [Defensiveness](../articles/difficult-conversations-and-conflict/defensiveness.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- Am I about to describe what happened, or to deliver a verdict on the person who did it?
- Have I signalled that the outcome is still open, or does my message sound like the decision is already made?
- When I felt criticised just now, did I restate their point before I explained or justified myself?
- Is the other person reacting to my content, or to a tone of superiority, certainty or hidden agenda they are picking up?

### [I-statements](../articles/difficult-conversations-and-conflict/i-statements.md) — Technique

1. Before speaking, name one specific, observable behaviour (what was said or done, and when) instead of a trait or a pattern word like "always" or "never".
2. Work out the concrete, tangible effect that behaviour has on you or your work - time lost, rework, a missed commitment.
3. Identify the feeling underneath, and check whether anger is masking something more precise such as worry or frustration.
4. Say it in the first person in roughly this order - behaviour, effect, feeling - and, if useful, a request for what you would like instead.
5. Add one sentence that shows you see the other person's side (their constraints, intentions or pressures) before or after your own view.
6. Stop and listen; expect some defensiveness, reflect back what you hear, and send a clearer second I-statement only if the first one was not understood.

*A project lead raises a recurring problem with a colleague who sends status updates late on the morning of the weekly client call.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| You never get your updates in on time. You're making the whole team look unprepared in front of the client. | I know Mondays are packed for you with the support queue. When the status update reaches me after 9 a.m. on call days, I can't check the numbers before we present them, and I get anxious that we'll give the client something wrong. Could we agree on Friday afternoon as the cut-off? |

**Why:** The rewrite replaces a character verdict ("never", "you're making") with one observable behaviour, its concrete effect and the speaker's own feeling, acknowledges the colleague's constraint, and ends with a specific request - the combination the 2018 rating study found least likely to provoke defensiveness.

Ask yourself:
- Have I described a specific behaviour I could point to, or slipped in a label, a motive or an "always"?
- Is my "I feel…" a real feeling, or a disguised accusation such as "I feel that you don't care"?
- Have I said anything that shows I understand the other person's side of the situation?
- After I speak, am I ready to listen to their reaction rather than repeat my point louder?

## digital-and-async-communication

### [Channel selection](../articles/digital-and-async-communication/channel-selection.md) — Technique

1. Name the job of the message in one line - is it mainly to pass on information the receiver can absorb alone (conveyance), or to agree on meaning, resolve disagreement or decide something together (convergence)?
2. For conveyance - updates, data, instructions, reference material - default to a written, asynchronous channel the receiver can reread and process in their own time.
3. For convergence - an ambiguous problem, a disagreement, a sensitive or emotional message, or a request you need someone to say yes to - default to a live channel with voice and fast feedback, such as a call, video or a face-to-face conversation.
4. If tone, sarcasm or humour carries part of the meaning, either move to voice or rewrite the text so the meaning survives without your tone of voice; read it aloud in a deliberately flat or opposite tone before sending.
5. Combine channels when the task needs both - send the facts in writing first, discuss live, then confirm the decision and owners in writing.
6. Check the receiver's side - their familiarity with you and the topic, time zone, norms and access - and adjust the default; a lean channel works better between people who already share context.

*A project manager needs a client's sign-off to cut a feature so the release can stay on date, and the client sponsor is known to be attached to that feature.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Email, sent at 6 pm - Subject: Scope change. Hi Priya, given the integration issues we will need to drop the reporting module from the March release. Let me know if that works. Thanks. | Short email first - Subject: Decision needed Thursday - reporting module vs. March date. Priya, I'd like 20 minutes Wednesday to talk through a trade-off: keep the March date by moving the reporting module to April, or keep the module and slip to mid-April. One-page options summary attached. Then a 20-minute call to discuss, followed the same day by a written recap of the decision, owner and new dates. |

**Why:** The facts travel in writing where they can be reread, the contested decision happens live where questions and concerns surface immediately, and the written recap locks in the shared understanding the call produced.

Ask yourself:
- Am I mainly passing information on, or do we need to reach agreement - and does my channel fit that job?
- If my reader misread the tone of this text, what is the worst plausible interpretation, and would a call prevent it?
- Am I choosing email or chat because it suits the message, or because it lets me avoid an awkward conversation?
- After a live discussion, have I put the decision, owners and dates in writing?

## executive-and-client-communication

### [Managing up](../articles/executive-and-client-communication/managing-up.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- Can I name my manager's top two goals and the pressure they are under this quarter, from what they have told me rather than my guess?
- Do I know whether my manager would rather read this in writing first or hear it live, and am I using that format?
- Have we explicitly agreed what "done" looks like and when they want to hear from me, or am I assuming?
- Is there a problem or slipping date I have not told them about yet, and why am I waiting?
- Am I spending their time and goodwill on something that truly needs them, and have I brought a proposed solution?
- Would I be comfortable if my manager saw exactly how I prepared for this conversation, or does it rely on flattery or spin?

### [Words of estimative probability](../articles/executive-and-client-communication/words-of-estimative-probability.md) — Technique

1. Agree a short likelihood scale with your team or client before you need it - for example remote (under 5%), unlikely (20-45%), roughly even (45-55%), likely (55-80%), very likely (80-95%) - with ranges that do not overlap.
2. Before writing a forecast, decide your own number or range first, then pick the one word on the scale whose range contains it.
3. Put the numeric range in brackets right after the word in the text itself ("likely (55-80%)"), not in a footnote, appendix or hover text.
4. Keep likelihood (how probable the event is) separate from confidence (how solid your evidence is), and say each in its own sentence.
5. Reserve "possible", "could" and "may" for cases where you genuinely cannot estimate the odds, and say so rather than using them as softeners.
6. Use the same words with the same meanings in every report, and add a short key whenever a new reader joins the distribution.

*A consultant updates a client steering committee on whether a system migration will hit its go-live date.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| There is a real possibility we could miss the March 1 go-live, although the team is working hard and it may well still be achievable. | We judge it unlikely (20-45%) that we hit the March 1 go-live; very likely (80-95%) by March 22. We have moderate confidence: the estimate rests on two weeks of test-defect data. Decision needed by Friday: hold the date with reduced scope, or move it. |

**Why:** The before version could be read anywhere from a small risk to a probable slip; the after version gives one shared range, separates odds from confidence, and turns the forecast into a decision the committee can make.

Ask yourself:
- If three readers each wrote down the percentage they think my likelihood word means, would their numbers land in the same range as mine?
- Have I put the number next to the word, or am I relying on readers to look up a key they will probably skip?
- Am I using "could", "may" or "possible" to hedge when I actually have an estimate I am reluctant to commit to?
- Have I kept my confidence in the evidence separate from the likelihood of the event?

## feedback-and-coaching

### [Situation-Behavior-Impact](../articles/feedback-and-coaching/situation-behavior-impact.md) — Technique

1. Pin down the situation - the specific meeting, document or moment, with a time and place ("in Tuesday's 10 a.m. client call"), not "lately" or "last week".
2. Describe the behaviour as something a camera could have recorded - words said, actions taken - and strip out labels such as "rude", "lazy" or "great attitude".
3. State the impact the behaviour had on you, the team or the work, in your own voice, naming a concrete consequence and, if useful, your reaction (frustrated, relieved, proud).
4. For redirecting feedback, stop and ask about intent ("What were you aiming for there?") and listen before proposing any fix.
5. Agree together on what to keep doing or what to try next time, and give the feedback close to the event while it is still fresh.
6. Use the same three parts for positive feedback, so the person knows exactly which behaviour to repeat.

*A team lead gives feedback to an analyst who presented the quarterly numbers at a leadership review.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| You came across as really unprepared today and kind of defensive. You need to step up your game before the next one. | In this morning's quarterly review with the VPs, when Priya asked where the churn figure came from, you said 'I'd have to check' and moved on. The VPs then questioned two other numbers and we lost ten minutes of the agenda, and I was worried they'd discount the whole forecast. What was going on for you at that point? |

**Why:** The rewrite swaps verdicts ("unprepared", "defensive") for a time-stamped situation, an observable behaviour and a concrete impact, then asks about intent - so the analyst can see exactly what to change and has room to explain instead of defending their character.

Ask yourself:
- Could the other person picture the exact moment I mean, or did I say something vague like "lately"?
- Is my behaviour description something a camera could record, or did I slip in a judgement or guess at a motive?
- Have I said what the impact actually was, rather than assuming they already know?
- Did I leave room to ask about their intent and listen before deciding what should change?

## foundations

### [Audience analysis](../articles/foundations/audience-analysis.md) — Technique

1. List every audience, not just the addressee - the decision maker, technical reviewers, people who are copied, people the message may be forwarded to and people affected by the outcome.
2. For the primary audience, write down what they already know, what they care about, what attitude they are likely to bring and what they must decide or do after reading.
3. Check your assumptions against evidence - the questions people actually ask you, past replies, usage data or a short conversation with a typical reader.
4. Choose content, order, vocabulary, tone and channel to fit the primary audience, putting what matters to them first.
5. Give secondary audiences their own clearly labelled section instead of mixing material for different readers.
6. Reread the draft as each audience in turn - including someone it might be forwarded to - and fix what would confuse or alienate them.

*An engineering lead emails a delay notice about a data-platform migration to a list that includes the client's finance director.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Heads up - the ETL refactor slipped because the CDC connector kept OOMing on the backfill. New ETA is sprint 14, assuming infra gets us the bigger nodes. | Subject: Data migration finishing three weeks later than planned (no change to cost). The move to the new reporting system will finish on 4 December instead of 13 November. Your month-end reports will run on the old system as usual until then. Technical details for your IT team are in the section below. |

**Why:** The finance director is the primary reader and needs the date, the impact on her work and the cost; the jargon and team shorthand move to a separate section for the technical secondary audience.

Ask yourself:
- Who will read or hear this - including people copied or forwarded to - and which of them is the one who must act?
- What does my primary audience already know, and which of my terms or steps would they not recognise?
- What is in this for them, and do they see it in the first few lines?
- Have I checked my picture of the audience against something they actually said or did, or am I only guessing?

## listening

### [Active listening](../articles/listening/active-listening.md) — Skill

Ask yourself:
- Could I state their point, and how they feel about it, well enough that they would say "yes, exactly"?
- Did I hold back advice, reassurance and my own counter-argument until they confirmed I understood?
- Was my attention on them, or was I rehearsing my reply or glancing at a screen?
- Did I ask at least one question that drew out something they had not yet said?
- Would they leave this conversation feeling heard, even if I did not agree with them?

### [Open-ended questions](../articles/listening/open-ended-questions.md) — Technique

1. Decide what you actually need to learn - their view, the obstacles, the options - before you ask anything.
2. Open with "what" or "how" ("What's getting in the way?", "How did the client react?") instead of "Is", "Do" or "Did".
3. Stop talking and let them answer fully; resist offering a suggested answer if they pause.
4. Build your next question on what they just said ("You mentioned the handover - what happened there?") rather than switching topic.
5. Reflect back what you heard before asking another question, so the exchange does not become an interrogation.
6. Switch to a closed or specific question when you need a commitment, a number, or a straight answer to a known risk ("Will this slip past the 30th?").

*A team lead checks in with an engineer on a database migration that has gone quiet for a week.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Is the migration still on track for the end of the month? | What's the part of the migration you're least confident about right now? |

**Why:** The closed question invites a reflexive "yes"; the open one asks for an explanation and presumes there is some uncertainty, which makes it easier to surface a problem early.

Ask yourself:
- Could the other person have answered my question with one word, and was that what I really wanted?
- Did my next question build on their answer, or did I jump to my own agenda?
- Did I ask a "why" question where a "what" or "how" would have felt less like a demand for justification?
- When I needed a firm answer on a specific risk, did I ask a direct, specific question instead of a vague one?

### [Paraphrasing](../articles/listening/paraphrasing.md) — Technique

1. Listen to the whole point without planning your reply; note the facts, what the speaker seems to feel and what seems most important to them.
2. Wait for a natural pause, then restate the core of their message in one or two sentences, using your own words rather than echoing theirs.
3. Where feelings are clearly present, name the feeling and its cause tentatively ("it sounds like the late change left you frustrated").
4. End with a check question such as "Have I got that right?" or "What did I miss?" and accept corrections without arguing.
5. If they correct you, paraphrase the corrected version; follow up with an open question if something is still unclear.
6. Only once they confirm you have understood, give your own perspective, answer or proposal.

*An operations lead tells a project manager that the new vendor's delivery schedule will not work for the warehouse, and the PM's instinct is to defend the schedule.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The schedule was signed off by procurement last month, so we really can't change it now. Let's just make it work. | So if I've understood you, the Tuesday deliveries land on the same morning as the weekly stock count, which means your team either delays the count or leaves trucks waiting - and you're worried we'll start paying demurrage. Is that the main problem, or is there more to it? |

**Why:** The rewrite restates the specific facts and the concern behind them in the PM's own words and invites correction, so the operations lead can confirm or fix the picture before anyone argues for a solution.

Ask yourself:
- Did I restate their point in my own words, or did I just repeat their phrases back like a parrot?
- Did I capture what mattered to them and how they felt, not only the facts?
- Did I end with a genuine check ("Have I got that right?") and accept a correction without defending myself?
- Did I hold back my own view until they agreed I had understood them?

## negotiation

### [BATNA](../articles/negotiation/batna.md) — Technique

1. Before the first meeting, list every realistic thing you could do if no deal is reached - other vendors, other candidates, building it in-house, waiting, or doing nothing.
2. Translate each alternative into the same terms as the deal on the table (total cost, scope, risk, timing, effort to switch) so you are comparing like with like.
3. Pick the single best alternative - that is your BATNA - and from it set your reservation value, the worst deal you would still accept over walking away.
4. Spend some preparation time improving the BATNA itself (get a real competing quote, line up a second option) rather than only describing it.
5. Estimate the other side's BATNA too, and ask about their alternatives, so you know how much they need this deal.
6. Anchor your opening ask on your goal, not on your BATNA; keep a weak BATNA to yourself and never invent an alternative you do not have.
7. Re-check the BATNA whenever new information arrives during the talks, and walk away only when an offer is genuinely worse than it.

*An operations manager is renegotiating an annual software contract after the vendor proposes a 12% price rise.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| We really can't move away from you, so please do what you can on the price. What's the best you can offer? | We've priced a comparable tool that would cost us about 8% less in year one, including migration. We'd rather stay - the team knows your product - so we're looking for a renewal at last year's rate with the new reporting module included. What would make that work on your side? |

**Why:** The manager did the BATNA homework first (a real, like-for-like competing quote), so the ask is anchored on a goal rather than on desperation, the alternative is mentioned truthfully without a threat, and the question invites the vendor to solve the problem with them.

Ask yourself:
- Do I know concretely what I will do tomorrow if this negotiation ends without a deal?
- Have I compared my alternative with the offer on equivalent terms, or only on the headline price?
- Is my opening ask set by my goal, or have I let a weak alternative drag it down?
- Is everything I am about to say about my alternatives true?

## persuasion-and-influence

### [Influence without authority](../articles/persuasion-and-influence/influence-without-authority.md) — Technique

1. Treat the person whose help you need as a potential ally, even if past dealings were tense, and drop any story that they are simply difficult.
2. Write down exactly what you need from them, which parts are essential and which are nice to have, and check that the request serves the work rather than only your own ambitions.
3. Diagnose their world - how they are measured and rewarded, what their boss expects, what their workload and pressures are this quarter.
4. List the currencies they are likely to value (resources, information, help, recognition, visibility, a link to a larger purpose) and the ones you can actually offer, including cheap ones you control yourself.
5. Assess the relationship; if trust is low, repair it first, and adapt to how they prefer to work (data or story, email or a quick call).
6. Make a concrete, honest offer that trades something they value for what you need, then deliver on it and keep in touch afterwards so the relationship exists before you need it again.

*A product analyst needs an engineer on another team to add tracking events before a launch, but has no authority over the engineer's priorities.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Hi, I need these six tracking events added before Thursday's release. It's important for the launch, so please prioritise it. Thanks! | Hi - I know your team is measured on the release shipping cleanly, so I don't want to add risk. Of the six events, two are essential for the launch review (sign-up and first purchase); the rest can wait a sprint. I've written the event specs and test cases so it should be about an hour of work, and I'll credit your team in the launch metrics readout to the VP. Would Wednesday morning work, or is there a better slot? |

**Why:** The rewrite shows an understanding of the engineer's goals, cuts the ask to what is essential, lowers the cost by doing the preparatory work, and offers currencies the engineer may value (less effort, visibility with leadership) instead of borrowing urgency the analyst has no standing to impose.

Ask yourself:
- Do I know what this person is measured on and worried about right now, or am I guessing?
- What can I offer that they would value, and is it something I can genuinely deliver?
- Have I separated what I must have from what would merely be nice?
- Would I be comfortable if they saw exactly how I planned this request, or does it rely on spin?

### [Selling issues to leadership](../articles/persuasion-and-influence/issue-selling.md) — Technique

1. Write the issue in one sentence and link it to a goal, metric or priority your leaders already own, framed as an opportunity where honestly possible.
2. Find out how the decision maker likes to receive proposals - data or story, a written pre-read or a conversation - and what evidence counts in your organisation.
3. Build a short business case - cost, benefit, risk and what happens if nothing changes - and bring at least one concrete proposed solution, not only the problem.
4. Test the idea with peers and one or two respected senior people first; fold in their objections and ask who should be involved or informed.
5. Read the context before you raise it - is leadership listening, under pressure, or recently burned by a similar idea? - and pick a moment and channel that fit.
6. Raise it calmly, acknowledge any past decision you are revisiting without blaming whoever made it, and ask for one specific next step.
7. Follow up, report what happened, and decide deliberately whether to persist, re-bundle the issue with another priority, or let it go for now.

*A support team lead wants leadership to fund a fix for a confusing billing page that drives a large share of tickets.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Hi all, I wanted to flag (again) that the billing page is a mess. My team is drowning in tickets and nobody seems to care. Can we please prioritise this? | Proposal for Thursday's ops review: about a third of last month's support tickets trace to the billing page. A two-sprint redesign, already scoped with product, would cut handling costs and supports this year's customer-retention goal. I'd like a decision on adding it to Q1. One-page case attached; finance has checked the numbers. |

**Why:** The rewrite ties the issue to a goal leaders own, brings data and a solution, shows peers are already involved, and uses a scheduled forum - the packaging, involvement and process moves the research links to success - instead of venting frustration.

Ask yourself:
- Can I state in one sentence which of leadership's existing goals this issue helps, and is that link honest?
- Am I bringing a proposed solution and the evidence my leaders usually ask for, or only a complaint?
- Who else have I involved, and would any of them be surprised to hear me raise it?
- Is this the right moment and forum, or am I raising it because I am frustrated right now?
- If they say no, what will I do next - and am I prepared to accept that answer?

## presenting-and-public-speaking

### [Anxiety reappraisal](../articles/presenting-and-public-speaking/anxiety-reappraisal.md) — Technique

1. Expect your heart rate, breathing and alertness to rise before the talk, and stop treating that as proof that something is wrong.
2. Remind yourself, in a sentence, what the arousal is for - it moves oxygen and attention to where you need them for the task.
3. Just before you start, say "I am excited" (aloud or under your breath) instead of telling yourself to calm down.
4. Turn the excitement toward the opportunity - name one thing that could go well, such as the decision you want the audience to make.
5. Pair the reframe with preparation and rehearsal; it changes how the nerves feel, not whether you know your material.
6. Afterwards, judge the talk by what the audience understood or decided, not by how anxious you felt.

*A finance analyst is about to present a quarterly variance review to the leadership team for the first time and notices her hands shaking in the corridor.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Calm down, calm down. They'll see I'm nervous. Just get through the slides without messing up. | My heart's racing because this matters - that's energy I can use. I'm excited to show them why the logistics overspend is a one-off and get the forecast approved. |

**Why:** The first version fights the arousal, which rarely works, and keeps attention on what could go wrong. The second accepts the arousal, labels it as excitement and points it at a concrete opportunity - the shift the reappraisal studies tested.

Ask yourself:
- Am I trying to force myself to feel calm, or accepting that being keyed up is normal before something that matters?
- What is one thing that could go well in this talk, and have I pointed my energy at it?
- Am I using the reframe as a substitute for preparing and rehearsing out loud?
- After the talk, am I judging it by what the audience did, or by how nervous I felt?

### [Public speaking anxiety](../articles/presenting-and-public-speaking/speaking-anxiety.md) — Barrier

Ask yourself:
- Am I declining this presentation because it is not my job, or because I am afraid of it?
- Have I rehearsed this out loud, standing, at least once - or only read my notes silently?
- Am I assuming the audience can see my nerves as clearly as I can feel them?
- What is the one point I need them to leave with, if everything else goes badly?
- Afterwards, am I judging the talk by how I felt or by what the audience actually did with it?

## questioning-and-conversation

### [Teach-back](../articles/questioning-and-conversation/teach-back.md) — Technique

1. Pick the one to three points the other person must get right to act - a deadline, a decision, a sequence of steps - rather than checking everything.
2. Explain each point in plain, specific language, one point at a time.
3. Take ownership of the check out loud, for example "I want to make sure I explained that clearly."
4. Ask an open request to restate or demonstrate ("How will you walk the client through this?"), never a yes/no question such as "Does that make sense?"
5. Listen for gaps or errors; if you hear one, re-explain that part differently and ask again until their version is accurate.
6. Close by confirming the agreed version in writing where it matters (a recap message or ticket).

*A team lead hands over an urgent client escalation to a colleague before going on leave.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| So that's the background - escalate to Priya's team if the patch fails, and keep the client posted. Does that all make sense? | I've thrown a lot at you, so let me check I explained it well. If the patch fails on Thursday, what are your next two moves, and who hears from you first? |

**Why:** The yes/no check invites a polite "yes"; the open request surfaces the missing piece (who to call first) while the lead can still fix it, and frames any gap as the explainer's problem.

Ask yourself:
- Did I ask them to say it in their own words, or did I accept a nod or a "makes sense"?
- Did I frame the check as testing my explanation, not their competence?
- Which one or two points would cause real damage if misunderstood, and did I check those?
- When their restatement was off, did I re-explain differently rather than repeat myself louder?

## writing

### [Active voice](../articles/writing/active-voice.md) — Technique

1. Find each sentence that has a form of "to be" plus a past participle (was sent, will be reviewed, has been approved).
2. Ask who does the action; if the sentence does not say, decide who should, and name them.
3. Rewrite with that actor as the subject and a strong verb right after it ("Finance will review the budget").
4. Address the reader as "you" when they are the one who must act.
5. Keep a passive only when the actor is unknown, unimportant or obvious, or when the thing acted on is what the paragraph is about.

*A project manager tells a client team what happens before go-live.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| The test results will be reviewed and any outstanding issues must be resolved before the release can be approved. | Our QA lead will review the test results by Tuesday. Your team needs to fix any open issues by Thursday, and then I will approve the release. |

**Why:** The original names no one, so each side can assume the other owns the work; the rewrite assigns every action to a person and a date.

Ask yourself:
- Can the reader tell, sentence by sentence, who is responsible for each action?
- Where I kept a passive, is the actor unknown, unimportant or obvious, or am I hiding who decided?
- Did I mislabel any sentence as passive just because it uses "was" or describes the past?

### [Bottom line up front](../articles/writing/bottom-line-up-front.md) — Technique

1. Before drafting, write one sentence that states your conclusion, recommendation or request, including any decision and deadline you need.
2. Make that sentence (after a short purpose statement if the format calls for one) the opening of the message, and echo it in the subject line or title.
3. Add only the background the reader needs to act on it, most important first, and move history and method to the end or an attachment.
4. Put any secondary requests or audiences in clearly labelled sections below the main point.
5. Reread only the first two lines and ask whether a busy reader could act on them alone; if not, rewrite them.
6. Decide deliberately whether this message is an exception - serious bad news or a reader who expects context first - and if so use an indirect structure on purpose rather than by habit.

*A project analyst emails her director about a vendor problem that needs a decision.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Hi Sam, I wanted to give you some background on the reporting vendor. Over the last two months we have seen several late deliveries, and after a call with their account team on Tuesday it looks like their new platform will not support our quarterly export. I have looked at a few options... | Subject: Decision needed by Thursday - switch reporting vendor for Q1. Sam, I recommend we move the Q1 reports to our backup vendor; it adds about two weeks of setup but keeps the March board deadline. Please approve or decline by Thursday. Background: our current vendor's new platform cannot produce the quarterly export (confirmed on Tuesday's call), and deliveries have been late twice since September. |

**Why:** The recommendation, the decision asked for and the deadline appear in the subject line and first two sentences, so the director can act without reading the history, which is still there for anyone who wants it.

Ask yourself:
- If my reader read only the subject line and first two sentences, would they know what I need from them and by when?
- Have I stated my conclusion or recommendation, or only described the situation and left the reader to infer it?
- Is this message an exception - serious bad news, or a reader who expects context first - and have I chosen the structure on purpose?

### [Plain language](../articles/writing/plain-language.md) — Principle

Ask yourself:
- Who exactly is reading this, and what do they already know about the subject?
- Which terms here did I learn inside my team or field, and have I replaced or defined each one where it first appears?
- Could a reader say in their own words what I want them to do next?
