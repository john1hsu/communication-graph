# Perception checking

Perception checking is a quick way to test your interpretation of someone's behaviour instead of acting on it as if it were fact. You describe what you observed, offer two possible readings of it, and ask the other person to say which is closer, or to tell you what is really going on.[^s2] It turns a private guess ("he's annoyed with me") into a question the other person can confirm, deny or reshape.[^s1] At work it is most useful in the small moments where a wrong guess quietly does damage: a colleague who goes quiet in a meeting, a client whose "fine" sounds anything but, a report who agrees a little too fast.

## Origin

The technique comes from the social psychologist John Wallen, based in Portland, Oregon, who taught it in interpersonal-relations workshops through handouts he largely left unpublished. A 1979 article in the *Canadian Journal of Counselling* gathered his ideas.[^s1] Wallen described an "interpersonal gap" between what a speaker intends and the effect their behaviour has on the listener. Intentions are private and effects have to be inferred, so each side encodes and decodes through its own filters and the gap widens.[^s1] He proposed four skills to narrow it: paraphrasing, behaviour description, description of feelings, and perception checking. In his version, perception checking means saying what you think the other person is feeling and inviting them to correct you.[^s1] Modern communication textbooks teach an expanded three-step form: describe, interpret (offering two readings), then ask for clarification.[^s2]

## Why it matters

People are poor at reading minds from faces and body language, and trying harder does not fix it. In 25 experiments, Eyal, Steffel and Epley (2018) found that asking people to imagine another person's perspective did not make them better at judging emotions from faces, postures or fake smiles; on the standard tests it made them slightly less accurate. What did improve accuracy was getting the other person's perspective directly, by asking them.[^s5] Perception checking is a structured, low-risk way to do that asking. It matters most when words and manner disagree. Instead of silently choosing between "sure, that works" and the flat voice that came with it, the listener names the mismatch and invites the speaker to explain.[^s3] Because you show you want to understand, people who feel misread get a chance to tell their side, which lowers the heat in a conflict.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Start by catching your own interpretation. Before saying anything, ask what is shaping your reading: your past experience with this person, your own standards, your mood. In high-stakes cases it can help to run your reading past someone else first.[^s4] Then use the three parts out loud.[^s2]

- **Describe the behaviour, not your verdict.** "You've replied with one word to my last three messages" is something you observed. "You seem fed up with me" is a conclusion.[^s1][^s2]
- **Offer two interpretations.** Giving two readings shows you have not made up your mind and gives the other person an easy, face-saving way to correct you.[^s2]
- **Ask, then listen.** End with a real question ("Can you help me understand?") and treat the answer as more reliable than your guess.[^s2][^s5]

Perception checking pairs naturally with paraphrasing. A paraphrase checks the *content* you received, and a perception check tests your reading of the *feelings* behind it. Wallen presented the two together as ways to confirm that the message received matches the one intended.[^s1] Face to face and on video you can check a pause or an expression. In chat and email, check the wording that made you wonder ("Your 'fine' read as short to me. Is this a bad time, or are you unhappy with the plan?"). Across cultures, checking more often than feels necessary reduces ambiguity, a point Westmaas draws from Gudykunst and Kim's work on intercultural communication.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

- **A disguised accusation.** "You obviously don't care about this. Am I right?" offers one hostile reading and asks for a confession. Keep the description neutral and the interpretations generous.[^s2]
- **Skipping the description.** Saying only "Are you angry?" leaves the other person guessing what prompted it; naming the specific behaviour makes the question answerable.[^s1]
- **Not accepting the answer.** If they say the date is fine but they were distracted by a family call, believe them, or at least ask a follow-up, rather than insisting on your first reading.[^s5]
- **Overuse.** A check after every pause starts to feel like surveillance, and checking is not always possible anyway. Save it for moments when acting on a wrong guess would cost something.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The technique is practitioner guidance, taught in counselling and communication textbooks, and has not itself been tested in controlled trials.[^s1][^s2] Its rationale rests on firmer ground: research on mind-reading shows that guessing others' feelings from nonverbal cues is unreliable, and that asking works better than imagining.[^s5] Verification is not always possible or appropriate, and sometimes you must act before you can check.[^s4]

## Connections

Perception checking is a technique within **active listening** (`cm:active-listening`), and it **addresses mixed messages** (`cm:mixed-messages`) by putting a word-manner mismatch on the table instead of letting the listener silently pick a side.[^s3] It was **introduced by John Wallen** (`cm:john-wallen`) as one of four skills for narrowing the interpersonal gap, and it **complements paraphrasing** (`cm:paraphrasing`), which checks content where perception checking checks feelings.[^s1] Its rationale is **supported by Eyal, Steffel and Epley 2018** (`cm:eyal-steffel-epley-2018`), the study behind **perspective-getting** (`cm:perspective-getting`) and its contrast with **perspective-taking** (`cm:perspective-taking`).[^s5] It is **applied in difficult conversations** (`cm:difficult-conversation`) and conflict, where misread attributions escalate disputes.[^s2] Neighbouring tools include **checking understanding** (`cm:checking-understanding`), **labeling emotions** (`cm:labeling-emotions`), **I-statements** (`cm:i-statements`), close to Wallen's skill of describing your own feelings, and the **illusion of transparency** (`cm:illusion-of-transparency`), the speaker-side belief that the listener already knows how you feel.[^s1]
