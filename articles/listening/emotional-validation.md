# Emotional validation

Emotional validation means letting someone know that their feelings or reactions make sense and are understandable in their situation.[^s2][^s6] It goes one step beyond hearing them: you say *why* the reaction is reasonable. It is not agreement. Clinicians who teach it stress that validating a feeling does not mean endorsing every view the person holds or excusing harmful behaviour.[^s6] Its opposite, invalidation, tells people they are wrong about their own experience - "you're overreacting", "it's not a big deal".[^s2]

## Origin

Validation as a deliberate technique comes from the psychologist Marsha Linehan and her colleagues, who made it one of the two core strategies of dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT), balancing acceptance of the person against pushing for change, and who described it as a series of levels.[^s5] Linehan's biosocial model proposed that people who struggle to regulate emotions are especially reactive to invalidation and may especially need validation.[^s2] The levels are usually taught as: being attentive; reflecting accurately; voicing what has not been said, as a tentative guess; explaining the reaction by the person's history; normalising it as what most people would feel; and radical genuineness, treating the person as an equal rather than as fragile.[^s6] Later writers in counselling psychology argued that validation works best as an ongoing stance in the conversation, not as a tactic to deploy.[^s5]

## Why it matters

In a lab study, Shenk and Fruzzetti found that people who received invalidating responses during a stressful task showed higher negative affect, heart rate and skin conductance over time than people who received validating responses.[^s1] At work, a closely related move - simply acknowledging a colleague's emotion out loud - raised trust across six studies by Berg and Zlatev, largely because people saw it as a costly signal that the listener was willing to invest in them; the effect was stronger for negative emotions.[^s3] Their field study at a children's hospital found that staff trusted coworkers more when those coworkers had recently acknowledged their feelings.[^s4] Managers often avoid this because they see emotional support as outside their role, while employees tend to see it as part of the job.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Work up the levels only as far as the moment needs. Attention and an accurate reflection are often enough; when a feeling is unspoken, offer it as a question you could be wrong about.[^s6] The distinctive step is the "because": link the reaction to what happened ("after being overruled in front of the client") or to how most people would react.[^s6] Keep your own view intact - you can validate the frustration and still disagree with the conclusion.[^s6] Then follow through. Acknowledgment that is not followed by any effort to understand the cause can backfire.[^s4]

**By channel and seniority.** Live, a brief validating sentence and a pause usually suffice; in the workplace studies even a one-line acknowledgment raised trust.[^s3] In email or chat, where tone is easy to misread, write the "because" explicitly and avoid one-word replies such as "noted". With senior stakeholders, validate the business pressure rather than the personal feeling.

## Common mistakes

- **Validating the invalid.** Saying a reaction makes sense when you don't believe it is insincere; find the part that is understandable instead.[^s6]
- **Agreeing to keep the peace.** Validation is not agreeing with whatever the person says or justifying harmful behaviour.[^s6]
- **"I hear you, but..."** A reflection followed immediately by a rebuttal or advice reads as invalidation.
- **Acknowledge and run.** Naming a feeling and then leaving the conversation erodes the trust it was meant to build.[^s4]
- **Condescension.** Treating the person as fragile violates the radical-genuineness level.[^s6]

## Evidence

The evidence is rated *mixed*. The core lab findings are consistent in direction: invalidation reliably raises arousal and negative affect relative to validation.[^s1] But the benefit of validation over a neutral response is less clear: a later study found invalidation raised heart rate relative to both validation and neutral replies, while validation did not differ from neutral.[^s2] Kuo and colleagues, testing 126 undergraduates, found validation lowered heart rate after anger and shame but not after fear or sadness, with mood recovering in both conditions; their manipulation was a 10-second on-screen message, samples were student convenience samples, and they made no correction for multiple comparisons.[^s2] The workplace trust studies tested acknowledgment rather than full validation and relied mostly on hypothetical scenarios.[^s3] Treat validation as a low-cost way to avoid the measurable harm of invalidation, not a guaranteed calming tool.

## Connections

Emotional validation sits under **active listening** (`cm:active-listening`) and was introduced as a technique by **Marsha Linehan** (`cm:marsha-linehan`) in dialectical behaviour therapy.[^s5] Its effect on stress reactivity was demonstrated in **Shenk & Fruzzetti 2011** (`cm:shenk-fruzzetti-2011`)[^s1] and is supported, with qualifications, by **Kuo et al. 2022** (`cm:kuo-et-al-2022`).[^s2] **Berg & Zlatev 2021** (`cm:berg-zlatev-2021`) supports the workplace payoff through trust.[^s3] It complements **labeling emotions** (`cm:labeling-emotions`), which corresponds to the "guess the unstated feeling" level; validation adds the explanation of why the feeling fits.[^s6] It draws on **empathy** (`cm:empathy`) and **paraphrasing** (`cm:paraphrasing`), supports **de-escalation** (`cm:de-escalation`) and **emotion regulation** (`cm:emotion-regulation`), and is most needed in a **difficult conversation** (`cm:difficult-conversation`).
