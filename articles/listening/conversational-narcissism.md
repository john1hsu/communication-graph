# Conversational narcissism

Conversational narcissism is a pattern of excessive self-focus in talk: the speaker keeps pulling the topic back to their own experiences, opinions and achievements and pays little attention to what the other person is trying to say.[^s1] It is a habit, not a personality diagnosis; Anita Vangelisti, Mark Knapp and John Daly treated it both as a stable style that varies between people and as something anyone can slip into when a situation pushes them toward self-presentation.[^s1]

## Origin

The term comes from the sociologist Charles Derber, whose book *The Pursuit of Attention* analysed how people in everyday settings compete for the floor.[^s5] Derber distinguished two ways of answering someone: a *support response* keeps attention on the speaker ("That's rough - what happened?"), while a *shift response* moves it to the listener ("I've had a headache all week too").[^s5] In 1990 Vangelisti, Knapp and Daly turned the idea into a research programme in *Communication Monographs*, running six studies on how people recognise and judge self-focused conversation.[^s1] More recently Michael Yeomans and Alison Wood Brooks described a specific form, "boomerasking", in which someone asks a question, lets the partner answer, then answers it themselves.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Self-focus has a social cost. In Vangelisti and colleagues' studies, people who role-played conversational narcissism were rated as significantly less socially attractive than those who did not.[^s1] Across eight studies, Yeomans and Brooks found that people who boomerasked were judged less sincere and less likeable, even though most of them expected that leading with a question would make a better impression.[^s2][^s3] At work, the HBS summary of that research notes that a manager who habitually redirects conversations to themselves can come across as self-serving, flatten team morale and make meetings feel pointless.[^s3]

The deeper cost is lost information. Every shift response closes a thread the other person opened, so the listener misses the detail, worry or idea that prompted them to speak.[^s1] Researchers attribute the opposite effect - the liking that follows good questions - to perceived responsiveness: questions that build on what someone said signal that you heard them and care about their view.[^s4]

## How to spot it

Vangelisti's participants agreed on a recognisable set of cues: boasting, shifting the topic back to oneself, exaggerated gestures, a loud voice, and visibly tuning out while the other person talks.[^s1] In practice, listen to your second sentence. If someone mentions a problem and your reply starts with "That happened to me...", "At my last company..." or a better story, that is a shift response.[^s5] Watch, too, for questions you ask mainly to set up your own answer - "How was your weekend? Mine was amazing" - which is the boomerasking pattern of ask-bragging, ask-complaining or ask-sharing.[^s2]

Not every self-reference is narcissistic. Vangelisti's participants said self-focus can be appropriate, for instance when a partner asks for your experience or when brief disclosure builds common ground, and the authors concluded that its effect depends on both people's needs and goals.[^s1] The warning sign is the ratio over a whole conversation: one person's topic keeps winning, and the other person stops offering theirs.[^s3]

## How to fix it

Make the support response your default. When someone raises a topic, reply first with something that keeps it theirs - a short acknowledgement, a paraphrase or a question about what they just said - before adding anything of your own.[^s5] Follow-up questions are the strongest tool: in Huang and colleagues' chat studies, people told to ask more questions were rated more responsive and more likeable, and follow-up questions drove much of that effect.[^s4]

If you want to share your own experience, ask whether it would help, keep it brief, and hand the topic back with a question.[^s3] Ask questions you cannot answer yourself, so the only thing to do with the reply is listen to it; and if you notice you have just answered your own question, simply disclose directly next time, since people prefer straightforward self-disclosure to a question used as a launch pad.[^s2][^s3] Managers can also name the habit openly in a team and include conversational style in 360-degree feedback.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The core finding - that heavy self-focus lowers how likeable and sincere people seem - rests on a handful of experimental and survey studies rather than large multi-lab replications, so it is best treated as a consistent but modestly tested result.[^s1][^s2] Derber's support/shift distinction came from sociological observation, and popular articles often present it with more precision than the original work supports.[^s5] Questions also have limits: observers of transcripts did not always prefer frequent askers, and repetitive or intrusive questions can backfire.[^s4]

## Connections

Conversational narcissism is a failure of **conversational turn-taking** (`cm:conversational-turn-taking`): one speaker keeps reclaiming the topic as well as the floor, which is why it often travels with **interrupting** (`cm:interrupting`).[^s1] It is **demonstrated by Vangelisti, Knapp and Daly 1990** (`cm:vangelisti-knapp-daly-1990`) and undermines **rapport** (`cm:rapport`), because self-focused partners are seen as less socially attractive.[^s1] **Yeomans and Brooks 2025** (`cm:yeomans-brooks-2025`), the **boomerasking** (`cm:boomerasking`) research, supports it with a specific, measurable variant and shows how it undermines **perceived partner responsiveness** (`cm:perceived-partner-responsiveness`).[^s2] It contrasts with **follow-up questions** (`cm:follow-up-questions`), the habit that most directly signals responsiveness.[^s4] The term and the shift/support distinction were introduced by **Charles Derber** (`cm:charles-derber`).[^s5] It sits close to **active listening** (`cm:active-listening`) and to healthy **reciprocal self-disclosure** (`cm:reciprocal-self-disclosure`), where sharing about yourself alternates with genuine interest in the other person.[^s2]
