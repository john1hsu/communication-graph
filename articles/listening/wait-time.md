steps:
  - Ask one question, then stop talking - do not rephrase it, add a second question or offer a sample answer.
  - Count silently to at least three (longer for a hard or open question, or when a group is involved) before saying anything else.
  - If the silence runs on, re-invite rather than rescue - "Take a moment; I'd rather hear a considered answer" - and keep waiting.
  - When the person finishes answering, pause again for a few seconds before you reply, so they can add the second thought that often matters most.
  - In a meeting, say up front that you will wait before taking answers, so the first fast voice does not set the direction.
  - When you are asked a hard question, give yourself the same pause - say you want a moment to think - instead of filling the gap with the first answer that comes to mind.
example:
  situation: A product manager runs a project retrospective with six engineers and wants to hear what really slowed the release.
  before: "So, what slowed us down? ... Was it the test environment? Or maybe the late spec changes? OK, Priya, the environment, right? Great - next item."
  after: "What slowed us down most on this release? I'll give everyone a few seconds to think before we start. [silent count to five] ... [after Priya answers, another three-second pause] ... Anything else behind that?"
  why: In the first version the manager fills the silence with guesses and accepts the first answer, so the group responds to his options; the pauses in the second version give quieter people time to form a view and give the first speaker room to add the underlying cause.

  - Did the answers I got come mostly from the fastest speakers, and would more think time have widened who spoke?
  - When I was asked something difficult, did I take a moment to think, or did I answer to escape the silence?

# Wait time

Wait time is the deliberate pause you leave in a conversation at two moments: after you ask a question, and after the other person finishes answering. Science educator Mary Budd Rowe named these wait time 1 and wait time 2, after finding that teachers typically allowed under a second before expecting an answer, and replied to an answer in under a second as well.[^s1] Stretching those pauses to a few seconds changes what people say. The idea came from classrooms, but it carries over to anyone who asks questions for a living: managers in one-on-ones, facilitators, consultants in client interviews and negotiators.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Rowe summarised the research on the pace of classroom questioning in a 1986 *Journal of Teacher Education* article, which separated the pause after the question from the pause after the reply and reviewed methods for training teachers to lengthen both.[^s1] In 1987 Kenneth Tobin reviewed the accumulated studies across subjects and grade levels in *Review of Educational Research*, concluding that the benefits appear once average wait time rises above about three seconds.[^s2] University teaching centres still teach it as a core questioning move.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Fast exchanges reward whoever answers first and cut thinking short. Tobin's review found that when average pauses exceeded about three seconds, both the teacher's and the students' talk changed, and students showed better higher-level reasoning, in science at several school levels and in middle-school mathematics; he attributed this to more time to think for everyone involved.[^s2] Teaching guidance from the University of Nebraska-Lincoln lists longer, more speculative answers, more use of evidence, wider participation and fewer "I don't know" replies as effects of longer pauses, and warns that when instructors cannot bear the silence they end up answering their own questions.[^s6]

Similar effects show up at work. In MIT Sloan research on simulated job negotiations, pauses of three seconds or more tended to precede breakthroughs, and negotiators told to use such pauses thought more deliberatively and were more likely to spot deals that helped both sides.[^s3][^s4]

## How to apply it

Ask, then stop. The pause has to be real silence: rephrasing the question, adding "or is it X?" or calling on someone in the first second all cancel it.[^s6] Count to three as a floor, and wait longer for open or difficult questions; the Nebraska guide suggests pauses of ten seconds or more after demanding prompts and admits that this feels very long to the person asking.[^s6] Then pause again after the answer: Rowe treated this second pause as a separate habit to train, because teachers who waited after asking still tended to fire back within a second of the reply.[^s1]

It feels hard because conversation is built for speed. A ten-language study of everyday talk found that replies typically start within about a fifth of a second of the previous turn ending, with only small differences between languages, so a three-second gap registers as conspicuous almost everywhere.[^s5] Naming the pause helps: Curhan suggests saying you want a moment to think, though that framing was not itself tested.[^s4]

**By channel and seniority.** In a meeting or video call, announce the thinking time so silence is not read as a frozen screen or a lack of ideas; for written channels the equivalent is not chasing an answer minutes after you ask.[^s6] Be careful when you hold less power: the negotiation studies found that silence from a lower-status party brought no benefit and left that person feeling worse.[^s3][^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Filling the gap.** Offering answer options or a second question during the pause turns an open question into a multiple-choice one and lets the asker's guesses steer the reply.[^s6]
- **Skipping wait time 2.** Responding the instant someone stops talking, as Rowe found teachers routinely did, closes their turn before they can build on it.[^s1]
- **Leaving the silence unexplained.** An unannounced pause can feel awkward; Curhan suggests saying you want a moment to think, so the silence reads as thinking time rather than pressure.[^s4]
- **Using silence as a tactic.** Silence in the negotiation studies helped by creating value for both sides, not by intimidating the counterpart, so treat it as room to think rather than a lever to extract concessions.[^s3]

## Evidence

The evidence is real but rated mixed. Rowe's and Tobin's findings come largely from 1970s-80s classroom studies, many of them correlational or small-scale training studies, with outcomes measured in school achievement rather than workplace results.[^s1][^s2] Tobin's review ties the benefits to average pauses above roughly three seconds; it does not establish an ideal length.[^s2] The strongest workplace evidence is Curhan and colleagues' 2022 paper in the *Journal of Applied Psychology*, which combined naturally occurring pauses with experiments that instructed negotiators to pause; it found more value creation and no measurable cost to the counterpart's feelings about the deal, but also a status boundary.[^s3] Transfer to meetings and interviews is plausible but mostly extrapolated.[^s6]

## Connections

Wait time is part of **questioning** (`cm:questioning`) and a specific use of **silence and pauses** (`cm:silence-and-pauses`). It was **introduced by Mary Budd Rowe** (`cm:mary-budd-rowe`), whose 1986 article set out the two pauses.[^s1] It is **supported by Tobin 1987** (`cm:tobin-1987`), the review that located the three-second threshold,[^s2] and by **Curhan et al. 2022** (`cm:curhan-et-al-2022`), which tested extended silence in negotiation.[^s3] It is **applied in salary negotiation** (`cm:salary-negotiation`), the job-offer setting of the MIT studies, and relates to **integrative negotiation**, since the pauses helped people find joint gains.[^s4] The difficulty of pausing is **explained by conversational turn-taking** (`cm:conversational-turn-taking`), the near-universal norm of minimal gaps between turns.[^s5] It pairs naturally with **open-ended questions**, **active listening**, **paraphrasing**, **meeting agendas** and **psychological safety**, because a question only works if people have both the time and the safety to answer it.[^s6]
