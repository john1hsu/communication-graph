steps:
  - Before advising, ask yourself whether you are the right person to help and whether they actually asked for advice; if they did not, offer it ("Would it help if I shared what I'd do?") rather than delivering it.
  - Listen until you could state the problem, what they have already tried and what outcome they want, and check that summary with them.
  - Acknowledge how the situation feels for them before turning to solutions, in a sentence or two.
  - Offer the advice as options with your reasoning - what each would achieve, what it would cost and how feasible it is for them - rather than a single "what you should do is".
  - Signal that the decision is theirs, then ask which option fits and what would get in the way of trying it.
example:
  situation: A junior analyst tells her team lead that a senior stakeholder keeps rejecting her reports at the last minute.
  before: "Lead: 'Oh, he does that to everyone. What you should do is just send him the draft a week earlier. Next topic?'"
  after: "Lead: 'That sounds draining - especially when it's late in the cycle. What kind of changes does he ask for?' [She explains they are about framing, not data.] 'Do you want ideas, or did you mainly want to flag it?' [She wants ideas.] 'Two things I've seen work: a ten-minute check-in on the storyline before you build the deck, or sending him a one-page outline first. The check-in costs more of his time but catches framing earlier. Which would suit how he works?'"
  why: The first reply gives unrequested, unexplained advice before understanding the problem, so it is easy to dismiss. The second acknowledges the strain, diagnoses the real issue, asks whether advice is wanted, and offers feasible options with reasons while leaving the choice with her - the content and politeness features that research links to advice being judged good and acted on.
selfCheck:

  - Could I have stated their problem, and what they had already tried, before I started recommending?
  - Did I explain why I was recommending each option, including its costs and limits?
  - Did I leave the decision with them, or did my wording imply they had to follow my suggestion?
  - Was I advising to help them, or partly to feel knowledgeable or in charge?

# Advice-giving

Advice-giving is the response most people reach for when a colleague brings them a problem: a recommendation about what to think, feel or do. Communication researchers treat it as a key form of social support, and they study it as a message whose wording, not just its wisdom, shapes whether it helps.[^s1] Done well, it follows listening rather than replacing it, explains its reasoning and leaves the choice with the person who owns the problem.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

The main research programme comes from communication scholars Erina MacGeorge, Bo Feng and colleagues, whose advice response theory (ART) predicts how recipients judge advice from three outcomes: whether they see it as good advice, whether it helps them cope, and whether they intend to act on it.[^s1] ART holds that these outcomes depend mostly on features of the advice message itself - its content (how effective, feasible and low-cost the recommended action seems) and its politeness (how much it respects the recipient's face and autonomy) - with the adviser's expertise, trustworthiness and closeness working largely through the message.[^s1] In management, Harvard's David Garvin and Joshua Margolis drew on research and interviews with advice experts to set out the common mistakes of advisers and advice-seekers at work.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Advice is routine in managing, mentoring and peer support, yet much of it is ignored. Landis and Fisher's research at UCL found that unsolicited workplace advice is often dismissed or discounted, even from close colleagues, partly because recipients suspect self-serving motives such as wanting control or showing off.[^s3] Advisers also have their own reasons to talk: in Schaerer and colleagues' 2018 studies, giving advice raised advisers' sense of power, people who sought power gave more advice, and the boost disappeared when advice was rejected.[^s5] That combination - a giver who enjoys advising and a receiver who resists it - is why the way advice is offered matters as much as what it says.[^s3][^s5]

## How to apply it

Start by asking whether you are the best person to help and whether advice is wanted at all; Garvin and Margolis warn that unsolicited advice often feels intrusive.[^s4] Listen at length before recommending anything, so the advice fits the real problem rather than the first version you heard.[^s4] Acknowledge the person's feelings first: in a 2017 study of 228 friends discussing real problems, better emotional support made recipients see the advice that followed as both more polite and better in content, and through those perceptions it improved advice outcomes.[^s6] When you do advise, lay out your reasoning and talk through options instead of handing down one answer; if you say "if I were you", remember that you are not them.[^s4] Make your helpful motive explicit and keep the decision with them, which counters the self-serving reading that blunts unsolicited advice.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is jumping to a solution before understanding the problem, which produces advice that misses its target.[^s4] A second is advising outside your expertise, or recommending what worked for you without checking it fits the other person's situation.[^s4] A third is getting the form wrong: in MacGeorge's 2016 study, politeness strongly predicted whether advice was judged good and helped people cope, while content predicted whether they meant to act on it - so blunt, face-threatening advice can fail even when it is correct.[^s1] Finally, advising because it makes you feel capable is a trap; the power boost advisers feel depends on the advisee seeming eager, which can tempt people to push advice on those who did not ask.[^s5] Landis and Fisher suggest instead signalling availability, sharing a self-deprecating story of your own mistakes and building a climate where people feel safe to ask.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

ART's core claims come from controlled studies of friends discussing real problems. MacGeorge and colleagues' 2016 test with 244 recipients supported the theory: adviser characteristics had no direct effect on outcomes once message content and politeness were accounted for.[^s1] Those participants were college students rated straight after a 15-minute conversation, so workplace hierarchy and longer-term follow-through were not tested.[^s1] A 2025 meta-analysis in *Communication Research* by Vu, Li and High found message features were positively associated with advice outcomes, gave only qualified support to the role of the adviser, and found study artifacts moderated several effects - evidence that is real but mixed.[^s2] The unsolicited-advice and power findings are each reported here through the researchers' own summaries of their studies, not the full papers.[^s3][^s5]

## Connections

Advice-giving is **explained by advice response theory** (`cm:advice-response-theory`), which links message content and politeness to how advice is received,[^s1] and it is **supported by the Vu, Li and High 2025 meta-analysis** (`cm:vu-li-high-2025`), which found message features related to better outcomes.[^s2] It **complements emotional validation** (`cm:emotional-validation`), because emotional support beforehand improved how advice was judged.[^s6] Done with permission and a clear motive, it **addresses unsolicited advice** (`cm:unsolicited-advice`), the habit recipients most often dismiss.[^s3] It is **applied in mentoring** (`cm:mentoring`), where seniors are expected to advise,[^s4] and it **causes a sense of power** (`cm:sense-of-power`) in the adviser.[^s5] It **requires active listening** (`cm:active-listening`), since understanding the problem comes first, and it sits close to **support responses**, **humble inquiry** and **coaching**, which hold back advice in favour of questions, and to the **Weger et al. 2014** experiment that contrasted active listening with advice.[^s4][^s1] Its politeness findings connect to **politeness theory**, **face and facework** and **psychological safety**, which makes asking for advice feel safe.[^s1][^s3]
