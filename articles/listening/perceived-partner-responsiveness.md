# Perceived partner responsiveness

Perceived partner responsiveness is the feeling that the person you are dealing with understands you, validates what you said and cares about you.[^s2] The key word is *perceived*: in the model it comes from, what matters is how the speaker interprets the listener's reply, not what the listener meant or did.[^s1] Two managers can say the same words in a one-on-one; the one whose report walks away feeling understood has been responsive, and the other has not.[^s1][^s6]

## Origin

The idea comes from Harry Reis and Shaver's 1988 interpersonal process model of intimacy. In that model one person discloses something, the other responds, and closeness grows only if the discloser reads the response as understanding, validating and caring.[^s1] Reis, Clark and Holmes later proposed responsiveness as an organising construct for close-relationship research, describing it as the belief that partners attend to and respond supportively to central parts of the self.[^s2] Research since then has moved beyond couples to strangers' conversations, friendships and supervisor-employee relationships.[^s3][^s5]

## Why it matters

At work, responsiveness is the outcome that listening and questioning are trying to produce. Guy Itzchakov, Reis and Netta Weinstein argue that high-quality listening is one of the main behaviours that creates it, and that listening and responsiveness overlap without being the same thing.[^s6] A review of listening at work by Kluger and Itzchakov links feeling listened to with trust, job satisfaction, psychological safety, lower burnout and stronger perceived leadership, and notes that feeling understood and perceived responsiveness travel with these effects.[^s4]

It also changes how people think. In Reis and colleagues' experiments, people who recalled a responsive partner showed less self-serving bias, and on days when they felt more responded to, they reported being more open to other viewpoints.[^s2] For anyone giving feedback or raising a disagreement, that suggests a practical order: make the other person feel understood first, then challenge.[^s2][^s6]

## How to show it at work

Responsiveness is judged by the receiver, so the test is what the other person can see in your reply.[^s1] Three behaviours carry the most evidence:

- **Follow up on what they said.** In Huang and colleagues' online-chat experiments, people told to ask more questions were rated as more responsive and were liked more, and follow-up questions were the type most tied to liking.[^s3]
- **Show you understood before you add your view.** Itzchakov, Reis and Weinstein place understanding, positive regard and visible care at the centre of both listening and responsiveness.[^s6]
- **Remove what competes for your attention.** Phones, screens and stress lower listening quality, which the speaker notices.[^s4]

For example, a team member says in a one-on-one that the new release process "keeps catching us out". An unresponsive manager replies, "Every process has teething problems - just follow the checklist." A responsive one says, "Catching you out how - where did it bite last week?", then repeats back the two failure points before discussing fixes. The second reply proves the manager heard the specific concern, which is what the employee uses to judge whether raising problems is worth it.[^s3][^s4]

## Evidence

The core finding comes from diary research. Laurenceau, Feldman Barrett and Pietromonaco had students log thousands of everyday interactions; self-disclosure and partner disclosure both predicted felt intimacy, and perceived responsiveness partly explained those links, especially with a fuller measure of feeling understood, accepted and cared for.[^s1] The data were correlational and from one side of each conversation, so they cannot show which way the effect runs.[^s1]

Experiments help. Huang and colleagues manipulated question-asking and found that coder-rated responsiveness partly mediated the liking effect, while people did not predict that asking questions would make them more likeable.[^s3] Reis and colleagues manipulated recalled responsiveness across several studies with hundreds of participants each, although some contrasts were not significant or only marginal, the samples were mostly students and online volunteers, and the tasks were low-stakes.[^s2]

The main challenge is construct overlap. In a 2025 survey of about 2,000 employees, Borut, Kluger, Reis and Shapiro found that ratings of a supervisor's listening, responsiveness and general relationship quality were so highly correlated that many were hard to tell apart, and only overall relationship quality predicted well-being.[^s5] Kluger and Itzchakov likewise flag unresolved discriminant validity and heavy reliance on self-report.[^s4] The status here is mixed: feeling responded to reliably goes with closeness, liking and openness, but how far it is distinct from "a good relationship", and how well relationship-lab findings transfer to workplace hierarchies and other cultures, is still open.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Perceived partner responsiveness was **introduced by Harry Reis** (`cm:harry-t-reis`) with Shaver's intimacy model, and its role is **demonstrated by Laurenceau, Feldman Barrett and Pietromonaco 1998** (`cm:laurenceau-feldman-barrett-pietromonaco-1998`).[^s1] **Huang et al. 2017** (`cm:huang-et-al-2017`) **demonstrates** it in conversations between strangers, where **follow-up questions** (`cm:follow-up-questions`) and **support responses** (`cm:support-response`) build it, and **conversational narcissism** (`cm:conversational-narcissism`) erodes it.[^s3] **Reis et al. 2018** (`cm:reis-et-al-2018`) shows that it **causes** greater **intellectual humility** (`cm:intellectual-humility`) in the experiments' tasks, and it is **studied in social psychology** (`cm:social-psychology`).[^s2] It is **supported by** **Kluger and Itzchakov's review** (`cm:kluger-itzchakov-2022`) of **listening** (`cm:listening`) at work, which ties it to **psychological safety** (`cm:psychological-safety`).[^s4] It is **challenged by Borut et al. 2025** (`cm:borut-et-al-2025`) on whether it differs from general relationship quality.[^s5] It sits under **rapport** (`cm:rapport`) and is fed by **active listening** (`cm:active-listening`), **emotional validation** (`cm:emotional-validation`) and **reciprocal self-disclosure** (`cm:reciprocal-self-disclosure`).[^s1][^s6]
