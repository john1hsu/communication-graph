# Listening

Listening is the learned process of making sense of what another person communicates: receiving their words and nonverbal signals, interpreting them against what you already know, holding them in memory, judging their credibility and worth, and responding.[^s2] It differs from hearing, which is only the physical intake of sound; you can hear every word in a meeting and still not have listened.[^s2] Workplace research adds a second test - how the speaker experiences you. Kluger and colleagues define perceived listening as the speaker's judgment of how interested in and responsive to their words the listener was, and they treat that judgment, not the listener's self-assessment, as what drives the outcomes.[^s1]

This article covers listening as the umbrella skill. Specific approaches sit beneath it: active listening is one well-known way of doing it, and listening styles describe the different goals people bring to it, from understanding to critical evaluation to empathising.[^s2]

## Origin

Listening became a subject of study in its own right within communication studies, which breaks it into the receive-interpret-recall-evaluate-respond sequence used in introductory textbooks, and which lists purposes ranging from understanding and critical evaluation to showing interest, empathising and reaching shared understanding.[^s2] Organisational psychologists took up the question of what listening does at work; a 2024 meta-analysis by Kluger, Lehmann, Aguinis, Itzchakov and colleagues gathered this scattered research and proposed a theory of why being listened to changes how people think, feel and perform.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The 2024 meta-analysis pooled 144 independent samples and 664 effect sizes. Feeling listened to was associated with work outcomes at an average correlation of about .39, most strongly with relationship quality (about .51) and also with clearer cognition, more positive job attitudes and higher job performance (about .36).[^s1] The authors argue that being listened to meets speakers' needs for clarity, autonomy, competence and belonging, while the listener gains information and humility.[^s1] A business-school summary of the same body of work highlights more satisfied employees, less burnout and more discretionary effort under leaders who listen.[^s4]

Listening also changes what speakers think. In four laboratory experiments, people who talked about their views to an attentive, non-judgmental listener became less socially anxious and less defensive, noticed more of the contradictions in their own attitudes, and held less extreme positions.[^s5]

## What good looks like

Good listening is not just staying quiet. In 360-degree data on 3,492 managers, the colleagues of the top 5% of listeners described conversations as two-way: these managers asked questions that prompted new insight, made the speaker feel supported and safe, took turns cooperatively rather than hunting for errors, and offered suggestions in a way that was heard.[^s3] Zenger and Folkman group these behaviours into rising levels, from creating a safe setting and removing distractions, through grasping the substance and noticing nonverbal cues and emotions, to asking questions that help the speaker see the issue afresh.[^s3]

A workplace example: a team lead hears a developer say the release date is "probably fine". A hearing-only response is to note the date and move on. A listening response notices the hedge, asks what would make it not fine, and summarises the two risks the developer then names - and the lead now has information the status report would not have shown.[^s2][^s3]

## How to practise it

- **Clear the channel.** Put devices away and set aside time pressure; distraction, stress, phones and the urge to offer unrequested advice are the most commonly cited barriers.[^s4]
- **Let them finish.** Wait until the speaker has completed the thought before replying, and resist preparing your rebuttal while they talk.[^s4][^s3]
- **Ask to understand.** Use open questions such as asking them to say more, then check the substance by restating it.[^s4][^s3]
- **Watch more than words.** Interpreting combines what you hear and what you see, so attend to tone and expression as well as content.[^s2]
- **Ask for the speaker's verdict.** Because outcomes track the speaker's perception rather than the listener's self-rating, ask a trusted colleague how listened-to they feel with you.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is consistent in direction but mostly correlational: about three-quarters of the meta-analysed measures were self-reports, only around 13% of listening measures came from experiments, and effects varied enormously across studies.[^s1] When job performance was rated by someone other than the person being listened to, the correlation fell to about .21.[^s1] The Zenger and Folkman findings come from one consulting firm's 360-degree dataset rather than a controlled study.[^s3] So treat listening as a well-supported, high-value skill whose exact payoff depends on setting, not as a guaranteed lever.
