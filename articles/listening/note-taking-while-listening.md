# Note-taking while listening

Note-taking while listening means capturing short, selective notes in your own words as someone talks, so you can remember the content, check it back with them and follow up afterwards. Research separates two functions: the act of writing can help you process what you hear (encoding), and the notes themselves become a record you can review later (external storage).[^s4] At work the second function often matters more, because interviews, one-on-ones, client calls and meetings all produce facts and commitments that are easy to forget or distort.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Most research on note-taking comes from education. Kiewra's 1985 review of 56 lecture studies found that 33 favoured note-taking over simply listening, and Kobayashi's 2006 meta-analysis estimated a small benefit (d = 0.22) for taking notes even without reviewing them.[^s4] Industrial-organisational psychologists carried the question into the workplace: Middendorf and Macan's 2002 experiment in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* tested whether interviewers who take notes remember candidates better and judge them more accurately.[^s1] A popular later strand, prompted by Mueller and Oppenheimer's 2014 study, claimed that handwriting notes beats typing them because typists transcribe instead of thinking.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Memory for a conversation fades and drifts quickly, and decisions are often made days later. In the interview study, interviewers who took notes recalled interview content more accurately than those who did not.[^s1] Notes also protect the relationship. In a 2021 interview study of 41 bank employees, people often felt heard only when the listener later acted on what was said; listeners who seemed open in the moment but never followed through left people feeling unheard.[^s5] A note of what you promised is the simplest way to make sure that follow-through happens.

## How to apply it

Prepare a skeleton of the questions you need answered, then listen for content that fits it rather than trying to capture everything.[^s1] Write keywords, figures and names, and record the speaker's own words only for points likely to be disputed. Notes that select, paraphrase and flag what you did not understand reflect deeper processing than copying everything down.[^s4] Use your notes to drive a read-back ("So the two blockers are..."), which pairs naturally with [[paraphrasing]] and [[summarizing]]. Afterwards, review the notes: in the interview study, reviewing them improved judgement accuracy for interviewers who took conventional notes, while note-taking alone did not.[^s1] Finally, turn marked actions into a written recap and complete them, since follow-up action is part of what makes listening register.[^s5]

**By channel and setting.** In person, say briefly that you will take notes so the speaker reads it as attention rather than the distracted listening that leaves people feeling unheard.[^s5] On a video call, notes taken in a second window are invisible, so occasionally say what you are capturing. In sensitive conversations, such as a grievance, keep notes sparse during the talk and complete them straight afterwards, because the review afterwards is where notes pay off.[^s1] These adaptations are practitioner judgement rather than tested findings.

## Common mistakes

- **Transcribing.** Capturing nearly every sentence drains attention, and in lecture studies more word-for-word overlap with the speaker went with weaker quiz scores, though that link was not robust in the 2021 replication.[^s2]
- **Writing instead of watching.** Head-down note-taking can look like the distracted or superficial listening that left employees feeling unheard.[^s5]
- **Never reviewing.** The benefit to judgement in the interview study came from reviewing notes, not from writing them.[^s1]
- **Fighting over the medium.** Insisting on pen and paper because "handwriting is better for learning" overstates the evidence (see below).[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence that taking notes helps recall is reasonably consistent but modest, and most of it comes from students listening to lectures rather than two-way workplace conversations.[^s4] The one direct workplace experiment found better recall but no better judgements from note-taking alone.[^s1] The popular claim that handwriting beats typing has not held up. A 2019 replication and extension found no consistent test difference between longhand, laptop and other groups, and a meta-analysis of direct replications found only small, non-significant effects favouring longhand.[^s3] A 2021 direct replication with 142 analysed participants likewise found no significant quiz advantage for handwriting, with mini meta-analyses showing the same pattern.[^s2] In both the original study and the replication, more words in notes predicted better scores, which suggests that what you capture matters more than the tool.[^s2] Evidence is therefore rated mixed.

## Connections

Note-taking is a supporting practice within [[active-listening]]: it helps you retain and check meaning, but it does not replace attention.[^s5] It is **applied in** [[job-interview]], where it was **demonstrated by** Middendorf and Macan's 2002 experiment on recall and judgement.[^s1] It **complements** [[paraphrasing]], because selective notes in your own words reflect the same deeper processing that a good read-back needs.[^s4] Its popular "handwrite, don't type" version is **challenged by** Urry and colleagues' 2021 replication[^s2] and by Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson's 2019 replication and extension.[^s3] It is **applied in** [[one-on-one-meetings]] and other conversations with managers, where feeling heard often depends on visible follow-through (low confidence: the study did not test note-taking directly).[^s5] It also relates to [[summarizing]], [[meeting-recaps]] and [[checking-understanding]], which turn notes into shared, confirmed records.[^s1][^s5]
