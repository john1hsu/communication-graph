steps:
  - Pick one open, specific prompt (for example, what has made this quarter hard for you?) and agree the length - a set number of rounds or minutes - before you start.
  - Seat everyone in a circle, or on video post a fixed speaking order, and choose a talking object that passes easily from hand to hand.
  - State the ground rules - only the holder speaks, everyone else listens without comment, anyone may pass, and what is said stays in the room - and ask for agreement.
  - Pass the object round in order; while others speak, put devices away and attend to their meaning instead of planning your own turn.
  - Speak briefly and from your own experience when the object reaches you, so others get their turn.
  - Close with a short check-out round in which each person names one thing they heard that they had not known, then agree any follow-up outside the circle.
example:
  situation: A team lead runs a retrospective after a missed launch date; in past retros two senior engineers did most of the talking and others stayed quiet.
  before: "Lead: \"OK, what went wrong? Anyone?\" A senior engineer blames the vendor; a second disagrees and they debate for twenty minutes while four people say nothing."
  after: "Lead: \"One round, two minutes each, phone face down. Hold the marker to speak; everyone else just listens - no replies until the round ends. Prompt: what did you see go wrong from where you sat?\" The marker goes round; a junior tester describes test environments that broke every Friday."
  why: Turn-taking with a talking object stops the loudest voices from dominating and stops rebuttals mid-turn, so people who would have stayed silent are heard and the team learns something new.
selfCheck:
  - While someone else held the object, was I following what they meant or composing what I would say next?
  - Did every person get a turn, including the quietest and the most junior?
  - Did I keep my own turn short enough that others had time for theirs?
  - Did I resist the urge to reply to or correct someone the moment the object moved on?

# Listening circle

A listening circle is a structured group conversation: people sit in a circle, only one person speaks at a time, and a talking object - a stick, stone or marker - shows whose turn it is.[^s1] Everyone not holding the object listens without commenting, and anyone may pass.[^s4] The structure does two jobs at once. It gives each member a protected turn on a shared topic, and it makes the rest of the group practise listening to understand rather than listening to reply.[^s2]

## Origin

The talking-piece circle, often called council, comes from several Native American traditions and has been adapted by non-Native groups for generations; the Center for Council Training summarises its spirit in four intentions - speak from the heart, listen from the heart, speak spontaneously and speak leanly.[^s4] Organisational psychologists Guy Itzchakov and Avraham Kluger brought the format into workplace research. Drawing on Carl Rogers' view that being listened to lowers anxiety and frees people to explore their own thinking, they ran Listening Circle workshops with employees and published the first theory-driven tests in 2017.[^s6][^s1] A 2022 review of listening in organisations groups such circles with other speaking-and-listening structures that pass an object to mark turns.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Ordinary meetings reward fast talkers and quick rebuttals. A circle changes the rules so that turns are equal and replies wait, which is why reviewers describe it as a remedy for weak listening skills or for team norms that discourage listening; it helps people withhold judgment long enough to discuss hard issues.[^s2] Itzchakov and Kluger predicted a chain of effects: feeling well listened to lowers social anxiety, which allows deeper reflection, which makes people more aware of the pros and cons of their own work attitudes and so less extreme in them.[^s1] Organisations also use circles for connection and support; a university medicine department runs them for staff and trainees, in person and online, as a space where people feel heard.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Keep the prompt narrow and the boundaries clear. Practice guides advise a facilitator who explains the structure, keeps people to the agreed rules, sets the length in advance (time, a fixed number of rounds, or until the object goes round in silence) and opens and closes with a short check-in and check-out.[^s4] Ask speakers to talk from their own experience - the medicine department's circles use "I" statements - and on video calls replace the physical object with a posted order of names.[^s5] Speaking leanly matters: others are waiting, and nobody is obliged to speak.[^s4] Treat the circle as a listening exercise, not a decision meeting; if action is needed, agree it afterwards in a normal discussion.

## Common mistakes

- **Using it for every meeting.** Circles take extra time and effort from both listeners and speakers, so reserve them for topics where being heard matters more than speed.[^s2]
- **Assuming it fixes information overload.** Researchers note it is still unclear whether circles help listeners process the larger volume of what they hear.[^s2]
- **Letting the facilitator dominate.** The facilitator's job is to hold the boundaries and gently restate the rules, not to comment on each turn.[^s4]
- **Breaking the turn.** Replying, nodding vigorously or whispering to a neighbour while someone holds the object turns the circle back into an ordinary debate.[^s4]
- **Skipping the opt-out.** Forcing a turn on someone who wants to pass undercuts the safety the format relies on.[^s4][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The core study tested the format in three quasi-experiments with employees (31, 66 and 83 participants). The Listening Circle was compared with a self-enhancement workshop, a conflict-management workshop and no training, and the authors report consistent support for their predicted effects on anxiety, self-awareness and less extreme attitudes.[^s1] The same research group later found that a group listening training - not specifically a circle - raised employees' sense of relatedness and lowered burnout and turnover intentions immediately and three weeks later, in two quasi-field experiments.[^s3] The evidence remains thin: the studies are small, quasi-experimental and largely from one research group, many workplace accounts are testimonials, few failed implementations are reported, and the conditions under which circles help or backfire are not well understood.[^s1][^s2][^s5] Treat it as a promising, low-cost practice rather than a proven fix.

## Connections

The circle is a group form of active listening (`cm:active-listening`) and a structured tool within facilitation (`cm:facilitation`).[^s2][^s4] Its tested aim is to develop listening (`cm:listening`) - the speaker's experience of being heard - and reviewers link it to promoting high-quality listening in teams.[^s1][^s2] Because only the holder speaks and every member gets a turn, it addresses interrupting (`cm:interrupting`) and unequal participation (`cm:unequal-participation`), and it is applied in workplace meetings (`cm:workplace-meetings`) such as team check-ins and department gatherings.[^s4][^s5] Its effects are demonstrated by Itzchakov and Kluger's 2017 study, and its rationale is influenced by Carl Rogers' client-centred account of listening.[^s1][^s6] It sits alongside 1-2-4-All (`cm:1-2-4-all`) and nominal group technique (`cm:nominal-group-technique`), other structures that equalise turns, and alongside psychological safety (`cm:psychological-safety`), which equal hearing can help build.[^s2]
