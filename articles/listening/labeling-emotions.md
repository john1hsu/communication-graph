  - Listen until you have a reasonable read of the feeling - from their words, pace, tone and what they keep returning to - rather than labeling the first sign of emotion.
  - Name the emotion and, if you can, its trigger in one short sentence that starts with "It sounds like..." or "It seems like...", choosing a milder word than your first instinct.
  - Phrase the label as a neutral observation about them, not a verdict on their behaviour or motives.
  - Stop talking and let the label land; give them time to agree, refine or reject it.
  - If they correct you, accept the correction, restate it in their terms and do not argue for your original read.
  - Once they confirm the feeling, move to the substance - their facts, priorities and what happens next.

# Labeling emotions

Labeling emotions means saying out loud, briefly and tentatively, what you think the other person is feeling - "it sounds like the reorg has you anxious about your role" - and then letting them confirm or correct it. Psychologists call the underlying process affect labeling: putting feelings into words, which in lab studies dampens emotional reactions compared with simply experiencing them.[^s1][^s2] When someone else supplies the words, it is called interpersonal affect labeling.[^s3] Negotiation trainers teach the same move as a "label", usually opening with "It seems like..." or "It sounds like...".[^s6]

## Origin

The research strand comes from Matthew Lieberman's social neuroscience lab at UCLA. In a 2007 fMRI study of 30 adults viewing angry and fearful faces, choosing a word for the emotion reduced amygdala activity compared with choosing the person's gender, and increased activity in right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, a region linked to regulation.[^s1] Follow-up work with people afraid of spiders found that those who described their fear during exposure had lower physiological arousal a week later than those who reappraised or distracted themselves.[^s4] In 2021, Shamay-Tsoory and Levy-Gigi extended the idea to labels chosen by another person, testing 74 romantic couples.[^s3] Separately, practitioners in crisis and commercial negotiation built labeling into their listening toolkit as a way to defuse negative emotions so the other side can think clearly.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Emotion that goes unacknowledged tends to leak into the substance of a conversation. A short, accurate label signals that you noticed, without asking the person to explain or justify the feeling. In the couples study, seeing a partner's label for one's reaction to distressing images reduced distress more than choosing one's own label, and the benefit grew with the labeler's empathy.[^s3] The 2011 Lieberman experiments also found a striking forecasting error: people predicted that labeling would make them feel worse, when it made them feel slightly better.[^s2] That mismatch is one reason managers avoid naming emotions they can plainly see - they expect it to inflame things.

## How to apply it

Wait until you have a reasonable read, then name the feeling and its trigger in one sentence, phrased as an observation about them rather than a statement about you.[^s6] Choose a milder word than your first instinct: across seven studies with 2,866 participants, people rated others' emotions as more intense than those people rated their own, especially for negative emotions, so an overstated label ("you're furious") is a common miss.[^s5] Keep the label neutral rather than accusatory - for example, "it seems like speed is your top priority" rather than "it seems like you don't care about quality".[^s6] Then pause. The label is a question in disguise, and the speaker's correction is useful information; trainers recommend a slight upward inflection to invite it.[^s6]

**By channel and seniority.** Live or on video, a label can be a single sentence with a slight upward inflection that invites a reply.[^s6] In email or chat, tone is harder to read and the intensity bias also shows up in text-based exchanges,[^s5] so label more tentatively and tie it to what they wrote ("your note suggests the timeline is a real worry"). With senior stakeholders, label the business pressure ("it sounds like the board date is driving this") rather than personal feelings; with a peer or report who is clearly upset, naming the feeling itself is usually more welcome.

## Common mistakes

- **Telling, not guessing.** "You're frustrated" invites a defensive denial; "it sounds like this is frustrating" leaves room for a correction.[^s6]
- **Overshooting the intensity.** Observers tend to overestimate how strongly others feel, particularly negative emotions, so calibrate down.[^s5]
- **Labels that carry a judgment.** A label that implies a criticism will be heard as an attack rather than as understanding.[^s6]
- **Using it as a script.** The couples study found the effect depended on the labeler's empathy, which suggests a formulaic label from someone who is not paying attention will do less good.[^s3]
- **Labeling and then moving on.** Without a pause, the label becomes a box-ticking preamble to your own agenda.

## Evidence

The evidence is rated mixed. The laboratory finding that naming emotions reduces neural and physiological reactivity has been shown repeatedly within Lieberman's group,[^s1][^s4] but the self-reported benefits are small - roughly a few tenths of a point on a nine-point distress scale in the 2011 experiments, which had samples of about 22 to 46 people per study.[^s2] In the spider-exposure study, labeling lowered arousal but did not lower self-reported fear relative to the other groups.[^s4] Most studies label pictures or one's own reactions, not emotions in live workplace conversations; the only direct interpersonal test used romantic couples and aversive images, not colleagues in conflict.[^s2][^s3] The negotiation version of labeling rests mainly on practitioner experience.[^s6] Treat it as a low-cost move with plausible, modest benefits, not a guaranteed de-escalation tool.

## Connections

Labeling emotions is a narrower technique within **active listening** (`cm:active-listening`), close to **paraphrasing** (`cm:paraphrasing`) but focused on the feeling rather than the content. Its effects were demonstrated in **Lieberman et al. 2007** (`cm:lieberman-et-al-2007`),[^s1] it is explained as a form of implicit **emotion regulation** (`cm:emotion-regulation`) and contrasts with deliberate **cognitive reappraisal** (`cm:cognitive-reappraisal`), which people correctly expect to help, whereas they wrongly expect labeling to hurt.[^s2] It is supported by **Shamay-Tsoory and Levy-Gigi 2021** (`cm:shamay-tsoory-levy-gigi-2021`), the first test of labels supplied by another person,[^s3] and is widely applied in **negotiation** (`cm:negotiation`).[^s6] It pairs with **perspective-taking** (`cm:perspective-taking`), which supplies the read you label, and with **wait time** (`cm:wait-time`), the pause that lets the other person respond.
