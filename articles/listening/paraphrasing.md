steps:
  - Listen to the whole point without planning your reply; note the facts, what the speaker seems to feel and what seems most important to them.
  - Wait for a natural pause, then restate the core of their message in one or two sentences, using your own words rather than echoing theirs.
  - Where feelings are clearly present, name the feeling and its cause tentatively ("it sounds like the late change left you frustrated").
  - End with a check question such as "Have I got that right?" or "What did I miss?" and accept corrections without arguing.
  - If they correct you, paraphrase the corrected version; follow up with an open question if something is still unclear.
  - Only once they confirm you have understood, give your own perspective, answer or proposal.

# Paraphrasing

Paraphrasing means telling someone, in your own words, what you understood them to say - the facts, and where relevant the feeling and the priority behind them - and then asking whether you got it right.[^s1] It is the verbal core of active listening: it turns silent attention into something the speaker can hear, check and correct.[^s2] Negotiation trainers describe it as the first of three listening moves, followed by inquiry and acknowledgement, and stress that nodding or saying "I understand" is not a substitute.[^s4]

## Origin

The technique comes from Carl Rogers's client-centred therapy, where reflecting a client's meaning and feelings was a way of expressing empathy - sensing the other person's world as if it were your own without losing yourself in it.[^s1] From counselling it spread into conflict mediation, where it is now a standard tool,[^s1] and into negotiation and management training as part of active listening.[^s4] Communication researchers study the "message paraphrase" as one separable element of active listening.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Paraphrasing does three jobs at once. It tests your understanding, because a wrong restatement surfaces a misunderstanding while it is still cheap to fix; it gives the other person a chance to correct details before tension rises; and it shows that you have taken their view seriously before you argue for yours.[^s4] In a small experiment on people recounting a real, ongoing conflict, an interviewer who paraphrased left them feeling less negative than one who silently took notes.[^s1] More broadly, a 2024 meta-analysis of 664 effect sizes found that people who feel listened to at work report better relationships and somewhat better performance, although most of that evidence is correlational.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Hold your reply until the speaker has finished a point, then restate its core briefly in fresh words rather than echoing their sentences.[^s1] Name feelings tentatively, as a guess they can reject, and close with an explicit check question; the Harvard negotiation guidance models ending with a question about whether you captured their main points.[^s4] If they correct you, paraphrase again, then use an open question to fill gaps.[^s3] Only after they confirm you understood do you move to your own view or a joint solution.[^s4]

**By channel and seniority.** The purpose - confirming understanding and letting the other side correct details - does not depend on the medium.[^s4] Live, a paraphrase can be a single sentence. In email or chat, a short "Here is what I understand you need..." at the top of a reply does the same job and prevents a thread of crossed purposes. With senior stakeholders, keep it to the decision-relevant core; with an upset colleague, the feeling matters more than the facts.

## Common mistakes

- **Parroting.** Repeating the speaker's exact words sounds mechanical; the evidence is mostly for restatements that show you processed the message, including its emotional content.[^s1]
- **Paraphrasing as a lead-in to rebuttal.** If the restatement is a setup for "but", the other person learns it was a tactic. Negotiation guidance treats it as a step toward understanding before you share your perspective.[^s4]
- **Skipping the check.** A paraphrase without "Have I got that right?" is a claim, not a test.[^s4]
- **Doing it on every sentence.** Overuse can feel like an interrogation; save it for points that are complex, contested or emotional.
- **Treating it as the whole of listening.** In one study a paraphrase alone did not make people feel more understood; what helped was paraphrasing combined with follow-up questions.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence

The evidence is real but modest, so this article is rated mixed. In Weger, Castle and Emmett's 2010 experiment with 180 students, a paraphrased reflection made the listener seem more socially attractive than a simple acknowledgement, but it did not raise the speaker's sense of being understood or their satisfaction with the conversation.[^s2] A 2014 follow-up with 115 students, in which listeners paraphrased and asked for more detail, did find that speakers felt more understood and more satisfied than with simple acknowledgement or advice; advice-givers, however, were rated as just as likable.[^s3] Seehausen and colleagues' 2012 study of 20 adults found that paraphrasing reduced negative feelings about a conflict, while physiological arousal actually rose during paraphrasing; the authors flag the small sample, the silent control condition and the need for replication.[^s1] The 2024 meta-analysis supports the value of perceived listening in general but does not isolate paraphrasing, and its effects shrink when self-report bias is removed.[^s5]

## Connections

Paraphrasing is a narrower technique under **active listening** (`cm:active-listening`) and one of the main practices that develops that skill; researchers test it as a distinct element of active listening.[^s2] It is applied in **difficult conversations** (`cm:difficult-conversation`), where it lowers the emotional temperature of conflict discussions,[^s1] and it was introduced through **Carl Rogers**'s client-centred therapy (`cm:carl-rogers`).[^s1] Its support comes from **Seehausen et al. 2012** (`cm:seehausen-et-al-2012`)[^s1] and **Weger et al. 2014** (`cm:weger-et-al-2014`),[^s3] and it is challenged by **Weger, Castle and Emmett 2010** (`cm:weger-castle-emmett-2010`), where a paraphrase alone did not increase feeling understood.[^s2] It pairs naturally with **open-ended questions** (`cm:open-ended-questions`), which the 2014 study combined with paraphrasing,[^s3] and with **I-statements** and **SBI feedback**, which give your own view once the other person feels heard.[^s4]
