steps:
  - Decide what you actually need to learn - their view, the obstacles, the options - before you ask anything.
  - Open with "what" or "how" ("What's getting in the way?", "How did the client react?") instead of "Is", "Do" or "Did".
  - Stop talking and let them answer fully; resist offering a suggested answer if they pause.
  - Build your next question on what they just said ("You mentioned the handover - what happened there?") rather than switching topic.
  - Reflect back what you heard before asking another question, so the exchange does not become an interrogation.
  - Switch to a closed or specific question when you need a commitment, a number, or a straight answer to a known risk ("Will this slip past the 30th?").

# Open-ended questions

An open-ended question is one the other person cannot settle with "yes", "no" or a single fact: "What worries you about the rollout?" rather than "Are you worried about the rollout?". It hands the shape of the answer to the speaker, so you hear their account, in their words, instead of a confirmation or denial of your own guess.[^s4] Behavioural researchers Alison Wood Brooks and Leslie John describe open questions as the better tool for uncovering information and learning something you did not already know.[^s5]

## Origin

There is no single inventor. In counselling, open questions are the first element of the OARS skill set - open questions, affirmations, reflective listening and summaries - that motivational interviewing adapted from the client-centred tradition, with the aim that the client does most of the talking.[^s4] Investigative and child-protection interviewing developed the same preference independently, favouring invitational prompts over focused or option-posing ones because they draw more credible accounts.[^s3] Survey researchers met the issue from another angle: offering a list of answers changes what people say, which is why open formats are used to find out what respondents think unprompted.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Closed questions quietly import the asker's assumptions. In a survey example Brooks and John cite, about 60% of parents chose "to think for themselves" as a top value when it appeared on a list, but only about 5% offered it when the question was open.[^s5] At work the same thing happens when a manager asks "Is the deadline OK?" and hears only whether that one guess was right. Questions also change how you are seen: in chat experiments, people instructed to ask more questions - especially follow-ups that built on the partner's last answer - were rated as more responsive and were liked more, an effect participants did not predict.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Start with "what" and "how". Motivational-interviewing guidance advises avoiding "why", because it can sound like a demand to justify yourself and make people defensive; "what" or "how" gets the same information more gently.[^s4] Let the person finish, then follow their thread: in Huang and colleagues' coding of conversations, follow-up questions were the type that predicted both perceived responsiveness and liking, while abrupt topic switches did not help.[^s1] Pair questions with reflections and summaries so the conversation stays a dialogue rather than a questionnaire.[^s4]

**When to close the question.** Open questions are not always best. Brooks and John note that in tense negotiations or with guarded counterparts, open questions leave room to dodge or lie by omission, and pointed, specific questions work better.[^s5] Experiments by Minson and colleagues found that questions presuming a problem exists ("What problems have you had with it?") drew more honest disclosure of bad news than general or optimistic ones, in both written tasks and face-to-face recruiting meetings.[^s2]

**By channel and seniority.** In email or chat, one open question is easier to answer than five; with senior stakeholders, a focused open question ("What would make you comfortable approving this?") respects their time better than a broad "Any thoughts?". The principle stays the same: ask in a way that lets them tell you something you did not already assume.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Open in form, closed in intent.** "Don't you think we should launch Monday?" is a leading question that still signals the answer you want; the bias of offered options is exactly what open questions are meant to avoid.[^s5]
- **Too general to be useful.** "How's it going?" invites "fine". General questions were the least effective at surfacing undisclosed problems in Minson's studies.[^s2]
- **Interrogating.** A run of questions with no reflection feels like a cross-examination; the authors of the question-asking studies themselves suspect there is an optimal balance between asking and answering.[^s1][^s4]
- **Using open questions when you need a commitment.** When a decision or a number is required, ask for it directly.[^s5]

## Evidence

The core claims have support but depend on context, so this article is rated mixed. A 2019 Norwegian Institute of Public Health systematic review of seven field studies (239 children) found that open-ended questions generally elicited more truthful accounts than focused questioning, but the authors called the evidence insufficient for firm conclusions and did not grade its certainty.[^s3] Huang et al. (2017) showed experimentally that asking more questions increases liking, with follow-up questions driving the effect; the speed-dating part of the work was correlational, and the paper did not isolate open versus closed form.[^s1] Minson et al. (2018) challenge the idea that a neutral, general open question is always the most revealing: when the goal is to surface a concealed problem, a pointed question that presumes the problem did better, though such assertive questions can cost the asker some goodwill when the behaviour is uncommon.[^s2] Most workplace advice on question types comes from practitioners and these adjacent fields rather than workplace trials.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Open-ended questions are a form of **questioning** (`cm:questioning`) and **contrast with closed questions** (`cm:closed-questions`), which suit commitments and specific facts but bias answers when used to explore.[^s5] They **complement paraphrasing** (`cm:paraphrasing`): in the OARS model, open questions draw the person out and reflective listening shows you understood.[^s4] They are **supported by** the 2019 systematic review of open-ended questioning (`cm:berg-et-al-2019`)[^s3] and, for question-asking and follow-ups generally, by **Huang et al. 2017** (`cm:huang-et-al-2017`),[^s1] and **challenged by Minson et al. 2018** (`cm:minson-et-al-2018`), which found pointed, problem-presuming questions more revealing than general ones.[^s2] They relate to **active listening**, **follow-up questions**, **teach-back**, which checks understanding by asking people to explain in their own words, and **psychological safety**, the climate in which people answer candidly.[^s1][^s4]
