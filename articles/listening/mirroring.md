steps:
  - Let the other person finish their point before you respond, and notice the one to three words that carry the most weight or the most ambiguity.
  - Repeat those words back as a short question, in a calm and curious tone ("Under-resourced?").
  - Stop talking for a few seconds so they can expand, rather than filling the silence with your own view.
  - Use what they add to ask a follow-up or summarise, and switch to paraphrasing or labeling if a second mirror would start to sound mechanical.

# Mirroring

Mirroring is a listening move in which you repeat a small part of what someone just said, usually their last one to three words, as a gentle question, then wait. Negotiation trainers present it as a way to hand the conversation back to the other person so they reveal more without being asked a direct question.[^s5] The word is also used more broadly for deliberately matching a counterpart's posture or wording, which researchers call strategic behavioral or linguistic mimicry.[^s1][^s2] Both versions rest on the same idea: people tend to respond well to signs that they have been heard and that the other person is "like them".[^s4]

## Origin

Mirroring builds on decades of research into unconscious mimicry, the tendency to copy the gestures, postures and words of people we interact with. Tanya Chartrand and Jessica Lakin's 2013 *Annual Review of Psychology* article summarises this work, describing mimicry as pervasive and generally linked to more prosocial behaviour between the people involved, and placing verbal mimicry alongside emotional contagion and other forms of interpersonal coordination.[^s4] In 2008, William Maddux, Elizabeth Mullen and Adam Galinsky turned the automatic habit into a deliberate tactic and tested it in negotiations.[^s1] Negotiation trainers such as the Black Swan Group teach the verbal form as "the mirror": repeat the last few words with an inquisitive inflection, then stay quiet.[^s5]

## Why it matters

In meetings, people often say something vague, hedged or loaded and then move on; mirroring is a low-effort way to slow down on exactly that word and get the missing detail.[^s5] It also seems to build trust when used early. In Maddux and colleagues' second study, a sale that most pairs failed to close, 67% of dyads reached a deal when the buyer subtly mimicked the seller, against 12.5% in the control group, and the effect ran through the seller's higher trust in the buyer.[^s1] In their first study, an eight-issue job negotiation, pairs where one side mimicked created more joint value, and the mimicker gained without the other side losing.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Pick the word that matters, not just the last word: a hedge ("assuming"), a judgement ("unrealistic") or a vague label ("the issue"). Say it back with a curious, upward tone, then pause; the silence is part of the technique, giving the speaker time to process and continue.[^s5] Use it early in a conversation, when trust is forming. In a text-chat negotiation study, recruiters who reused candidates' words, jargon and emoticons during the first ten minutes earned better outcomes, through higher trust, than those who did so in the last ten minutes.[^s2] Late mimicry tended to echo accommodating language and helped the counterpart instead.[^s2] Pair mirroring with positive rather than neutral or negative language when writing.[^s6]

**By channel and seniority.** Live, a one- or two-word mirror is quick and natural. In email or chat, word-for-word echoes look odd, so mirror by adopting the other person's terms and framing in your first replies rather than swapping in your own vocabulary.[^s2][^s6] With executives, a single well-placed mirror on a key term ("Non-negotiable?") can surface constraints faster than a long question; repeated mirrors can feel like interrogation.

## Common mistakes

- **Parroting.** Echoing everything, or in a flat tone, sounds mechanical or mocking. Researchers instructed participants to keep mimicry subtle, and warned it could backfire if noticed.[^s1][^s6]
- **Skipping the pause.** A mirror followed immediately by your own point wastes the move; the silence is what invites elaboration.[^s5]
- **Mirroring late and agreeably.** In the chat study, echoing a counterpart's accommodating language late in a negotiation helped them more than the mimicker.[^s2]
- **Using it as a trick.** Mirroring is for understanding, not for faking agreement. Repeating someone's words to manufacture a false sense of alignment is manipulation; the line is whether you would be comfortable if they knew what you were doing and why.

## Evidence

The evidence is rated mixed. The negotiation studies are randomised but small: about 100 and 60 MBA students in the 2008 paper and 66 and 48 in the 2011 paper, each using classroom exercises.[^s1][^s2] Maddux and colleagues also report that mimicry did not help in a purely distributive, single-issue negotiation, and their trust mediation rests on one study.[^s1] The broader claim that being mimicked reliably increases liking and rapport is weaker than popular accounts suggest. In a 2016 virtual-reality study by Joanna Hale and Antonia Hamilton, a rapport effect in an exploratory study did not hold up in a pre-registered follow-up, and Bayesian analysis favoured no effect on rapport or trust; the authors note mixed results in earlier mimicry research too.[^s3] The verbal "last three words" technique itself rests mainly on practitioner experience.[^s5] Treat mirroring as a cheap way to draw out information and show attention, not a guaranteed route to trust.

## Connections

Mirroring is a narrow technique within **active listening** (`cm:active-listening`) and a lighter cousin of **paraphrasing** (`cm:paraphrasing`), which restates meaning in your own words. Its negotiation payoff was demonstrated by **Maddux, Mullen and Galinsky 2008** (`cm:maddux-mullen-galinsky-2008`), and it is applied above all in **negotiation** (`cm:negotiation`).[^s1] **Swaab, Maddux and Sinaceur 2011** (`cm:swaab-maddux-sinaceur-2011`) supports it for the verbal, text-based form and adds the timing caveat.[^s2] **Hale and Hamilton 2016** (`cm:hale-hamilton-2016`) challenges the assumption that mimicry reliably builds rapport.[^s3] The underlying mechanism is the automatic **chameleon effect** (`cm:chameleon-effect`) described in Chartrand and Lakin's review.[^s4] Practitioners pair mirrors with **labeling emotions** (`cm:labeling-emotions`), each followed by silence, which links it to **wait time** (`cm:wait-time`).[^s5] It supports **rapport** (`cm:rapport`) and is often followed by **open-ended questions** (`cm:open-ended-questions`) once the speaker has opened up.
