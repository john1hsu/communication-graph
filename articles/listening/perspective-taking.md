# Perspective-taking

Perspective-taking is the deliberate effort to see a situation from someone else's point of view: what they are trying to achieve, what constrains them, and how they are likely to read what you say.[^s1][^s4] Organizational researchers treat it as an effortful, goal-directed process that people can choose or be prompted to use, rather than a fixed trait.[^s4] At work it shows up whenever you anticipate a stakeholder's objection, frame a request around a peer's priorities, or look for a deal that serves both sides of a negotiation.[^s1] The modern evidence adds one crucial qualifier: imagining another person's view reduces self-centred thinking, but it does not by itself tell you what they actually think.[^s2]

## Origin

The construct comes from developmental and social psychology. Mark Davis's 1983 empathy measure treated perspective-taking as one cognitive sub-dimension of empathy, next to the emotional dimension of empathic concern, a framing later researchers say blurred the two.[^s4] Work and organizational psychologists, notably Sharon Parker and Carolyn Axtell from 2001 onward, recast it as a state that varies by relationship and situation, which is the version most useful to practitioners.[^s4] Adam Galinsky and colleagues then separated the cognitive and emotional routes experimentally in negotiation, defining perspective-taking as considering the world from other viewpoints and empathy as an other-focused emotional connection.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Understanding the other side's interests is what turns a stalemate into a deal. In Galinsky, Maddux, Gilin and White's 2008 studies with MBA students, buyers told to think about what a seller was thinking and what the seller's interests were reached a deal 76% of the time, against 39% for buyers focused on their own role; buyers told to imagine the seller's feelings reached 54%.[^s1] In a multi-issue hiring negotiation, recruiters prompted to take the candidate's perspective created more joint value and claimed more for themselves, while empathizing recruiters gave the candidate more but did not gain themselves.[^s1] Outside negotiation, a 2021 set of three studies found that colleagues who took a workplace actor's perspective blamed that person less and judged their performance and ideas more favourably.[^s4]

## What good looks like

A skilled perspective-taker treats their picture of the other person as a draft. They can describe the other side's goals, pressures and likely objections in that person's terms, then check those guesses with questions before acting on them.[^s2] They keep perspective-taking (understanding reasoning) distinct from empathy (sharing feelings), because in bargaining the cognitive route found creative trades while the emotional route led people to concede without finding them.[^s1] They also notice when imagining a rival's motives starts to sour their own behaviour, a side effect documented in competitive settings.[^s3]

## How to practise it

1. **Write their brief.** Before a negotiation, review or pitch, write three lines from the other person's side: what they want, what they fear, and what they must answer to.[^s1]
2. **Focus on thoughts and interests, not just feelings.** The instruction that worked in the 2008 studies asked people to understand what the other side was thinking and what their interests and purposes were.[^s1]
3. **Convert guesses into questions.** Turn each assumption into an open question you can ask; in Eyal and colleagues' final experiment, only directly asking the other person improved accuracy.[^s2]
4. **Name the risk of cynicism.** If imagining a counterpart leads you to expect selfishness, check that expectation against evidence before you act on it.[^s3]
5. **Debrief the gap.** After the conversation, compare what you predicted with what you heard, and note where your own view was colouring the guess.[^s2]

## Evidence

The status here is `mixed`. The negotiation benefits rest on three peer-reviewed experiments with MBA samples of about 70 to 150 people, so they are credible but small and simulated.[^s1] The strongest qualification comes from Eyal, Steffel and Epley's 2018 package of 25 experiments covering emotion reading, fake versus genuine smiles, lie detection and predicting a spouse's preferences: perspective-taking reduced egocentric bias but gave no consistent gain in accuracy, often lowered it slightly and sometimes raised confidence.[^s2] Epley, Caruso and Bazerman's experiments on dividing scarce resources showed a further cost: people who considered what was fair for every party said they should take less, yet actually took more, apparently because they expected others to act selfishly.[^s3] The 2021 workplace studies relied on vignettes, self-report and a lab task, which limits how far their findings generalise.[^s4] In short, use perspective-taking to generate hypotheses and soften judgment, and rely on asking to get the answer right.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a core interpersonal-communication skill, the cognitive counterpart to empathy.[^s1][^s4]
- **Demonstrated by** Galinsky, Maddux, Gilin and White (2008), which separated perspective-taking from empathy at the bargaining table.[^s1]
- **Applied in** salary and job-offer negotiation, the multi-issue hiring setting where recruiters who took the candidate's perspective created more joint value; it also underpins integrative negotiation more broadly, where understanding the other side's interests finds value-creating trades.[^s1]
- **Addresses** egocentric bias by pulling judgments away from one's own viewpoint, though without guaranteeing accuracy.[^s2]
- **Challenged by** Eyal, Steffel and Epley (2018), whose 25 experiments found no reliable accuracy benefit.[^s2]
- **Complements** perspective-getting, the practice of asking that the same studies found actually improves understanding.[^s2]
- **Challenged by** Epley, Caruso and Bazerman (2006), which showed perspective-taking can increase selfish behaviour in competitive settings.[^s3]
- **Related:** empathy, active listening and open-ended questions; Adam Galinsky and Nicholas Epley are the researchers most associated with its promise and its limits.[^s1][^s2]
