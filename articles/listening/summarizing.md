steps:
  - While the other person talks, note the separate points, concerns and decisions they raise, not just the last thing they said.
  - At a natural transition, a long monologue or before any decision, signal the summary ("Let me pull together what I've heard so far").
  - Select the points that matter for the next step and group them in two to four short chunks, in their language where possible; leave out your own opinions.
  - Include both sides when the person is torn, and name any open questions instead of quietly dropping them.
  - Close with a check ("What have I missed or got wrong?") and fix the summary out loud if they correct it.
  - Use the confirmed summary as the bridge to what comes next - a decision, an action list, or a new topic.
example:
  situation: A team lead is in a one-on-one with an engineer who has spent ten minutes describing problems with a cross-team project, and the lead is about to jump to a fix.
  before: "OK, sounds rough. I'll ping the platform team and ask them to prioritise your tickets."
  after: "Let me check I've got the whole picture. Three things: the platform team's API changes keep breaking your builds, nobody told you the deadline moved to March, and you're unsure whether you or Priya owns the migration. You also said the first one is the most urgent. Is that right, and is anything missing?"
  why: The rewrite gathers scattered points into one organised statement, records the speaker's own priority and invites correction, so the lead can act on the real problem rather than the most recent one mentioned.
selfCheck:

  - Did I leave my own opinions and solutions out of the summary itself?
  - Did I ask them to correct it, and did I change it when they did?
  - Did the summary lead clearly into the next step of the conversation?

# Summarizing

Summarizing is the listening move of gathering several things a person has said and handing them back as one compact, organised statement, then asking whether it is right. Counselling textbooks distinguish it from paraphrasing by scope: a paraphrase restates one point, while a summary covers more ground, such as a whole topic or session, and may capture feelings and meaning as well as facts.[^s4] In motivational interviewing the same idea appears as summaries that show how separate pieces of what someone said fit together, rather than reflecting one thing at a time.[^s3]

## Origin

Summarizing is one of the counselling microskills that Allen Ivey began codifying in his counsellor-education programmes around 1971; the current textbook version is Ivey, Ivey and Zalaquett's *Intentional Interviewing and Counselling*.[^s4] Miller and Rollnick later built summaries into motivational interviewing, where trainers teach three kinds: collecting summaries that gather related points, linking summaries that connect today's remarks to something said earlier, and transitional summaries that close one topic and move to the next step, such as planning.[^s3][^s5] In negotiation research, Neil Rackham and John Carlisle's 1978 study, one of the few to observe real face-to-face negotiations, listed summarizing among the clarifying behaviours they coded.[^s1][^s6]

## Why it matters

Long or emotional conversations leave each side holding a different version of what was said. A summary surfaces that difference while it is still cheap to fix.[^s4] Huthwaite's account of the negotiation research reports that 49 skilled negotiators, observed over 103 sessions, spent about 7.5% of their behaviour summarizing and 9.7% testing understanding, roughly double the rates of average negotiators (4.2% and 4.1%).[^s2] Counselling sources add a relational payoff: a good summary shows the person they were heard, invites them to keep exploring and helps steer the conversation towards a plan.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Listen across the whole stretch of talk, not just the last sentence, and pick out the points that belong together.[^s3] Summarize at transition points and before closing a topic, which is where counselling texts place it.[^s4] Be selective: motivational-interviewing trainers advise choosing what to include and giving the most weight to what will move the person forward, rather than replaying everything.[^s5] When someone is torn between options, a neutral summary of both sides helps them see where they are stuck.[^s3] Finish by inviting correction, then use the confirmed version to launch the next step - a decision, an action list or a new topic.[^s3][^s4]

The transition-point habit from counselling carries over to workplace talk: summarize before a meeting moves from discussion to decision, just as a counsellor summarizes before moving from exploring to planning.[^s3][^s4] The spoken summary is distinct from written [[meeting-recaps]], which record outcomes afterwards. In chat or email the correction loop is slower, so keep a typed summary short and ask explicitly for a reply confirming or correcting it, in the same spirit as the live check.[^s2][^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Summarizing only the latest point.** A summary that skips earlier concerns signals that you stopped listening; the technique is defined by its wider scope.[^s4]
- **Smuggling in your own position.** In negotiation coding, summarizing and testing understanding sit under clarifying, separate from proposing or disagreeing; mixing them turns a check into an argument.[^s6]
- **Replaying everything.** An unselective list wastes time and hides the priorities; effective summaries choose.[^s5]
- **Skipping the check.** Without an invitation to correct, the summary becomes a pronouncement rather than a test of understanding.[^s2]

## Evidence

The negotiation evidence is observational and old: Rackham and Carlisle compared behaviour frequencies of negotiators judged effective with those of average negotiators, which shows an association, not that summarizing causes better outcomes.[^s1][^s2] Huthwaite, which reports the figures, also sells training built on them.[^s2] The counselling and motivational-interviewing guidance rests mainly on practitioner consensus and training traditions rather than experiments isolating summaries.[^s3][^s5] The status here is therefore `practitioner`: widely taught, plausible and low-risk, but not separately tested as a stand-alone intervention.

## Connections

Summarizing sits under [[active-listening]] and builds that skill, because it trains listening for the whole message rather than the last line.[^s4] It extends [[paraphrasing]], which it requires and complements: paraphrase one point, summarize many.[^s4] It is part of motivational interviewing's core skill set.[^s3] Its strongest workplace evidence comes from [[negotiation]], where skilled negotiators summarized about twice as often as average ones in the study by Rackham and Carlisle.[^s2][^s6] It is a natural tool in [[one-on-one-meetings]], where a transition summary can close a topic before moving to actions.[^s4] Related techniques include [[teach-back]], which reverses the direction by asking the other person to summarize, [[open-ended-questions]] to gather what you will summarize, and written [[meeting-recaps]].
