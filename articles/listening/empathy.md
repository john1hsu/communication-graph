# Empathy

Empathy is the inclination and ability to grasp what another person is feeling and thinking, to feel some of it with them, and to let them see that you have understood. Researchers do not agree on one definition: a 2016 review in *Emotion Review* found 43 distinct definitions and sorted the disagreements into eight themes, including whether empathy is cognitive or affective, whether it must lead to behaviour, and whether it is automatic or controlled.[^s1] A useful working model comes from Stanford psychologist Jamil Zaki, who splits empathy into *experience sharing* (taking on some of another's emotional state), *mentalizing* (inferring their beliefs, intentions and feelings) and the basic perception that the other person has a mind at all, with helping behaviour as a downstream result.[^s2] At work, the communicative part matters most: empathy that the other person never perceives does little for the conversation.[^s4]

## Origin

Modern workplace and helping-profession ideas of empathy trace largely to psychologist Carl Rogers, who described it as sensing a client's inner world from the client's own frame of reference and treated it as a core condition of effective counselling.[^s4] Later psychotherapy researchers broadened Rogers's idea into a set of modes: building empathic rapport, staying attuned moment to moment in conversation, and holding a sustained understanding of the person's history and context.[^s4] Social psychology and neuroscience added the split between feeling-with and thinking-about, and Zaki's 2014 review reframed empathy as something people are motivated to approach or avoid, not a fixed reflex.[^s2]

## Why it matters

In a study by the Center for Creative Leadership of 6,731 managers in 38 countries, managers whom their direct reports rated as empathic were more likely to be rated as strong performers by their own bosses, a link that held most clearly for mid-level and senior managers.[^s5] The best-controlled evidence comes from counselling, where a 2018 meta-analysis of 82 samples and 6,138 clients found a moderate link between therapist empathy and client outcomes (r = .28).[^s4] The same analysis found that the client's sense of being understood predicted outcomes best, while tests of how accurately the helper read emotions predicted almost nothing, which suggests that *conveyed* understanding is what does the work.[^s4]

## What good looks like

An empathic colleague notices emotional cues, tries to understand the situation from the other person's frame, checks that reading instead of assuming it, and responds in a way the person recognises as understanding before moving to solutions.[^s4] Practitioners in psychotherapy recommend focusing on what an experience means to the person rather than only on their words, revising assumptions as the conversation unfolds, and asking how well the person feels understood.[^s4] In management, CCL highlights noticing signs of overwork, learning each person's needs and goals, staying open when someone has personal difficulties, and acknowledging a loss with compassion even without having lived through it.[^s5]

Consider a project lead whose analyst snaps in a stand-up that the new deadline is "impossible". A low-empathy reply defends the date. An empathic reply first names what is going on ("you have just absorbed two scope changes and now the date moved; that is a lot") and asks what would make the timeline workable, then discusses the deadline.[^s4][^s5] The deadline may not change, but the analyst is more likely to engage with the trade-offs once they feel heard.[^s4]

## How to practise it

Empathy can be trained. A 2016 meta-analysis of 18 randomised controlled trials (1,018 participants) found a medium average effect of training on measured empathy (g = 0.63, about 0.51 after adjusting for likely publication bias); effects were larger for health professionals and students and when measured objectively rather than by self-report.[^s3] Zaki's motivated account suggests a practical route: people avoid empathy when it feels painful, costly or at odds with competing, and approach it when they want connection or see it as valued, so leaders can make it easier by changing what people attend to and how they appraise others' emotions.[^s2]

- **Attend on purpose:** in one-on-ones, give the person your full attention and listen for tone and nonverbal cues, which CCL treats as part of active listening.[^s5]
- **Reflect, then check:** say back the feeling and the reason you think lies behind it, and invite correction.[^s4]
- **Ask about the person, not only the task:** regular check-ins on workload and goals surface strain before it becomes conflict.[^s5]
- **Notice avoidance:** when you catch yourself changing the subject or judging someone's reaction as overblown, ask whether that is a motive to avoid discomfort.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but mixed in quality. The leadership findings are correlational and use 360-degree ratings, so they cannot show that empathy causes better performance.[^s5] The strongest outcome data come from therapy, not offices, and training effects depend on who is trained and how empathy is measured.[^s3][^s4] Critics led by psychologist Paul Bloom argue that empathy spotlights single identifiable people, favours the in-group, and can push decisions that are not the most effective; one 2017 commentary agreed that empathy is biased but argued that compassion is equally biased unless guided by deliberate reasoning.[^s6] For managers, the practical lesson is to use empathy to understand people, and to use explicit criteria when allocating work, pay or exceptions.[^s6] Some people also find heavy displays of empathy intrusive, so the amount and style should fit the person.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a disposition within interpersonal communication, combining emotional sharing and inference about others.[^s2]
- **Supported by** Elliott, Bohart, Watson and Murphy (2018), the meta-analysis linking conveyed empathy to client outcomes.[^s4]
- **Supported by** Teding van Berkhout and Malouff (2016), showing that empathy responds to training.[^s3]
- **Applied in** one-on-one meetings, where regular check-ins on workload and goals give managers the chance to notice and respond to how people are doing.[^s5]
- **Complements** active listening, the observable skill through which empathy is conveyed; CCL recommends teaching it, with attention to tone and nonverbal cues, to build empathic leaders.[^s5]
- **Influenced by** Carl Rogers, whose client-centred definition still anchors research on empathic communication.[^s4]
- **Challenged by** Paul Bloom's 2017 critique that empathy is narrow and biased, discussed in a peer-reviewed commentary.[^s6]
- **Related:** perspective-taking and perspective-getting (the mentalizing side, and asking to confirm it), labeling emotions, paraphrasing and the support response (ways to show understanding), nonviolent communication and role reversal (structured practices that build it), and the hot-cold empathy gap and emotional contagion (limits and side effects of feeling with others).[^s2][^s4]
