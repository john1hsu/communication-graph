steps:
  - When someone raises a topic, notice the urge to reply with your own matching story, opinion or advice, and hold it for one turn.
  - Make your first reply about their topic - a short acknowledgement, a paraphrase of what you heard, or an open question such as "What happened next?"
  - Build your next question on a specific word or detail in their answer, so it could only be asked by someone who was listening.
  - Share your own experience only if it is asked for or clearly useful, keep it brief, and hand the topic back with a question.
  - Afterwards, roughly count your shift and support replies; if shifts dominated, open the next conversation with two support replies in a row.
example:
  situation: At the start of a project stand-up, an analyst mentions that the data migration over the weekend took twelve hours instead of three.
  before: "Lead: 'Twelve hours is nothing - on my last programme we had a migration run for three days straight. Anyway, let's go round the board.'"
  after: "Lead: 'Twelve hours - that's a long weekend. What slowed it down?' [Analyst explains a vendor API kept timing out.] 'So the timeouts are the real risk for the next run. What would you need to stop that happening?'"
  why: The first reply is a shift response that turns the analyst's report into the lead's war story and closes the topic. The second uses a support response and a follow-up question, which surfaces a risk the whole team needs to know about and shows the analyst they were heard.

# Support response

A support response is a reply that encourages the other person to keep going on their own topic - in effect, "tell me more" - rather than redirecting attention to yourself.[^s5] Its opposite is the shift response, which takes what someone has said and turns it into an opening for your own story or opinion: a colleague mentions a lost dog, and you reply that yours is scared of its own shadow.[^s5] A support reply to the same remark would be "Oh no - where did you finally find him?"[^s5]

## Origin

The distinction comes from the Boston College sociologist Charles Derber, who recorded and transcribed more than a hundred informal dinner conversations and sorted the replies into shift and support responses.[^s5] Shift responses were the more common of the two in his recordings, and he treated their habitual use as the core of what he called conversational narcissism.[^s5] The journalist Kate Murphy brought the idea to a wider audience in her book on listening, recommending that people monitor their own balance of shift and support replies.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Experimental work on question-asking, the most studied kind of support reply, shows a clear social payoff. In three studies of live conversations - online get-to-know-you chats and face-to-face speed dating - Karen Huang and colleagues found in 2017 that people who asked more questions, especially follow-up questions, were better liked by their partners.[^s1] Participants told to ask more questions were seen as more responsive, and that perceived responsiveness explained the extra liking; speed daters who asked more follow-up questions were more likely to get a second date.[^s1] Notably, people did not predict that asking questions would make them more likeable, which helps explain why shift responses stay so common.[^s1]

The mechanism is perceived partner responsiveness: the sense that a conversation partner understands, values and cares about you.[^s3] A 2022 review by Guy Itzchakov, Harry Reis and Netta Weinstein argues that high-quality listening is one of the main behaviours that creates this feeling, and that responsiveness in turn feeds well-being, openness and closer relationships.[^s3][^s4] At work, the practical benefit is information: each support reply keeps open a thread the other person chose to raise, so problems and ideas surface instead of being talked over.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Start by noticing the reflex. When someone raises a topic, the pull is to match it with your own experience or to jump to advice; delay that by one turn.[^s5] Make your first reply about their topic, using one of the moves high-quality listeners rely on: steady attention, a paraphrase that shows what you understood, or an open question inviting them to say more.[^s4] Then build on a specific detail in their answer - Murphy notes that once you have asked a relevant question, you have a stake in hearing the answer, which keeps you listening.[^s5] Finally, share your own view when it helps, but treat it as one turn in a give-and-take and hand the topic back.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

Support responses fail when they are mechanical. Itzchakov, Reis and Weinstein caution that speakers detect inauthentic listening quickly, so a stream of formulaic "interesting, tell me more" replies without real attention will not build responsiveness.[^s4] A second mistake is overcorrecting into silence about yourself: Murphy stresses that conversation is a give-and-take, and telling a personal story does not by itself make someone a narcissist.[^s5] A third is the disguised shift - unrequested advice, or a question asked only to set up your own answer - which looks like support but moves the focus back to you.[^s5] Distractions such as checking a phone, interrupting or passing judgement undo the effect of even well-chosen words.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence for question-asking is experimental but rests on one research team's studies. When Kluger and Malloy criticised the speed-dating analyses, the original authors replied in 2019 that the critique tested the wrong variables and that the original results held across all three studies; the exchange is a reason to treat the effect as real but still modestly replicated.[^s2] Derber's shift/support categories come from qualitative sociological observation, not controlled experiments, and are best used as a practical lens rather than a measured construct.[^s5] The responsiveness model is a review that links two research literatures; it does not itself test support responses directly.[^s3]

## Connections

A support response **addresses conversational narcissism** (`cm:conversational-narcissism`), the habit of shifting talk to yourself, and was **introduced by Charles Derber** (`cm:charles-derber`) as the counterpart of the shift response.[^s5] Its benefits are **demonstrated by Huang et al. 2017** (`cm:huang-et-al-2017`), where follow-up questions raised liking, and **explained by perceived partner responsiveness** (`cm:perceived-partner-responsiveness`).[^s1][^s3] Practising it **develops active listening** (`cm:active-listening`), its broader skill, because each reply requires attention and shows understanding.[^s4] The main forms of support reply are **follow-up questions** (`cm:follow-up-questions`), **open-ended questions** (`cm:open-ended-questions`) and **paraphrasing** (`cm:paraphrasing`), and its common counterfeit is **boomerasking** (`cm:boomerasking`).[^s1][^s4][^s5] It keeps **conversational turn-taking** (`cm:conversational-turn-taking`) balanced while leaving room for **reciprocal self-disclosure** (`cm:reciprocal-self-disclosure`), which together build **rapport** (`cm:rapport`).[^s3][^s5]
