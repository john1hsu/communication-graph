# Active listening

Active listening is the skill of trying to grasp the whole of what another person means - what they are saying and what they feel about it - and letting them see that you have grasped it, before you evaluate, advise or argue.[^s1] It is a skill rather than a single technique: paraphrasing, open questions and attentive silence are its tools, but Rogers and Farson insisted that the tools ring hollow without genuine respect for the speaker and a willingness to see the issue from their side.[^s1] In negotiation training it is described as asking open questions, pressing for specifics and then demonstrating that you understand the other party's view.[^s5]

## Origin

The term comes from psychologist Carl Rogers and Richard Farson, whose 1957 essay for the University of Chicago's Industrial Relations Center adapted Rogers's client-centred counselling for supervisors and everyday workplace problems.[^s1] They argued that every message carries both content and an underlying feeling, and that the listener's job is to respond to the total meaning.[^s1] Later management training often narrowed the idea to a checklist of behaviours such as paraphrasing; listening researchers Kluger and Itzchakov note this drift and prefer to study listening quality as a combination of attention, comprehension and a non-judgmental, helpful intention.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Rogers and Farson observed that people defend their self-image when they feel judged, and that being understood lowers that threat, so listening can shift attitudes that argument hardens; they also noted that a manager's listening tends to spread to the team.[^s1] Modern research is broadly consistent. A 2024 meta-analysis pooling 664 effect sizes found that feeling listened to at work is associated with better relationships (the strongest link), clearer thinking, more positive job attitudes and higher performance.[^s4] A 2022 review links good listening with trust, leadership ratings, job satisfaction and lower burnout, and reports experiments in which speakers who were listened to well felt more psychologically safe and less socially anxious.[^s2]

## What good looks like

- **Attention:** devices away, eyes and body oriented to the speaker, no rehearsing your reply while they talk.[^s2]
- **Comprehension:** you listen for feeling as well as facts, including cues in tone, hesitation, posture and facial expression.[^s1]
- **Reflection and checking:** you restate their meaning in your own words and keep checking until they agree you have it.[^s1]
- **Questions that open up:** you ask open questions and probe for specifics rather than steering toward your answer.[^s5]
- **Withheld judgment:** you resist evaluating, advising, persuading and quick reassurance - Rogers and Farson warn that even praise or encouragement can shut down honest talk.[^s1]

A workplace example: an engineer tells her lead the release date is unrealistic. The weak response defends the date or offers fixes. The active-listening response reflects both parts - that she sees two integrations still untested and is uneasy about being blamed if it slips - checks that summary, then asks what would have to be true for the date to hold. Only then does the lead share constraints.[^s1][^s5]

## How to practise it

Use the discipline Rogers and Farson proposed for heated discussions: before you state your view, restate the other person's point to their satisfaction.[^s1] Practise it first in low-stakes one-on-ones, then in disagreements. Pick one hindrance to remove - phones on the table, unsolicited advice and status-driven interrupting are all named as common obstacles - and remove it for a week.[^s2] Ask a trusted colleague afterwards whether they felt heard; Kluger and Itzchakov note that training reliably changes listeners' behaviour but there is little evidence that speakers notice the change, so the speaker's view is the real test.[^s2]

**By channel, seniority and culture.** In email or chat, open a reply by restating what you understand the sender needs before answering. With senior stakeholders, reflect the decision-relevant core; with peers or reports in distress, the feeling matters more. Most listening research is American and one experiment found effects only among American participants, so expectations about eye contact and emotional reflection may differ across cultures.[^s2]

## Evidence

The evidence supports listening in general more firmly than any particular active-listening script, so this article is rated mixed. In a 2014 experiment with 115 participants, people who received active listening (paraphrase plus questions) felt more understood than those who received advice or a simple acknowledgement, but satisfaction and liking were no higher than for advice, and the effects were small.[^s3] The 2024 meta-analysis found substantial associations, yet about three-quarters of the data are self-reports; using only other-rated data, the link with job performance fell to about r = .21.[^s4] The 2022 review adds that most work outcomes are correlational, only one experiment directly tested listening's effect on trust, and the construct's distinctness from related ideas is not yet settled.[^s2]

## Connections

Active listening was **introduced by Carl Rogers** (`cm:carl-rogers`) with Richard Farson, and it **addresses defensiveness** (`cm:defensiveness`), which they argued understanding reduces.[^s1] It is **applied in one-on-one meetings** (`cm:one-on-one-meetings`) between supervisors and staff and in **difficult conversations** (`cm:difficult-conversation`), the two settings their essay centres on.[^s1] It **complements open-ended questions** (`cm:open-ended-questions`), which negotiation training treats as part of the same listening move.[^s5] It is **supported by Weger et al. 2014** (`cm:weger-et-al-2014`), where it made speakers feel more understood,[^s3] and by the **Kluger et al. 2024 meta-analysis** (`cm:kluger-et-al-2024`) of perceived listening at work.[^s4] **Paraphrasing** is its core verbal technique, and it pairs with **I-statements** once the other person feels heard and with **psychological safety**, which good listening was found to raise.[^s2]
