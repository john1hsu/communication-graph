# Interrupting

Interrupting is taking the floor before the current speaker has finished their point, typically to redirect, disagree or move the discussion on. Researchers separate *intrusive* interruptions, which take the turn away and change direction, from overlaps that support the speaker, and the costs described here belong mainly to the intrusive kind.[^s4] In experiments, people who interrupted were rated as having more status and influence but were liked less, and the person interrupted lost standing in observers' eyes and in their own.[^s1]

## Origin

Interruption became a research topic through conversation analysis and the sociolinguistics of gender in the 1970s and 1980s, when studies coded who cut in on whom in recorded talk. By 1998 Kristin Anderson and Campbell Leaper could pool 43 published studies of adult women and men in a meta-analysis, which also showed how much the answer depended on how interruptions were defined and where they were observed.[^s4] Later work moved from dyads to task groups and asked how interruptions shape who keeps talking over a whole discussion.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Interrupting changes how people are seen. In Sally Farley's two experiments, one with a recorded conversation and one with live confederates, interrupters gained perceived status while their targets lost it, interrupted participants rated themselves as less influential, and interrupters were liked less, with the likeability penalty larger for women who interrupted.[^s1] In a team, that means a habitual interrupter can win the moment while eroding goodwill, and the people they cut off can come away feeling sidelined.[^s1]

Interruptions also compound. In a 2019 analysis of 29 six-person task groups, people who had already been interrupted were more likely to be interrupted again, and early interruptions predicted lower participation later; the authors call this a cumulative conversational disadvantage.[^s2] For meetings, the first few minutes therefore matter: whoever gets cut off early may say little for the rest of the discussion.[^s2]

## How to spot it

Watch what the incoming speaker does with the turn. If they finish the other person's sentence and carry on with a new topic, contradict before the point is made, or take the floor while the speaker is mid-clause, that is an intrusive interruption.[^s4] Brief overlaps that agree, echo or encourage ("right", "exactly") are different, and listeners do not all judge them the same way: in a 500-person perception study, whether an overlap sounded competitive depended both on the speaker's pitch and loudness and on the listener's own conversational style.[^s3] So a colleague who overlaps enthusiastically may be read as rude by some people and as engaged by others; ask, rather than assume.[^s3]

In groups, look for patterns, not single incidents: the same people being cut off, and the same people never needing to cut in because others hand them the floor.[^s2]

## How to fix it

- **Let the sentence land.** Since cutting in costs you likeability and costs the other person standing, wait until the speaker has clearly finished before replying; if you notice you have cut in, stop and say "Sorry, go on."[^s1]
- **Keep overlaps supportive.** Because listeners judge overlaps partly by pitch and loudness, keep any overlapping "yes" or "right" quiet and brief, and save disagreement for your own turn.[^s3]
- **Protect early turns.** As a facilitator, give each person a first speaking turn, respond to the first interruption you see ("Let's let Sam finish"), and hand the floor back explicitly; the 2019 study suggests these early moves stop interruptions snowballing.[^s2]
- **Reclaim your turn calmly.** If you are cut off, finish the point in a level voice and then invite the other person in, instead of yielding at once.[^s1]

## Evidence

The core findings come from peer-reviewed experiments and observational studies.[^s1][^s2] Popular claims that men constantly interrupt women outrun the evidence: across 43 studies men interrupted more overall, but the effect was negligible (d = .15), larger for intrusive interruptions (d = .33), and bigger in naturalistic settings and groups of three or more than in lab dyads.[^s4] The 2019 group study found no gender difference in who interrupts, though women were about 30% more likely to be interrupted, an effect carried by having been interrupted earlier in the discussion.[^s2] Because cultures and individuals differ in how much overlap they tolerate, treat interruption as a context-dependent pattern rather than a fixed rule.[^s3]

## Connections

Interrupting is a breakdown of **conversational turn-taking** (`cm:conversational-turn-taking`), and the 2019 task-group study explains its snowball effect through turn-taking dynamics.[^s2] It is **demonstrated by Farley 2008** (`cm:farley-2008`), the status-versus-likeability experiments,[^s1] and by **Cannon, Robinson and Smith-Lovin 2019** (`cm:cannon-robinson-smith-lovin-2019`).[^s2] Because interrupters are liked less, it **undermines rapport** (`cm:rapport`).[^s1] It **contrasts with cooperative overlap** (`cm:cooperative-overlap`), supportive simultaneous talk that some listeners hear as engagement.[^s3] Remedies sit in **active listening**, **wait time** and **paraphrasing**, while **facilitation**, **meeting norms** and **psychological safety** shape whether a group lets interruptions slide.[^s2]
