# Zoom fatigue

Zoom fatigue, also called videoconference fatigue, is the drained, low-energy feeling many people report after a day of video meetings. The name comes from one product, but the problem applies to any video platform.[^s1] Researchers describe it as having physical, emotional, cognitive and social sides.[^s6] At work it looks like the analyst who is worn out by 3 p.m. after six back-to-back calls, or the new hire who keeps the camera on all day and goes quiet as the afternoon goes on.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

The phrase spread in the popular press in 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic moved most meetings online.[^s2] In 2021 Stanford communication professor Jeremy Bailenson gave it a theory in *Technology, Mind, and Behavior*. He argued that "nonverbal overload" comes from four design features: close-up eye gaze from a grid of large faces, the extra work of sending and reading cues on screen, a constant self-view that acts like an all-day mirror, and being kept still inside the camera frame.[^s1] Bailenson said these were hypotheses built from older research and had not yet been tested directly on video calls.[^s1] Researchers then measured the effect in field and lab studies.[^s2][^s3][^s4][^s5]

## Why it matters

Fatigue changes how people take part in meetings. In a four-week field experiment, 103 employees were assigned to keep their cameras on or off on different days, giving 1,408 daily reports. Camera-on days produced more fatigue, and on those days people spoke up less and were less engaged in meetings.[^s2] The effect was stronger for women and for employees with less time in the organization, who may feel more pressure to look engaged on screen.[^s2] Further analyses suggested fatigue hurt meeting performance the same day and the next.[^s2] A survey of 9,787 people also found that women reported more Zoom fatigue than men, mainly because they reported more mirror anxiety and a stronger sense of being trapped in the frame.[^s3]

## Evidence

The effect appears to be real, but which features cause it is still debated. In the 9,787-person survey, people who used video more often, for longer, and with fewer breaks reported more fatigue. Mirror anxiety, feeling trapped, the sense of being stared at, and the effort of producing and reading cues each added to fatigue beyond how much video people used.[^s3] Those data are cross-sectional self-reports, so they show links rather than causes.[^s3] A 2023 lab experiment with 35 students compared a 50-minute lecture given by video with the same lecture given in person. EEG and heart measures, along with questionnaires, showed more fatigue in the video condition.[^s5]

The camera question is less clear. Shockley and colleagues found that turning the camera on caused fatigue.[^s2] Bennett and colleagues surveyed 55 remote employees every hour for a working week, more than 1,700 surveys in all. They found that a sense of group belonging did the most to reduce fatigue, and that early-afternoon meetings were less tiring. Camera use, self-view and meeting length had no significant effect on fatigue after meetings.[^s4] A 2023 systematic review found only 14 studies that met its criteria and noted that much of the discussion still rests on anecdote.[^s6] Most studies were done during the pandemic, when remote work was new and stress was high, so the status here is `mixed`: the overall fatigue effect has support, but the size of each mechanism, especially the camera, is still uncertain.[^s4][^s6]

## How to spot it

- You or your team feel more drained after a day of calls than after a day of in-person meetings with the same people.[^s5]
- Attention fades and fewer people speak in the third or fourth back-to-back call, especially among newer colleagues.[^s2]
- People spend the call adjusting their framing, watching their own tile or exaggerating nods to show they are listening.[^s1][^s3]
- People feel stuck in their chair and cannot stand, stretch or pace while they think.[^s1][^s3]

## How to fix it

1. Choose the channel first. Use a phone call, a shared document or an async update when a conversation does not need faces.[^s1][^s4]
2. Make cameras optional by default, and tell newer staff directly that turning the camera off is fine.[^s2]
3. Hide self-view once you have checked your framing, and shrink the gallery so faces are smaller.[^s1][^s3]
4. Leave gaps between calls, and take breaks to stand, move or look away from the screen.[^s3][^s4]
5. Spend the first minutes building connection, such as a check-in, a breakout pair or small talk. Group belonging was the strongest buffer in Bennett's study.[^s4]
6. Let people move. An external webcam or a set audio-only stretch gives them room to stand or walk while they listen.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a problem within video-call communication and workplace meetings more generally.[^s1][^s4]
- **Explained by** nonverbal overload, Bailenson's account of gaze, cue effort, self-view and reduced movement.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Shockley and colleagues' 2021 within-person camera experiment and by Riedl and colleagues' 2023 EEG/ECG comparison of video and in-person lectures.[^s2][^s5]
- **Supported by** Fauville and colleagues' 2023 survey of nearly 10,000 people, which linked the proposed nonverbal mechanisms to fatigue.[^s3]
- **Challenged by** Bennett and colleagues' 2021 hourly study, which found no significant effect of camera use or self-view and pointed to group belonging instead.[^s4]
- **Undermines** engagement and voice in meetings, according to the camera experiment.[^s2]
- **Related:** channel selection and media richness (choosing a leaner channel is often the fix), asynchronous communication, proxemics (screen faces appear at close personal distance), the spotlight effect (self-focus from a constant mirror) and information overload.[^s1][^s3][^s6]
