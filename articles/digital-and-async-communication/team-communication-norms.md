steps:
  - In the first weeks of a project (or when membership changes), book a short session to agree how the team will communicate, rather than letting habits form by default.
  - Map channels to message types - for example chat for quick coordination, email or the project tool for decisions and records, a call or live meeting for sensitive or interpersonal issues.
  - Agree default reply windows per channel and each person's working hours and time zone, plus how to flag something genuinely urgent.
  - Name a short list of always-do and never-do behaviours for meetings and threads (start on time, give everyone a turn, no talking over people), and how anyone can call out a breach.
  - Write the agreement in one shared place, ask new joiners to read it, and have leads model it visibly.
  - Put a recurring five-minute "how are we working?" check on the team agenda and revise any norm that is being ignored or no longer fits.
example:
  situation: A product team split across London and Singapore keeps missing hand-offs; some people answer chat at midnight, others feel ignored, and a tense design disagreement escalates in a long email thread.
  before: "No agreement. Requests arrive by chat, email and comments at all hours; whoever replies fastest gets the work; the design dispute runs to 40 emails before anyone talks."
  after: "One-page charter in the team wiki - Chat: same working day. Email/tracker: decisions and anything others must find later, reply within two working days. Urgent: phone the on-call owner. Core overlap 08:00-10:00 London. Disagreements about people or direction: move to a call after two replies. Reviewed monthly in the retro."
  why: Each person no longer has to guess the urgency, channel or hours others expect, so hand-offs land, off-hours pressure drops, and conflict moves to a channel that can carry it.

# Team communication norms

Team communication norms are the shared expectations a team holds about how its members talk to each other: which channel carries which kind of message, how quickly people reply, when they are reachable, how meetings run and what conduct is out of bounds.[^s3] Every group develops norms, but most form implicitly and are noticed only when someone breaks them; this technique makes the important ones explicit, usually as a short written team charter or working agreement, and keeps them under review.[^s3][^s1]

## Origin

Group norms are a long-standing topic in small-group and organizational research. Daniel Feldman's 1984 analysis argued that workgroups enforce norms that help the group survive, make behaviour predictable, avoid embarrassment and express the group's values, and J. Richard Hackman observed that norms usually emerge gradually as situations demand them, though a group can speed the process up deliberately.[^s3] Team-research scholars then studied the deliberate route: Mathieu and Rapp's 2009 study of team charters - written plans for how members will work together - treated the charter as a tool for setting teamwork expectations before problems arise.[^s1] Practitioner versions spread with distributed work; Sharon Hill (George Washington University) and Kathryn Bartol (University of Maryland), studying globally dispersed teams, recommend a charter that states which technologies to use for which tasks and how fast to answer new requests.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Implicit norms differ across people, offices and cultures, so a team that never discusses them ends up with members applying different rules without realizing it.[^s3][^s4] Haas and Mortensen, building on Hackman's work and their own 15 years of team studies, list clear norms of conduct as part of the structure great teams need, and note they matter most when members come from different national or organizational cultures and when membership keeps changing.[^s4] In Hill and Bartol's study of dispersed teams at a multinational, teams that communicated better virtually - choosing suitable tools, staying in sync, being responsive - were rated as higher-performing by their managers.[^s6] Agreed norms can also break an "always on" cycle: in Perlow and Porter's experiments at the Boston Consulting Group, teams that committed to predictable, fully offline time off and discussed their working process weekly reported more open communication and greater job satisfaction than comparison teams.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Agree the norms early, in a short dedicated conversation, and cover the questions that cause friction: channel by message type, reply windows, working hours and time zones, how to escalate something urgent, and a small number of always-do and never-do meeting behaviours.[^s6][^s4] Haas and Mortensen recommend keeping the list short - a few behaviours everyone must always show, such as punctuality and giving each person a chance to speak, and a few they must never show, such as interrupting.[^s4] Hill and Bartol add practical items: handle sensitive or interpersonal issues outside email, standardize small formats such as marking action items, set response-time expectations, and agree what gets shared with the whole team.[^s6] Then build in a routine review: Perlow and Porter's teams held a regular check-in on how the team was working, which surfaced problems early, and senior people visibly following the norm made it acceptable for others to do the same.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Writing it and forgetting it.** A 2017 study of 239 student project teams measured how good each charter was, and charter quality was what predicted performance in less conscientious teams; a vague, box-ticking charter gives little to steer by.[^s2]
- **Assuming everyone already knows.** The Maryland researchers warn managers not to assume members understand the pitfalls of virtual communication; new joiners need the norms restated.[^s6][^s4]
- **Leaders exempting themselves.** In the BCG experiments, the norm took hold when partners attended the check-ins and took their own time off.[^s5]
- **Too many rules.** A long rulebook becomes background noise; a short list of behaviours that matter is easier to keep.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is encouraging but narrow. Mathieu and Rapp followed 32 MBA teams in a business simulation and found that teams with both high-quality charters and high-quality performance strategies sustained the strongest performance over time.[^s1] Courtright and colleagues found that charter quality mattered mainly for teams low in conscientiousness, suggesting charters substitute for, rather than add to, members who would organize themselves anyway.[^s2] Both used student teams, and the Perlow and Porter work is a field experiment in one consulting firm reported in a practitioner journal, so effects in other organizations are less certain.[^s1][^s2][^s5] Hill and Bartol's finding concerns communication behaviours generally, not charters specifically.[^s6] The status is therefore **mixed**: well-made agreements appear to help, especially for less self-organizing or distributed teams, but the size of the benefit is not settled.[^s2][^s6]

## Connections

Team communication norms are a practice within **group communication** (`cm:group-communication`) and **digital communication** (`cm:digital-communication`), their broader fields.[^s3][^s6] The approach is **supported by Mathieu and Rapp 2009** (`cm:mathieu-rapp-2009`), the team-charter growth study,[^s1] and by **Courtright et al. 2017** (`cm:courtright-et-al-2017`), which qualifies the benefit by team conscientiousness.[^s2] Agreed offline time and reply windows **address workplace telepressure** (`cm:workplace-telepressure`), the felt pressure to answer messages immediately,[^s5] and explicit meeting rules **address interrupting** (`cm:interrupting`).[^s4] Norms are especially valuable **in multicultural teams** (`cm:multicultural-team`), where members bring different defaults,[^s4] and **in asynchronous communication** (`cm:asynchronous-communication`), where response-time expectations replace live cues.[^s6] Narrower practices that sit under this umbrella include **setting reply-time expectations** (`cm:setting-reply-time-expectations`), **chat etiquette** (`cm:chat-etiquette`), the **camera-optional norm** (`cm:camera-optional-norm`) and the **CREW civility intervention** (`cm:crew-civility-intervention`); the channel rules draw on **channel selection** (`cm:channel-selection`), and the habit of discussing how the team works relates to **psychological safety** (`cm:psychological-safety`).[^s5][^s6]
