# Camera-optional norm

A camera-optional norm is a clear, shared agreement that webcams are a choice in video meetings rather than a default requirement. It is more than a line in a policy: the expectation is set for each kind of meeting, written down, and shown by the people who run the meetings.[^s2][^s3] It differs from a blanket cameras-off rule. The researchers who found the strongest camera effect said they were not arguing for cameras to be off all the time, but for giving people autonomy and planning meetings more deliberately.[^s3] In practice it means a project lead can say "stand-up is audio-only, design reviews are cameras-on for the first ten minutes" and mean it.[^s3]

## Origin

The idea grew out of research on Zoom fatigue during the COVID-19 shift to remote work. In February 2021 Stanford communication professor Jeremy Bailenson set out four likely causes of video-call tiredness and suggested fixes, including turning video off periodically as a group norm, hiding self-view, and taking audio-only breaks during long meetings.[^s2] Later that year, Kristen Shockley, Allison Gabriel and colleagues tested the camera piece directly in a field experiment, and practitioners turned the findings into advice for managers on camera policies.[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

In Shockley's four-week experiment, 103 employees were assigned to keep cameras on or off on different days, giving 1,408 daily reports. Camera-on days were more tiring, and the extra fatigue went with less speaking up and less engagement in meetings.[^s1] The cost was larger for women and for newer employees. The authors linked this to self-presentation pressure, the work of looking attentive and competent on screen.[^s1][^s3] Without an explicit norm, people tend to copy what they see: a 2022 survey of 333 people found that the share of visible webcams strongly predicted whether someone turned their own camera on, and expectations from others mattered in professional meetings.[^s5] So a manager who keeps their own camera on all day, or comments on dark tiles, sets an unspoken rule even if no one wrote one.[^s3][^s5]

## How to apply it

Start from purpose, not habit. Agree per meeting type when faces add value, such as small working sessions or first meetings, and when they do not, such as large updates.[^s3] Write the agreement down, then model it yourself, because seeing others switch off is what makes people feel free to do so.[^s3][^s5] Say it directly to newer staff, who feel the most pressure to stay visible.[^s1][^s3] When video is on, suggest hiding self-view once framing looks right and add short audio-only breaks, two of Bailenson's suggested fixes.[^s2] Keep the human side: group belonging was the strongest buffer against fatigue in one hourly study, so a short check-in with cameras on can be worth more than an hour of faces.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Saying "optional" but rewarding cameras-on.** If the leader is always on screen or praises "showing up on camera", people read the real rule from behaviour, not the policy.[^s3][^s5]
- **Swinging to cameras-off for everything.** Some people find cameras-off meetings impersonal, and video still helps small groups and new relationships.[^s3][^s4]
- **Losing participation.** With cameras off, people may multitask as they did on old conference calls; agree a no-multitasking norm and call on people by name.[^s3][^s4]
- **Treating it as the only fix.** Choosing a lighter channel, leaving breaks between calls and scheduling well also matter; in one study early-afternoon meetings were less tiring.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is `mixed`. Shockley's within-person experiment is the strongest causal test and supports letting people turn cameras off.[^s1] But Bennett and colleagues, surveying 55 employees hourly across a working week (more than 1,700 surveys), found no significant effect of camera use or self-view on fatigue after meetings; group belonging mattered more.[^s4] Both studies ran during the pandemic, when remote work was new.[^s1][^s4] The norms survey was correlational and measured willingness to use webcams, not outcomes.[^s5] Bailenson's causes were hypotheses when he proposed them.[^s2] The safest reading is that choice and clear expectations help, especially for those under the most pressure to perform on screen, while connection still needs active effort.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** one of the norms a team sets for how its meetings run.[^s3]
- **Addresses** Zoom fatigue, by letting people rest from the camera on the days and meetings where video adds little.[^s1]
- **Applied in** video calls, where Bailenson proposed turning video off periodically as a group norm.[^s2]
- **Supported by** Shockley and colleagues' 2021 camera experiment.[^s1]
- **Challenged by** Bennett and colleagues' 2021 hourly study, which found no significant camera effect.[^s4]
- **Explained by** self-presentation theory: being on camera adds the work of managing how one looks.[^s1]
- **Introduced by** Jeremy Bailenson, whose 2021 fixes popularised camera breaks as a team norm.[^s2]
- **Related:** channel selection (sometimes the answer is no video call at all), psychological safety (people must feel safe to switch off), the spotlight effect (self-view feeds self-focus) and workplace meetings generally.[^s2][^s3][^s4]
