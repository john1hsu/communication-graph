# Phone call

A phone call is a live conversation carried by voice alone, whether on a desk phone, a mobile or an audio-only internet call. It sits between writing and meeting: it is as fast and interactive as talking in person, but the listener has only words, tone, pace and silence to go on.[^s6] Research on voice suggests that this narrower channel is often an advantage for connection and for reading how someone feels, and a disadvantage for gathering detail, so using the phone well means choosing it for the right jobs and making up for what it lacks.[^s1][^s3][^s5]

## Origin

The phone was the default live channel at work long before email, chat and video, and telephone guidance for service staff has long stressed that, with no face or body language to read, word choice and tone carry the whole message.[^s6] Its psychology was studied more closely once text took over. Michael Kraus at Yale tested voice-only against visual and combined channels in 2017.[^s3] Juliana Schroeder, Michael Kardas and Nicholas Epley compared spoken and written opinions the same year,[^s2] and Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley tested why people choose to text rather than call.[^s1]

## Why it matters

People avoid calls for reasons that turn out to be wrong. In Kumar and Epley's experiments, participants correctly expected that phoning an old friend would feel more connecting than emailing, but wrongly expected it to be more awkward; actual awkwardness did not differ, and fear of awkwardness, not expected benefit, drove their choice of medium.[^s1] With strangers, voice and video chats produced more connection than live text chat, and video added little beyond the voice.[^s1] Voice also changes how a person is judged. When listeners disagreed with someone, hearing the argument spoken, in audio or video, made the speaker seem more thoughtful and warmer than reading the same words; the audio alone was enough, and vocal cues such as intonation and pauses appeared to carry the effect.[^s2] For reading emotion, Kraus found across five experiments with 1,772 participants that people who only heard a conversation judged feelings slightly more accurately than those who could also see it.[^s3]

The cost shows up in detail. In a comparison of 106 recorded family-doctor consultations, telephone calls were about half as long as in-person visits, covered fewer problems, and contained less information gathering and rapport building, even after adjusting for length; patients were equally satisfied.[^s5] Calls feel complete while leaving things unsaid.

## What works here

Use the phone to reconnect with someone, to raise something sensitive with a colleague in another office, to settle a disagreement that is going in circles over email, or to get a quick decision.[^s1][^s2] Then make the call carry what a meeting or a document normally would:

1. **Call instead of typing when connection or tone matters.** Expect the first few seconds to feel awkward and the rest to be fine; a call often takes no longer than writing and answering an email.[^s1]
2. **Open with your name, why you are calling and what you need.** Callers cannot see your purpose on your face, so state it.[^s6]
3. **Speak plainly at a moderate pace and choose your emphasis.** Stressing different words changes the meaning, and jargon is harder to decode without a page to reread.[^s6]
4. **Listen without interrupting, and give the call your full attention.** The voice carries most of the emotional signal, so treat pitch, pace and hesitation as information.[^s6][^s3]
5. **Ask the other person to repeat back numbers, dates and decisions.** This replaces the shared screen or document you do not have.[^s6]
6. **Ask a deliberate final question to catch what was not raised.** Calls tend to cover fewer issues, so check whether anything else needs attention.[^s5]
7. **Follow up in writing when details matter.** Send a short note confirming what was agreed.[^s5]

## Pitfalls

- **Texting to avoid imagined awkwardness**, and losing the connection a call would have built.[^s1]
- **Arguing a disagreement in writing**, where the other side is more likely to judge you as cold or less thoughtful than if they heard you.[^s2]
- **Treating a short call as a full review** of a complex or high-risk issue; calls gather less information, so use them for follow-up and simple matters, and meet or write for the rest.[^s5]
- **Reading silence as agreement.** With no face to watch, a pause can mean confusion or doubt, so ask.[^s6]
- **Long holds and unclear openings**, which frustrate callers before the conversation starts.[^s6]

## Evidence

The evidence is consistent in direction but modest in size, so the status is `mixed`. Kumar and Epley's results rest on reconnection and stranger tasks, not workplace calls.[^s1] Schroeder and colleagues' study was preregistered, but the effect was clearest when listeners disagreed with the speaker and less consistent when they agreed.[^s2] Kraus reported small-to-medium effects from U.S. samples of mostly strangers.[^s3] A published comment by Rossiter argued the voice-only gain is too small to matter in practice; Kraus agreed the effects were small but held that practical importance is still an open empirical question.[^s4] McKinstry and colleagues' comparison comes from primary care, and the authors called for more research before generalising.[^s5] Service guidance on phone manner is practitioner advice rather than tested intervention.[^s6]

## Connections

- **Demonstrated by** Kumar and Epley (2020) on the underestimated value of calling, Schroeder, Kardas and Epley (2017) on the humanising voice, and Kraus (2017) on voice-only emotion reading.[^s1][^s2][^s3]
- **Alternative to email and instant messaging**, which people pick over calls because they overestimate awkwardness; calls build more connection with similar effort.[^s1]
- **Related to** video calls and face-to-face conversation, the richer live channels it competes with; paralanguage and silence, which carry the signal on a call; checking understanding and closed-loop communication, the read-back habits that make up for the missing visual channel; plain language and interrupting, from service guidance on phone manner; and channel selection and media richness theory, which frame when to pick up the phone.[^s3][^s5][^s6]
