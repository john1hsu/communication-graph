steps:
  - Before sending anything that could be read two ways (a short reply, a joke, a critique, a "can we talk?"), reread it as a stressed recipient who does not know your mood.
  - Put the tone into words first - say "no rush", "happy to help", "this is a small fix, not a problem" - rather than relying on punctuation or an emoji to carry it.
  - Decide whether an emoji fits by context - informal internal chat with people who already know you, yes; first contact, external clients, formal requests and anything about performance, money or bad news, leave it out.
  - If you use one, pick a single common positive face or symbol whose meaning is hard to misread, put it at the end of the sentence, and never let it replace words.
  - Match the other person's level once a relationship exists - if they never use emoji, keep to plain text; if the team uses reactions, a thumbs-up can acknowledge a message without another reply.
  - When a thread turns tense or ambiguous, stop adjusting the emoji and switch to a call.

  situation: A team lead replies in chat to a new analyst who has asked whether a draft report is ready to send to a client.

# Emoji and tone in text

Signalling tone in text means making the intended feeling of a work message - friendly, relaxed, serious, joking - explicit, because writing loses the voice and face that usually carry it. Senders consistently overestimate how well their tone survives email, and recipients read ambiguous lines through their own mood.[^s4] Emoji are the most common shortcut for putting tone back in, and the evidence says they can help in casual exchanges but tend to lower perceived competence in formal or first-contact work messages.[^s3][^s1] The practical skill is to carry tone in words first and treat emoji as an optional, context-dependent extra.[^s1][^s6]

## Origin

The underlying problem was documented in 2005 by Justin Kruger, Nicholas Epley, Jason Parker and Zhi-Wen Ng, whose experiments showed that people sending sarcastic, serious, angry or funny emails were more confident their intent would be understood than it actually was, even between friends; they traced this to egocentrism, the difficulty of stepping outside one's own perspective.[^s4] Research on emoji themselves followed as smartphones spread them. A 2016 University of Minnesota study showed people interpret the same emoji very differently, and a 2018 set of experiments by Ella Glikson, Arik Cheshin and Gerben van Kleef tested smileys in work-related first impressions.[^s2][^s1]

## Why it matters

Misread tone is costly at work: a terse reply sounds angry, a joke reads as criticism, and the sender never finds out.[^s4] Emoji can genuinely help. In two experiments, readers were more likely, and quicker, to grasp the indirect meaning of a reply to an opinion or disclosure question when it carried an emoji, although the effect did not appear for indirect replies to requests.[^s3] But emoji are not real smiles. In Glikson and colleagues' studies, a smiling photo raised both warmth and competence, whereas text smileys in a work email to an unknown colleague lowered perceived competence, and recipients then shared less information in their replies.[^s1] A 2026 University of Ottawa survey of workplace instant messages found the same pattern: emoji-free messages made senders seem most competent, negative emoji lowered competence, and positive emoji did not soften bad news or criticism.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Reread risky messages from the recipient's side, then state the tone in plain words ("no rush", "small fix", "happy to help") before reaching for symbols.[^s4] Reserve emoji for informal, internal exchanges with people who know you: in Glikson's third experiment, smileys raised warmth in an informal context with no loss of competence, but lowered competence and appropriateness in a formal one.[^s1] University etiquette guidance makes the same split, advising emoji mainly for informal internal messages, not formal or external email, and suggesting a reaction such as a thumbs-up to acknowledge a message instead of another reply.[^s6] Choose one common, positive emoji, because readers disagreed about whether a single emoji's sentiment was positive, neutral or negative about a quarter of the time, and disagreement grew when they saw different platforms' renderings.[^s2] When a chat becomes long, tense or unclear, move it to a call.[^s6]

## Common mistakes

Using an emoji to soften criticism or bad news is the most common error: the Ottawa study found positive emoji did not cushion negative messages and could read as insincere.[^s5] Other mistakes are adding smileys to a first email to a new colleague or client, where they cost competence;[^s1] choosing faces with contested meanings, such as the grimace, that vary by platform;[^s2] and assuming a playful line will be read playfully because it felt playful when written.[^s4]

## Evidence

The core finding that tone is lost in text is well established in the egocentrism literature.[^s4] Evidence on emoji is real but context-dependent, so it is rated mixed. Glikson's competence penalty replicated across three experiments with participants from many countries, but the studies used hypothetical first-impression scenarios and convenience samples, and excluded East Asian participants because of different emoticon conventions.[^s1] The Ottawa results are a small survey reported so far through a news release.[^s5] The disambiguation benefit comes from student samples and everyday rather than workplace exchanges.[^s3] Effects inside established relationships, where mirroring a colleague's emoji style may build rapport, are less studied.[^s1]

## Connections

The technique counters the **illusion of transparency**, since emoji and explicit tone words can help readers recover indirect meaning a sender assumed was obvious.[^s3] The gap it fills is explained by **egocentric bias**, the root of senders' overconfidence about tone.[^s4] It is applied in **email**, where Glikson's landmark study (**glikson-cheshin-van-kleef-2018**) showed the competence cost of smileys in formal first contacts,[^s1] and in **instant messaging** such as Teams chat, where reactions can stand in for short replies.[^s6] Emoji work as a text substitute for **paralanguage** and **nonverbal communication**, but readers decode them far less consistently than a voice or a face.[^s2][^s1] For bad news, see **delivering bad news**: say it plainly rather than cushioning it with a smiley.[^s5]
