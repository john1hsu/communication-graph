# Digital communication

Digital communication is any exchange carried by networked technology instead of shared physical space: an email to a client, a chat thread with a teammate, a video stand-up, a comment on a shared document.[^s1][^s4] Researchers usually call it computer-mediated communication (CMC), and the field asks one practical question: when a medium removes or reshapes the cues people normally rely on (faces, tone of voice, the setting, visible status), what happens to the message and to the working relationship?[^s1][^s2] For most knowledge workers this is no longer a special case; it is the default way work gets coordinated.[^s4]

## Origin

Early organisational research on email, led by Lee Sproull and Sara Kiesler at Carnegie Mellon, framed digital communication as a loss of social context cues.[^s1] Their 1986 field study at a Fortune 500 company, built on questionnaires and real messages from employees at every level, found that email did not just speed up existing exchanges: much of what people sent would not have been communicated through any other medium.[^s1] With fewer cues about status and setting, communication became less regulated, showing more self-absorption, more status equalisation and more uninhibited behaviour.[^s1]

Joseph Walther and Judee Burgoon challenged that "cues-filtered-out" picture in 1992.[^s2] They argued that early findings came from short laboratory sessions and coding schemes built for face-to-face talk, so slow typing, not the medium itself, explained much of the apparent coldness.[^s2] In their study, time had a stronger effect than medium: computer-mediated groups grew more positive over repeated exchanges, and people carried relational meaning through language itself, such as forms of address, self-disclosure, informal sign-offs and capitalisation.[^s2] This became the social information processing view: digital relationships develop more slowly, not differently in kind.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Digital channels are now where most workplace coordination happens, and that shift changes the shape of an organisation's network.[^s4] When Microsoft moved its workforce home in March 2020, analysis of employees' collaboration data found that the share of collaboration time spent across group boundaries fell by about a quarter, teams became more inward-looking, and people added fewer new collaborators.[^s4] Scheduled meeting and call hours dipped slightly while email and instant messaging grew, and the authors warned that leaner, asynchronous media may make complex information harder to convey and process.[^s4]

Misunderstanding is the everyday cost. Kruger, Epley, Parker and Ng found that emails meant to sound sarcastic, serious, angry, sad or funny were often misread, even between people who knew each other, and that senders were usually overconfident and unaware of the misreading.[^s3] The authors traced this to egocentrism: the writer hears their own tone in their head and assumes the reader does too.[^s3]

## How to apply the research

The research converges on a few workplace habits:

- **Assume less tone gets through than you intend.** Because senders overrate how clearly their sarcasm or urgency lands, state intent in words ("this is a quick question, not a concern") rather than relying on phrasing to imply it.[^s3]
- **Use language to do the relational work faces would do.** A greeting, a name, a thank-you or a brief personal line are how warmth travels in text, so do not strip them out in the name of efficiency.[^s2]
- **Give new digital relationships time and repeated contact.** Expect trust with a remote colleague to build over more exchanges than it would in person, and avoid judging a working relationship on the first few messages.[^s2]
- **Watch for the uninhibited message.** Reduced cues make it easier to send something blunter than you would say to someone's face; reread anything written while annoyed before sending.[^s1]
- **Deliberately bridge silos.** In distributed teams, cross-team ties decay without effort, so schedule some cross-group contact and move complex or contentious topics to a richer, live channel.[^s4]

## Evidence

The field's findings are real but conditional. The early reduced-cues effects held in Sproull and Kiesler's field data, yet Walther and Burgoon showed that some differences shrink with time and repeated interaction, and noted that their own relational measures were participant judgments rather than behavioural counts.[^s1][^s2] The Microsoft study tracked one large technology firm during a sudden, pandemic-driven shift, so its effect sizes may not transfer to firms that choose remote or hybrid work deliberately.[^s4] The email-misreading results come from controlled experiments with short messages; they show a reliable gap between confidence and accuracy rather than how often real projects fail because of tone.[^s3]
