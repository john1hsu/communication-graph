# Instant messaging

Instant messaging (IM) is the short-message channel of workplace chat tools such as Slack, Teams or Google Chat. Messages arrive in near real time and a presence indicator shows who is online, but the recipient can answer now, later or not at all. That mix makes IM a different channel from both email and a call: people use it to ask quick questions, coordinate, and check whether a colleague is free before interrupting them more heavily.[^s1][^s3] Using it well means picking it for those jobs and knowing when to leave it.

## Origin

Researchers began studying IM at work as it spread from consumer use into offices around 2000. An ethnographic study by Bonnie Nardi, Steve Whittaker and Erin Bradner found that much of its value lay in what they called outeraction: the work of reaching people, negotiating whether they are available and managing the conversation, rather than exchanging information itself.[^s4] Two years later, Ellen Isaacs and colleagues at AT&T Labs logged about 21,000 workplace IM conversations among 437 users over 16 months, the first large picture of how people actually used it.[^s1] A 2006 U.S. national survey analysed by R. Kelly Garrett and James Danziger then asked whether IM made work more or less interrupted.[^s2]

## Why it matters

IM is not just small talk. In the Isaacs logs, work was the topic of about 62% of conversations, quick questions about 28% and scheduling or coordination about 31%; purely social greetings were about 5%. Conversations averaged a little over four minutes and 17 turns, and frequent pairs used IM for fast, multi-topic working discussions, not only one-line queries.[^s1] Because messages can be read and answered later, IM can also lower interruption. In Garrett and Danziger's sample of 912 office workers, IM users reported slightly fewer interruptions than non-users after controls, and spent no more time communicating overall, suggesting IM replaced some calls, emails and desk visits rather than adding to them.[^s2] Garrett explained that workers used IM to check whether colleagues were busy, to get quick answers, and to defer conversations, because ignoring a message was socially acceptable.[^s3]

## What works here

1. **Use IM for quick questions, status checks and coordination.** These are the jobs it handles best and the ones people already use it for most.[^s1][^s3]
2. **Ask before you launch a long exchange.** A short "got ten minutes for a question about the budget?" uses IM's lightness to negotiate availability before a heavier interruption.[^s3][^s4]
3. **Treat presence as a hint, not a summons.** Showing as online does not mean someone is free; the channel works because people can defer replies, so let them.[^s3][^s4]
4. **Switch channels when the topic outgrows the chat.** In the Isaacs logs, about 16% of conversations moved to phone, face-to-face or email, mostly to set up a meeting; Garrett notes IM is not where extended problem-solving happens.[^s1][^s3]
5. **Write decisions down where they will be found.** U.S. federal guidance counts chats on collaboration platforms as records when they conduct business, and expects decisions made in such conversations to be documented as they would be for a meeting.[^s5]

## Pitfalls

- **Using chat for complex or contentious discussions** that need a call or meeting, so the thread drags on.[^s1][^s3]
- **Expecting instant replies.** IM's interruption benefits depend on people being free to answer later; pressure to respond at once removes them.[^s2][^s3]
- **Running several chats while doing focused work.** In the Isaacs logs, attention left the chat window in about 86% of conversations, and scholars have proposed that this kind of polychronic communication and pop-up notification can raise workload and cut productivity, though that model is theoretical.[^s1][^s6]
- **Treating chat as off the record.** Business chats can be records with retention duties, so decisions should not live only in a direct message.[^s5]

## Evidence

The evidence is `mixed`. The large log study is detailed but comes from one company's prototype tool in 2000-2001.[^s1] Garrett and Danziger's result is cross-sectional, self-reported, measured interruption with a single item, explains little variance and predates modern team chat; the authors present it as a correlation.[^s2] The ethnographic findings are qualitative.[^s4] Claims that chat harms productivity rest largely on theory rather than field data.[^s6] Today's tools add channels, threads and group alerts that these studies did not cover, so treat the findings as a guide to IM's character rather than precise effect sizes.

## Connections

- **Demonstrated by** Isaacs et al. (2002), which showed how and for what workplace IM is used, and by Garrett and Danziger (2008), which linked IM use to fewer reported interruptions.[^s1][^s2]
- **Alternative to email**, phone calls and desk visits, which IM partly replaced in Garrett and Danziger's survey.[^s3]
- **Studied in** computer-supported cooperative work, where the founding IM studies were published, and in digital communication research.[^s4][^s2]
- **Related to** chat etiquette, the shared habits that make this channel work for a team; emoji and tone in text; setting reply-time expectations; channel selection, media synchronicity theory and asynchronous communication, which frame when to chat rather than call or write; information overload; the phone call it often replaces; and the netiquette guidelines and early pioneers behind online messaging.[^s1][^s3]
