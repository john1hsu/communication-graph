# Cyber incivility

Cyber incivility is rude or disrespectful behaviour in computer-mediated work communication that breaks shared norms of mutual respect; as with face-to-face incivility, the sender may or may not intend harm.[^s1] Researchers now separate two forms. **Active** incivility is something the sender does: demeaning or sarcastic remarks, shouting in capitals, condescending replies. **Passive** incivility is respect the sender withholds: ignoring a request, never replying, or replying without addressing the questions asked.[^s4] Other recurring examples include criticising someone on reply-all, copying people who do not need to be copied, and firing off urgent requests with no notice.[^s5]

## Origin

The idea extends research on low-intensity workplace incivility into digital channels. Vivien Lim and Thompson Teo at the National University of Singapore developed an early workplace measure of it in 2009, surveying 192 bank and financial-services employees about emails from their supervisors; they also distinguished hostile messages from neglectful ones such as not acknowledging mail or handling sensitive matters by email.[^s1] Later work moved to experiments and daily diaries: Giumetti and colleagues simulated uncivil and supportive email supervisors in the lab in 2013,[^s2] Park, Fritz and Jex tracked rude emails day by day in 2018,[^s3] and Yuan, Park and Sliter formalised the active/passive split in 2020.[^s4]

## Why it matters

In Lim and Teo's survey, employees who received more uncivil supervisor email reported lower job satisfaction and commitment and more deviance and intention to quit.[^s1] In Giumetti's within-person experiment with 84 participants, the same people answered fewer math problems correctly, felt less energetic and engaged, and reported worse mood after working under an uncivil email supervisor than under a supportive one.[^s2] Over four workdays, Park and colleagues found that days with email incivility ended with more emotional and physical distress, which carried into the next morning.[^s3] Passive incivility is easy to dismiss but not harmless: in Yuan's ten-day diary study of 119 employees, being ignored predicted insomnia and next-morning negative mood, while active rudeness did not.[^s4] Recipients also tend to replay rude messages in their heads: a 2026 study of two New Zealand samples linked email incivility to job anxiety and depression, partly through work rumination.[^s5]

## How to spot it

- **In your own drafts:** all caps, double punctuation, sarcasm, "as I already said", or criticism sent to a list instead of one person.[^s4][^s5]
- **In your outbox and inbox:** requests you have left unanswered for days, or replies that skip half the questions asked.[^s4]
- **In the channel choice:** sensitive feedback, bad news or conflict handled by email or chat when it needed a conversation.[^s1][^s6]
- **In the reaction:** a recipient who goes quiet, escalates or answers curtly; a short, efficient message can read as rude even when it is not meant to.[^s6]

## How to fix it

- **Pause before sending charged messages.** Porath notes that email makes it easy to send hostile notes without first regulating emotion; draft, wait, then reread as the recipient.[^s6]
- **Criticise privately and specifically.** Name the problem, the fix and the deadline to the person concerned; take patterns of behaviour to a call or meeting.[^s5][^s6]
- **Close the loop.** Acknowledge requests even when you cannot act yet, give a date, and answer every question asked; omission is the passive form.[^s4]
- **Agree team norms.** Set expectations for tone, response windows, reply-all and copying, and use synchronous channels for urgent or sensitive matters; leaders should model them.[^s4][^s5]
- **As a recipient, protect recovery.** Higher job control softened same-day distress, and detaching from work in the evening weakened its carry-over to the next morning.[^s3]

## Evidence

Associations between email incivility and lower well-being and attitudes recur across countries and designs,[^s1][^s3][^s4][^s5] and one lab experiment shows a causal effect on short-term performance and energy.[^s2] The evidence is still mixed in strength: most studies are cross-sectional or diary surveys built on self-report, with small or convenience samples, and the lab study used students on 15-minute tasks.[^s1][^s2][^s4] Which form does more harm also varies by outcome: active incivility tracked commitment and deviance more strongly in 2009,[^s1] while passive incivility drove sleep problems in 2020.[^s4] Results also differ by context; hybrid employees showed stronger effects than office-based colleagues in the 2026 samples.[^s5] The claim that people are ruder online because of disinhibition is an expert explanation rather than a tested finding here.[^s6]

## Connections

Cyber incivility is an **instance of workplace incivility** (`cm:workplace-incivility`) carried into digital channels.[^s1] Its effect on people is **demonstrated by** Giumetti et al. 2013 (`cm:giumetti-et-al-2013`)[^s2] and its active/passive structure **supported by** Yuan, Park and Sliter 2020 (`cm:yuan-park-sliter-2020`).[^s4] It **undermines work engagement** (`cm:work-engagement`)[^s2] and **causes work rumination** (`cm:work-rumination`), the mechanism behind its links to anxiety and depression.[^s5] Experts **explain** it partly through the **online disinhibition effect** (`cm:online-disinhibition-effect`).[^s6] Practical neighbours: **setting reply-time expectations** and **chat etiquette** prevent passive incivility; a **cooling-off period**, **channel selection** and care with **emoji and tone in text** reduce active incivility; **psychological detachment** helps recipients recover; norms must avoid feeding **workplace telepressure**.[^s3][^s4][^s5][^s6]
