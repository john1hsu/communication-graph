# Media richness theory

Media richness theory (MRT) holds that channels of communication vary in how well they can shift a receiver's understanding within a short span of time, and that this capacity - richness - should be matched to the message. Richard Daft and Robert Lengel judged a medium's richness on four criteria: how fast it returns feedback so interpretations can be checked, how many cues it carries (voice, tone, gesture), how personal it is, and how much natural language variety it allows. On those criteria an in-person conversation sits at the top, a phone call next, addressed letters and memos below that, general written documents lower still, and tables of numbers at the bottom.[^s1] The theory's practical promise is simple: ambiguous messages need rich media, clear-cut ones can go lean.

## Origin

Daft and Lengel built the theory in the early 1980s while studying organisations as information processors, and set it out most fully in a 1986 *Management Science* paper. Its key move was to separate two kinds of information problem. Uncertainty is a lack of data, and it is cured by getting more of it - reports, databases, formal systems. Equivocality is a surplus of meanings: people hold conflicting interpretations of the same situation, and more data does not help, because what is needed is discussion until they agree what the problem actually is. Rich, interactive media were proposed as the tool for equivocality; lean, high-volume media as the tool for uncertainty.[^s1] Later reviews describe the theory as resting on three assumptions - that people choose media to get a task done, evaluate media rationally, and are able to use whatever medium they choose.[^s3]

## Why it matters

MRT gave managers and later remote teams a vocabulary for a question they face every day: should this be an email, a chat message, a call or a meeting? Its distinction between missing information and conflicting interpretations is still a useful diagnostic. A status update, a schedule or a data request is mainly an uncertainty problem and travels well in writing; a reorganisation, a disputed priority or a sensitive performance issue is an equivocality problem and usually needs voice and fast back-and-forth.[^s1] Field research on media selection found that middle managers tended to pick channels with the receiver in mind, using richness and social presence, whereas senior directors more often picked whatever was quickest and easiest for themselves - a gap the authors argued shapes how leaders' messages are read further down the organisation.[^s6]

A workplace example: a team lead needs to tell a colleague that their part of a launch will be cut. Sent as a two-line chat message, the news leaves the reason, the decision's permanence and the lead's attitude open to interpretation. MRT's advice is to have the conversation live, where the colleague can ask questions and the lead can read and respond to the reaction, then confirm the outcome in writing.[^s1]

## Evidence

The descriptive part of MRT - that people perceive media as differing in richness, and that cues and feedback drive those perceptions - has held up well.[^s2] The prescriptive part - that matching richness to equivocality improves performance - has not. In a 1998 experiment, Alan Dennis and Susan Kinney had two-person teams decide on tasks of differing equivocality using computer-mediated or video communication. Participants did notice the richness differences, and lean media slowed decisions, but matching media to task equivocality did not improve decision quality, consensus or satisfaction.[^s2] A 2020 encyclopedia review by Vivian Sheer concluded that support for the matching proposition appears mainly in studies that satisfy the theory's assumptions, that few real-life media choices do, and that the richness construct has proved more useful than the theory itself.[^s3]

Two lines of work refine rather than discard it. Channel expansion theory (John Carlson and Robert Zmud, 1999) showed in cross-sectional and multi-wave studies of email that perceived richness is not fixed by the medium: it grows with a person's experience of the channel, the topic, the organisation and the partner.[^s5] Lionel Robert and Alan Dennis's "paradox of richness" model argues that rich, high-presence media raise people's motivation to attend but lower their ability to process complex content, while lean media do the reverse - so a dense technical proposal may be better read in writing even when the decision around it needs a meeting.[^s4] Because the theory's central prescription is still widely taught as a rule while its direct tests are mixed to negative, its status here is contested.[^s2][^s3]

Practical reading of the evidence: use the equivocality question as a prompt, not a law. Expect lean channels to work better than MRT predicts between people who already share context, and expect live channels to struggle with detail-heavy content that people need time to study.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

MRT was introduced by Richard Daft and Robert Lengel and grew out of organisational communication research on how firms process information.[^s1] It is the main theoretical basis for channel selection, and the 1987 study *Message Equivocality, Media Selection, and Manager Performance* applied it to managers' everyday choices. Dennis and Kinney's 1998 experiment is its best-known challenge,[^s2] and the paradox-of-richness model challenges its assumption that richer is always better for hard messages.[^s4] Channel expansion theory complements it by explaining why the same channel, such as email, can feel rich to experienced users and lean to newcomers.[^s5] Media synchronicity theory and research on asynchronous communication and Zoom fatigue carry the same question into modern tools.
