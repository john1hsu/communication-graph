# Setting reply-time expectations

Setting reply-time expectations means telling the people you write to how soon you actually need an answer: a specific reply-by time when there is a real deadline, and an explicit "this can wait" when there is not. It can live in the message itself, in the subject line, in a standing email-signature line, or in a team agreement about default reply windows for each channel.[^s1][^s5][^s6] The technique exists because receivers fill the silence with guesses, and they tend to guess that the sender wants a reply sooner than the sender does.[^s1]

## Origin

The practice grew out of workplace email charters and out-of-hours policies rather than from a single author. In 2018, for example, the University of Glasgow's Institute of Health and Wellbeing told staff they need not read or answer email outside working hours and asked everyone to add a signature line saying replies were not expected out of hours.[^s6] The University of Bristol's email charter similarly states that instant replies are not expected, asks senders to label subject lines as information or action and to indicate priority, and steers urgent matters to chat or phone.[^s5] The research case came in 2021, when Laura Giurge and Vanessa Bohns named the "email urgency bias" and tested a one-sentence sender note as a fix.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

Across eight preregistered experiments with 4,004 participants, Giurge and Bohns found that people receiving non-urgent work emails outside normal hours overestimated how quickly the sender expected a reply, and that this overestimate fed perceived stress and, through it, lower well-being.[^s1] Senders themselves held low expectations, so the pressure was largely imagined.[^s2] The authors draw on egocentrism: each side reads the message from its own vantage point, and the sender's relaxed intent does not travel with the text unless it is written down.[^s1]

Unstated expectations are not harmless. In three studies, including one of 138 employee-partner pairs, William Becker and colleagues found that an organisation's expectation that staff monitor email after hours was linked to anxiety, poorer health and more relationship conflict, with effects spilling over to partners.[^s3] Urgency signals also steer attention: in a three-week field experiment in which people received experimenter-sent emails in their real inboxes, Anna Cox and colleagues found that emails marked urgent were answered first, even when they were worth less than non-urgent ones.[^s4] Accurate urgency cues therefore matter in both directions - overstating urgency pulls people off more important work, and leaving it out lets them assume the worst.[^s4][^s1]

## How to apply it

Decide your real deadline before you write, then make it visible early. A reply-by time with a reason ("by Wednesday noon, for Thursday's client meeting") lets the reader plan; a plain "no rush" sentence lets them leave it until working hours.[^s2] The note matters most when you send outside working hours or across time zones, because that is where Giurge and Bohns found the bias.[^s1] For routine traffic, agree default reply windows per channel with your team so that individual messages only need to flag exceptions, and route true emergencies to phone or chat instead of email.[^s5] If you write at odd hours, schedule-send or use a standing signature line.[^s5][^s6]

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving the deadline at the end** of a long message, or implying it with "when you get a chance", so the reader still has to guess.[^s5]
- **Crying wolf.** Marking routine messages urgent trains people to drop more important work for low-value requests.[^s4]
- **"Quick question" subject lines**, which the Bristol charter notes can pressure people into replying fast.[^s5]
- **A signature line contradicted by behaviour.** If a manager praises instant weekend replies, a "no need to reply" footer will not undo the expectation; the organisational norm is the stressor.[^s3]
- **Relying on the note for in-hours work.** Giurge and Bohns found the sender note made no measurable difference to the gap during normal working hours.[^s2]

## Evidence

The best evidence comes from Giurge and Bohns's Study 6, with 852 full-time US employees: when senders added a one-line note that the matter was not urgent, the gap between receivers' and senders' expected reply speed for out-of-hours email disappeared.[^s2] The note did not directly reduce rated stress; a weaker indirect benefit appeared only in exploratory analysis.[^s2] Most of the studies used imagined or recalled emails rather than real inboxes, so field effects are untested, and the evidence on organisational expectations is correlational.[^s2][^s3] The core finding is preregistered and consistent across studies, but the remedy rests on one scenario experiment, so the status is `mixed`.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

- **Addresses the email urgency bias**, receivers' overestimate of how quickly senders want a reply to non-urgent messages.[^s1]
- **Applied in asynchronous communication** generally and **in email** in particular, where reply timing is invisible unless stated.[^s1][^s5]
- **Demonstrated by** Giurge and Bohns's email urgency bias studies, whose Study 6 tested the sender note directly.[^s2]
- **Explained by egocentric bias**: senders assume their relaxed intent is obvious, and receivers assume their own sense of pressure is shared.[^s1]
- **Broader** than one message: it belongs to the team communication norms a group agrees on, as in university email charters.[^s5][^s6]
- **Related to** bottom line up front, which puts the request and deadline first; the illusion of transparency; information overload, which urgency flags can worsen; and channel selection, which sends emergencies to faster channels.[^s4][^s5]
