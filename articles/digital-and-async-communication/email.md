# Email

Email is the default written channel of most workplaces: asynchronous, text-only, easy to forward and search, and therefore the place where requests, updates, approvals and records accumulate. Its strengths - the reader can process the message when they choose, and both sides keep a copy - are also its costs, because every message competes for attention in an inbox the reader did not design.[^s2][^s5] Writing good email is less about etiquette than about making a message the reader can understand, prioritise and act on without a follow-up conversation.[^s5]

## Origin

As email became the backbone of office work, researchers began treating it as a distinct source of strain. In a 2011 *Organization Science* study combining quantitative and qualitative data, Stephen Barley, Debra Meyerson and Stine Grodal found that the more time people spent handling email, the more overloaded they felt, and that email had become a cultural symbol of overload that drew attention away from other sources of pressure in their work.[^s2] Workplace writing guides now treat email as its own genre with its own rules for subject lines, structure and length; the Australian Army's guidance for its staff is a typical example.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Email is cheap to send and expensive to receive. In a 2016 CHI study that logged the computer use and heart-rate variability of 40 information workers for about two weeks, Gloria Mark and colleagues found that more daily time on email went with lower self-rated productivity, and more email time in a given hour went with higher measured stress.[^s4] Receivers also misread urgency: across eight preregistered studies with about 4,000 participants, Laura Giurge and Vanessa Bohns found that people receiving non-urgent work emails outside working hours believed senders expected much faster replies than the senders actually did, and that this "email urgency bias" was linked to stress and lower well-being.[^s3] A sender who is vague about the ask, the deadline or the urgency therefore pushes cost onto the reader.[^s3][^s5]

## What works here

Email works for conveyance - updates, decisions already made, instructions, documents for review, and requests the reader can handle alone. When sending, the Army guidance and the email-urgency research suggest:[^s5][^s3]

1. **Check the channel first.** If the matter is sensitive, contested or needs quick back-and-forth, call or meet instead and confirm in writing.[^s5]
2. **Write a specific subject line** of roughly eight words or fewer, with the main point or action in the first few words.[^s5]
3. **Keep one topic per email**; if you must cover two, name both in the subject and separate them in the body.[^s5]
4. **Lead with the point.** Put the request or conclusion in the first sentence or paragraph, stating what is needed, from whom and by when.[^s5]
5. **Format for scanning** once a message runs past about two-thirds of a screen: short paragraphs, headings and bullets.[^s5]
6. **State the urgency.** When a message is not urgent - especially one sent after hours - say so; in Giurge and Bohns's sixth study a one-line note that a reply could wait closed the gap between what receivers assumed and what senders meant.[^s3]

For managing the inbox, the evidence favours checking on purpose rather than constantly. In a two-week field experiment with 124 adults, Kostadin Kushlev and Elizabeth Dunn found that people asked to check email three times a day, with notifications off, reported lower daily stress than in a week of unlimited checking.[^s1] Mark and colleagues found that, among heavy email users, people who checked on their own initiative rated their productivity higher than those prompted by notifications, and batchers rated it higher than people with mixed habits, although neither pattern lowered stress.[^s4]

## Pitfalls

- **Burying the request** under background, so the reader has to dig for what to do.[^s5]
- **Vague subjects** such as "Update" or "Quick question", which the reader cannot prioritise.[^s5]
- **Using email to avoid a hard conversation**, or sending pushy or angry wording you would not say aloud; the Army guidance advises rewriting any sentence that sounds rude or overbearing and asking a colleague to check tone when unsure.[^s5]
- **Implied urgency.** Off-hours messages with no stated deadline are read as more urgent than intended.[^s3]
- **Careless cc, bcc and reply-all**, which adds to other people's volume.[^s5][^s2]

## Evidence

The findings are useful but softer than they look. Barley and colleagues' data are cross-sectional and from one company; they also found that processing more email went with a greater sense of coping, so email is not simply harmful.[^s2] Mark's study was correlational, in a single highly educated workplace, and could not log phone email.[^s4] Kushlev and Dunn describe their experiment as exploratory, with self-reported checking, a convenience sample and no effect on overall weekly stress.[^s1] Giurge and Bohns's studies were preregistered but relied mostly on hypothetical scenarios and recall.[^s3] Status is `mixed`: the direction is consistent, but effect sizes are modest and depend on role and organisational norms.[^s1][^s3][^s4]

## Connections

- **Causes information overload**: time spent on email tracks felt overload in Barley, Meyerson and Grodal's study.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by** Barley et al. (2011), the email urgency bias studies, Kushlev and Dunn (2015) and Mark et al. (2016), which together describe email's costs and the habits that reduce them.[^s2][^s3][^s1][^s4]
- **Complements bottom line up front**: the Army guidance asks writers to summarise the point and the action in the opening lines.[^s5]
- **Alternative to face-to-face conversation** for matters that are sensitive or need discussion.[^s5]
- **Related to** channel selection, which decides when email is the right medium; audience analysis and plain language, which shape the message; and the curse of knowledge, which leads senders to assume a terse note is clear.[^s5]
