# Workplace telepressure

Workplace telepressure is the preoccupation with work messages and the urge to answer them quickly - the itch to check email or chat, and the discomfort of leaving a message unanswered - whether or not the sender needs a fast reply.[^s1] It is a psychological state rather than a count of messages: two people with the same inbox can feel very different pressure, and the pressure itself, not just message volume, is what predicts strain.[^s1][^s4] For communicators it matters in both directions: the urge degrades your own focus and recovery, and the way you send messages can create it in other people.[^s5][^s6]

## Origin

Psychologists Larissa Barber and Alecia Santuzzi named and measured the construct in a 2015 paper in the *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology*, describing it as a by-product of relying on asynchronous tools such as email.[^s1] Across two studies they built a short self-report scale, found that telepressure was moderately associated with techno-overload, perceived response expectations and public self-consciousness, and showed it was distinct from job involvement, commitment and general work demands.[^s1] Later work by the same group tested why it harms people and what organisations can do about it.[^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

Fast replies can speed up decisions, which is why telepressure feels productive.[^s1] The cost is recovery: in Barber and Santuzzi's validation study, telepressure predicted physical and cognitive burnout, poorer sleep quality and absence, over and above general and technology-related work demands.[^s1] Expectations that staff will monitor email after hours have been linked, in experience-sampling and couple studies, to higher anxiety, poorer health and lower relationship satisfaction for both employees and their partners.[^s5] For a team, telepressure also turns asynchronous channels into pseudo-live ones, so people answer the newest message instead of the most important one.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence

The core associations have been reproduced by independent groups, but almost all the data are cross-sectional self-report surveys, so causal direction is not settled.[^s2][^s4] A 2019 study of 233 full-time workers linked telepressure to all three burnout dimensions and poorer sleep quality; it ran mainly through boundary crossing (doing work tasks during home time), and the hypothesised route through reduced psychological detachment was not supported.[^s2] A 2022 study of 173 administrative staff who moved to home working found that after-hours email cut psychological detachment more sharply for people high in telepressure than for those low in it.[^s4] Interventions are barely tested: in two samples, having a formal disconnection policy was not associated with lower telepressure, while unwritten norms of after-hours availability and a family-unsupportive climate were.[^s3] Hence `mixed`: a reliable, measurable pressure with consistent correlates, but small effects and little experimental evidence about remedies.

## How to spot it

- You check messages within minutes of a notification, including in meetings, at dinner or in bed, and feel tense until you have replied.[^s1]
- You answer non-urgent messages at night or weekends because you assume the sender expects it - receivers routinely overestimate how fast senders want a reply.[^s6]
- After-hours replying leaves you unable to switch off from work, with poorer sleep or a sense of being worn out.[^s2][^s4]
- On a team, people send late-night messages with no stated deadline, and others answer them at once, quietly setting the norm.[^s3][^s6]

## How to fix it

Treat it as both an individual habit and a team signal. As a receiver, batch your checking at set times rather than reacting to each arrival, and turn off alerts outside those times; people high in telepressure appear to benefit most from this structure.[^s4] Before replying out of hours, ask whether the message actually states a deadline.[^s6] As a sender, say how soon you need a reply - especially for evening or weekend messages - and schedule non-urgent sends for working hours; stating expectations reduces the receiver's inflated sense of urgency.[^s6][^s4] As a manager, change the unwritten norm rather than only writing a policy: model not replying after hours, agree which channel signals a genuine emergency, and make it acceptable to answer tomorrow.[^s3]

**Example.** A project lead finishes reviewing a draft at 9:40 p.m. *Before:* she messages a junior analyst "Can you look at section 3?" and the analyst, assuming it is urgent, works until midnight. *After:* she writes "No rush - please look at section 3 by Thursday's check-in; nothing needed tonight" and schedules it for 8:30 a.m. The analyst no longer has to guess at urgency, and the lead's habit stops signalling that evenings are on call.[^s6]

## Connections

Workplace telepressure is a narrower form of **workplace stress** (`cm:workplace-stress`), centred on the pull of incoming messages.[^s1] It was **demonstrated by Barber and Santuzzi's 2015 studies** (`cm:barber-santuzzi-2015`), which **link it to burnout** (`cm:burnout`).[^s1] It **undermines psychological detachment** (`cm:psychological-detachment`), the mental switch-off from work that recovery depends on.[^s4] It is partly **explained by the email urgency bias** (`cm:email-urgency-bias`) - receivers overestimating the sender's expected reply speed - and is **supported by Becker and colleagues' 2019 studies** (`cm:becker-et-al-2019`) on after-hours monitoring expectations.[^s6][^s5] Practical remedies include **setting reply-time expectations**, **scheduled inbox checks** and the field experiment on checking less often (`cm:kushlev-dunn-2015`); it sits close to **information overload** and the always-available side of **email**, **instant messaging** and **asynchronous communication**.[^s4][^s6]
