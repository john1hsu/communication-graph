# Media synchronicity theory

Media synchronicity theory (MST) explains when a channel helps or hurts a piece of work by looking at what the people communicating are trying to do at that moment. It splits communication into two processes. Conveyance is getting information to others and letting them think about it; convergence is agreeing on what that information means and confirming that everyone agrees. The theory predicts that conveyance goes better in low-synchronicity media, where people can read, reread and respond in their own time, and that convergence goes better in high-synchronicity media, where people work on the same thing at the same moment with fast back-and-forth.[^s1][^s2] Because almost every team task involves both, its central practical advice is to use more than one channel rather than pick a single "best" one.[^s1]

## Origin

Alan Dennis and Joseph Valacich proposed MST at the 1999 Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences as a replacement for media richness theory, which they argued had weak support for newer electronic media, ranked channels on a single scale even though no medium is best on every dimension, and wrongly assigned one medium to a whole task.[^s2] A 1998 lab experiment by Dennis, Valacich, Cathy Speier and Michael Morris had already given the core distinction preliminary support.[^s5] The theory's fullest statement came in a 2008 *MIS Quarterly* article by Dennis, Robert Fuller and Valacich, which defined synchronicity as the degree to which people work together on the same activity at the same time and added that a team's familiarity with its task and with each other shifts the mix of conveyance and convergence it needs.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Most workplace channel advice still asks whether a message is "rich" or "lean". MST asks a more useful question: which part of the work is this message doing? In the 1999 version, five media capabilities determine fit: speed of feedback, symbol variety (text, images, voice), parallelism (how many conversations can run at once), rehearsability (how much the sender can polish a message before sending) and reprocessability (how easily the receiver can go back over it).[^s2] The 2008 version keeps the same logic, recasting feedback speed as transmission velocity and symbol variety as symbol sets.[^s1] Email and shared documents score high on rehearsability, reprocessability and parallelism, which is why they suit sharing data and options; a call or meeting scores high on velocity, which is why it suits closing disagreements.[^s1][^s2]

The theory also explains why the same team should change channels over time. Its propositions hold that newly formed groups, groups with new members and groups without agreed norms need more high-synchronicity communication, and that mature groups with settled routines can rely more on asynchronous channels.[^s2]

A workplace example. A product manager needs a new cross-functional team to choose between three launch plans. Before: she calls a one-hour video meeting, walks through a 30-slide deck, and asks for a decision at the end; half the team sees the cost figures for the first time and the meeting ends with "let's take it offline". After: she circulates a two-page comparison two days ahead and asks for written questions (conveyance in a low-synchronicity channel), holds a 25-minute call devoted only to the open disagreements and the decision (convergence in a high-synchronicity channel), then posts the decision and owners in the team channel. The second version gives people time to process detail and saves live time for agreement, which is the split MST predicts will work.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence

Support is real but limited and conditional. In a two-site action research study, eight largely virtual process-improvement teams rated communication media while solving complex problems; their experience fitted MST, with email and discussion boards working for sharing information and meetings and calls working for reaching shared meaning. The authors noted the teams were newly formed and called for tests with established teams.[^s3] A 2019 survey of 96 multilingual student teams found that MST held for native and highly proficient English speakers, who rated online conferences as most useful for reaching consensus, but limited-proficiency speakers rated those live sessions significantly lower, leading the authors to conclude the theory needs adjusting for language diversity.[^s4] Their review of earlier work reports similar conditions: MST explained performance in cooperative but not non-cooperative settings, team norms and social structures mattered as much as the tool, and studies of linguistically diverse teams questioned its assumptions.[^s4] Much of the evidence relies on perceptions, students or small samples, so the status here is mixed.[^s3][^s4]

Practical reading: treat the conveyance/convergence split as a planning tool, and check who might be disadvantaged by a live channel - non-native speakers, people in distant time zones, or those anxious about speaking up - before assuming a meeting is the best place to converge.[^s4]

## Connections

MST was introduced by Alan Dennis and Joseph Valacich and was proposed as an alternative to media richness theory, which assigns channels to whole tasks by richness rather than to processes within them.[^s2] It is supported by DeLuca and Valacich's 2006 study of virtual teams and by the earlier 1998 experiment by Dennis, Valacich, Speier and Morris,[^s3][^s5] and challenged in part by Fleischmann, Aritz and Cardon's 2019 study, which found its predictions break down for multicultural and multilingual teams.[^s4] It underpins asynchronous communication practice, where the 2008 paper's case for low-synchronicity media in conveyance justifies written-first workflows,[^s1] and it gives channel selection its conveyance/convergence test. It sits under digital communication and links to video calls, workplace meetings and common ground (shared understanding), all of which belong to the convergence side of the work.[^s1]
