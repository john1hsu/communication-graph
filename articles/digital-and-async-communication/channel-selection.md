steps:
  - Name the job of the message in one line - is it mainly to pass on information the receiver can absorb alone (conveyance), or to agree on meaning, resolve disagreement or decide something together (convergence)?
  - For conveyance - updates, data, instructions, reference material - default to a written, asynchronous channel the receiver can reread and process in their own time.
  - For convergence - an ambiguous problem, a disagreement, a sensitive or emotional message, or a request you need someone to say yes to - default to a live channel with voice and fast feedback, such as a call, video or a face-to-face conversation.
  - If tone, sarcasm or humour carries part of the meaning, either move to voice or rewrite the text so the meaning survives without your tone of voice; read it aloud in a deliberately flat or opposite tone before sending.
  - Combine channels when the task needs both - send the facts in writing first, discuss live, then confirm the decision and owners in writing.
  - Check the receiver's side - their familiarity with you and the topic, time zone, norms and access - and adjust the default; a lean channel works better between people who already share context.
example:
  situation: A project manager needs a client's sign-off to cut a feature so the release can stay on date, and the client sponsor is known to be attached to that feature.
  before: "Email, sent at 6 pm - Subject: Scope change. Hi Priya, given the integration issues we will need to drop the reporting module from the March release. Let me know if that works. Thanks."
  after: "Short email first - Subject: Decision needed Thursday - reporting module vs. March date. Priya, I'd like 20 minutes Wednesday to talk through a trade-off: keep the March date by moving the reporting module to April, or keep the module and slip to mid-April. One-page options summary attached. Then a 20-minute call to discuss, followed the same day by a written recap of the decision, owner and new dates."
  why: The facts travel in writing where they can be reread, the contested decision happens live where questions and concerns surface immediately, and the written recap locks in the shared understanding the call produced.
selfCheck:
  - Am I mainly passing information on, or do we need to reach agreement - and does my channel fit that job?
  - If my reader misread the tone of this text, what is the worst plausible interpretation, and would a call prevent it?
  - Am I choosing email or chat because it suits the message, or because it lets me avoid an awkward conversation?
  - After a live discussion, have I put the decision, owners and dates in writing?

# Channel selection

Channel selection is the habit of deciding on purpose which medium will carry a message - a meeting, a video or phone call, a chat message, an email or a formal document - instead of defaulting to whatever is open on your screen. The classic rationale comes from organisational theorists Richard Daft and Robert Lengel, who argued that media differ in how quickly they can change a receiver's understanding, and that a mismatch between the medium and the message's ambiguity costs clarity.[^s1] Later work reframed the choice around whether a message must simply be transmitted or must produce shared understanding.[^s3]

## Origin

Daft and Lengel's 1986 *Management Science* paper ranked media by "richness": face-to-face first, then telephone, personal written documents such as letters and memos, impersonal written documents, and finally numeric reports.[^s1] They attributed the ranking to four features - immediate feedback, the number of cues and channels, personal focus and natural language - and argued that rich media suit equivocal problems where people hold different interpretations, while lean, formal media suit routine data that only reduces uncertainty.[^s1] In 2008 Alan Dennis, Robert Fuller and Joseph Valacich proposed media synchronicity theory, which splits communication into conveyance (getting information across) and convergence (agreeing on what it means) and predicts that low-synchronicity media such as email serve conveyance better, while high-synchronicity media serve convergence.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Text strips out tone, and senders rarely notice. In experiments by Justin Kruger, Nicholas Epley and colleagues reported in 2005, senders expected about 78% of their sarcastic or serious statements to be read correctly; listeners to voice recordings got about 75% right, but email readers managed only 56%, barely above chance, while still feeling confident.[^s4] The channel also changes whether people say yes. In Mahdi Roghanizad and Vanessa Bohns's first study, students who asked strangers in person to complete a short survey succeeded about 70% of the time, versus about 10% by email, although both groups had predicted roughly the same success.[^s5] For a workplace request, an apology or a sensitive piece of news, the medium can matter as much as the wording.[^s4][^s5]

## How to apply it

1. **Name the job.** Decide whether the message is mainly conveyance or convergence; most real tasks contain both.[^s3]
2. **Use lean, asynchronous channels for conveyance.** Written updates, data and instructions let the receiver reread and think, which media synchronicity theory links to rehearsability and reprocessability.[^s3]
3. **Go live for ambiguity and disagreement.** Daft and Lengel's account puts equivocal, contested issues in rich channels with fast feedback.[^s1]
4. **Protect tone.** If humour, sarcasm or emotion carries meaning, use voice, or rewrite so the plain words carry it; Kruger and Epley found that senders who first read their message aloud in the opposite tone stopped overestimating how clearly it would come across.[^s4]
5. **Mix channels.** Because most group tasks need both processes, Dennis and colleagues expect better results from combining media than from any single one - for instance, read-ahead in writing, discussion live, recap in writing.[^s3]
6. **Adjust for the relationship.** Channel expansion research finds that experience with a channel, partner and topic can make a lean medium work like a richer one, though the effect depends on setting and culture.[^s6]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating richer as always better.** A call to deliver a routine status number wastes time; Daft and Lengel themselves saw rich media as inefficient for large volumes of routine data.[^s1]
- **Hiding in email.** Choosing text because a conversation feels awkward trades your comfort for a higher risk of misreading, and Bohns's work suggests requesters underestimate how much a live conversation helps.[^s5]
- **No written record.** Live conversations reach agreement but leave nothing to reprocess; follow up in writing.[^s3]
- **Using presence as pressure.** Roghanizad and Bohns found that targets felt more awkward refusing in person.[^s5] Ask live to be understood, not to make "no" harder, and leave a genuine way to decline.

## Evidence

The practical patterns above rest on fairly direct experiments, but the grand theory is weaker. Dennis and Kinney's 1998 experiment, in which two-person teams worked on decision tasks through computer-mediated and video communication, found that participants noticed richness differences, but matching richness to task equivocality did not improve decision quality, time, consensus or satisfaction; the authors reported no support for media richness theory's central proposition.[^s2] Media synchronicity theory was developed partly in response.[^s3] A 2026 meta-analysis of 31 studies of channel expansion theory describes earlier results as mixed and identifies synchronicity, setting and power distance as moderators.[^s6] The email-tone and in-person-request findings come from student samples, and Roghanizad and Bohns call theirs exploratory.[^s4][^s5] Status is therefore `mixed`: treat "match the channel to the job" as a sound default, not a law.[^s2][^s6]

## Connections

- **Explained by media richness theory**, the Daft-Lengel ranking of media by feedback, cues, personal focus and language.[^s1]
- **Explained by media synchronicity theory**, which links conveyance and convergence to different media capabilities.[^s3]
- **Challenged by Dennis and Kinney (1998)**, whose experiment found no performance benefit from matching richness to equivocality.[^s2]
- **Supported by** the egocentrism-over-email experiments on misread tone, and by the ask-in-person studies on request success.[^s4][^s5]
- **Applied in email**, the channel where the tone findings were obtained.[^s4]
- **Requires audience analysis** and relates to answer-first writing, asynchronous communication and the curse of knowledge, the same egocentric gap that leads senders to overrate how clear their text is.[^s4]
