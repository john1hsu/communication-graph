# Communicating uncertainty to decision makers

Communicating uncertainty is the skill of telling the people who have to decide what you know, how precisely you know it, how good the evidence is, and what you are doing to learn more. At work it shows up whenever you forecast a date, size a risk, estimate a cost or report early findings to an executive or client. A 2019 review by Anne Marthe van der Bles and colleagues describes a spectrum of ways to do it, from giving a full probability distribution or a range, through verbal qualifiers such as "probably", down to merely admitting that uncertainty exists, never mentioning it, or denying it outright.[^s1] The skill lies in choosing the most precise expression your knowledge honestly supports.[^s1]

## Origin

Uncertainty has long been handled within individual fields - confidence intervals in statistics, likelihood scales in intelligence and climate reports - but that 2019 review, whose authors include the statistician David Spiegelhalter, pulled these traditions into one framework.[^s1] Adapting Lasswell's classic model of communication, they asked who is communicating, what they are uncertain about (a fact, a number or a scientific model), in what form, to whom and with what effect.[^s1] Their key distinction is between **direct** uncertainty about the estimate itself and **indirect** uncertainty about the quality of the evidence behind it, which they recommend reporting separately.[^s1] Crisis communicators reached similar practical advice independently: CDC's Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication (CERC) training tells spokespeople to say what information is not yet available and what is being done to get it, and warns against over-reassuring.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Many professionals fear that admitting uncertainty will make them look less competent, so they either project false confidence or bury their estimate under hedges. The evidence suggests the first fear is largely misplaced and the second habit is costly. In five experiments with 5,780 participants, van der Bles and colleagues found that stating a numerical range around a figure (for example, unemployment of 3.9% within a stated range) had little or no effect on trust in the number or its source, whereas a vague verbal caveat reduced trust more.[^s2] In a field experiment on the BBC News website, neither format reduced trust in the number or in the statisticians.[^s2] Verbal hedges also fail on clarity: readers translate words such as "very likely" into very different probabilities, often shaped by their prior views.[^s1] A decision maker given "should be fine" can neither plan for the downside nor challenge the estimate.

## What good looks like

- **The estimate and its range travel together.** A point estimate plus a plausible range is the format the 2020 experiments recommend.[^s2]
- **Evidence quality is stated separately.** "Our best estimate is X (range Y-Z); we have moderate confidence because it rests on one pilot" keeps direct and indirect uncertainty apart, as the 2019 framework advises.[^s1]
- **Precision matches knowledge.** Use a range where you can estimate one, a list of possibilities where you cannot, and a plain "we don't know yet" only when that is the truth.[^s1]
- **Gaps come with a plan.** Say what is unknown, what is being done to find out and when you will update.[^s4]
- **Reassurance is not overstated.** CERC guidance pairs acknowledging fear and uncertainty with avoiding over-reassurance, and advises stating continued concern before good news.[^s4]

## How to practise it

1. **Name what you are uncertain about.** Before a briefing, write down whether the doubt concerns a fact (did the outage cause the churn?), a number (how many accounts will cancel?) or a model (will the trend continue?), and why - natural variation, thin data or disagreement.[^s1]
2. **Translate hedges into numbers.** Take a draft status report, circle every "probably", "should" and "may", and replace each with a range or an explicit probability where you have one.[^s1][^s2]
3. **Add a one-line evidence note.** After each estimate, say in one sentence what it rests on and how solid that is.[^s1]
4. **Script the "don't know" answer.** Rehearse: "We don't know yet; here's what we're doing to find out, and we'll update you by Thursday."[^s4]
5. **Test it on one reader.** Ask a colleague what number they take from your wording; if it differs from yours, rewrite. The 2019 authors recommend testing uncertainty messages with the intended audience where possible.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is `mixed`. A 2020 systematic review of 68 experiments (38,947 participants) found that communicating uncertainty produced positive, negative and null effects on belief and trust, with many nulls.[^s3] The type of uncertainty mattered: technical uncertainty such as ranges and probabilities showed only positive or null effects, acknowledging knowledge gaps had mixed effects, and highlighting disagreement among experts produced most of the negative effects.[^s3] Effects also depended on audiences' prior views and trust in science.[^s3] The review pooled no effect sizes, many included studies were small, and the authors flagged possible publication bias.[^s3] The van der Bles experiments were large, but the verbal-caveat penalty seen online did not reappear in their BBC field test.[^s2] Nearly all of this work studies the public reading science or statistics, not managers reading business forecasts, so applying it in the boardroom is a reasonable but untested extrapolation.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a core skill of risk communication, which covers how organisations talk about hazards and probabilities.[^s1][^s4]
- **Addresses vague probability language:** verbal qualifiers are read inconsistently, and replacing them with ranges is the main fix.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by van der Bles et al. (2020),** whose five experiments showed numerical ranges barely dent trust.[^s2] **Introduced in van der Bles et al. (2019)** as a unified framework.[^s1]
- **Supported by Gustafson and Rice (2020),** whose review found technical uncertainty rarely backfires while consensus uncertainty can.[^s3]
- **Applied in crisis communication,** where CDC's CERC guidance asks spokespeople to say what is unknown and what is being done.[^s4]
- **Narrower:** words of estimative probability is a specific technique for pinning likelihood words to numeric ranges.[^s1]
- **Related:** credibility, which candour about uncertainty can protect;[^s2] bottom line up front, status reporting and managing up, where the estimate should lead; delivering bad news; and the illusion of transparency, which makes writers assume their hedges are understood as intended.[^s1]
