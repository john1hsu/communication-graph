# Executive briefing

An executive briefing is a short, usually scheduled exchange in which you bring a senior decision maker up to speed on an issue or ask them to decide something. It may be a 15-minute slot in a leadership meeting, a one-to-one with a director, or a two-page paper read on the way to the next meeting. Military staff training distinguishes the purposes clearly: an information brief aims only at understanding, a decision brief exists to get a choice made, and a staff brief keeps a leader current and coordinates effort.[^s1][^s2] Knowing which one you are giving decides almost everything else.[^s1]

## Origin

The most codified version comes from military staff practice. U.S. Army and Marine Corps instruction has long taught formal briefing types with set structures: an introduction that states the purpose, a body of facts, assumptions and options, and a close that asks for a decision.[^s1][^s2] An Army lesson plan from 1996 already told officers to state the recommended course of action at the start and to word it so that, once approved, it can stand as the decision itself.[^s2] Civil services teach the written equivalent; New Zealand's central policy guidance, for example, describes a briefing as a short paper giving a minister initial information and advice, and expects it to fit on about two pages.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Senior leaders' attention is fragmented and heavily pre-committed. In a two-year ethnographic study of seven chief executives of English NHS hospital and mental-health trusts, Nicolini and Korica (2021) found the CEOs facing a high volume of varied, competing demands; they read meeting papers in transit and relied on assistants and a trusted inner circle to filter and summarise what reached them.[^s3] The authors describe how attention can become spread too thin, how small issues pile up, and how much of a month's attention is already committed before it starts.[^s3] A briefing that makes the decision maker dig for the point therefore competes badly with everything else on their desk. The study is small and confined to one sector and country, so it illustrates the conditions briefers face rather than measuring how briefings perform.[^s3]

## What works here

**Decide the purpose and the ask before anything else.** Training materials tell briefers to define the purpose and desired outcome first and to inform rather than sell or entertain.[^s1] For a decision, open by saying a decision will be sought, frame the problem as a question, and give your recommendation up front.[^s2]

**Lead with the answer, keep detail in reserve.** Put findings, conclusions and the request first, then supporting detail; Duarte suggests planning as if your slot has been cut to five minutes and keeping the rest of the material as backup to pull up when asked.[^s5] In writing, identify the one question the briefing answers and cut anything that does not serve it.[^s4]

**Make the recommendation easy to accept or decline.** Recommendations should be specific rather than requests for opinion, should follow from the analysis, and should not introduce new options at the end.[^s1] If no decision comes, ask for one.[^s2]

**Expect questions, and plan for them.** Senior listeners often interrupt before you finish.[^s5] Answer a question before moving on, or say where in the brief you will answer it - and then do so.[^s1][^s2] Telling the group how the time will be split between your summary and discussion can make them more willing to let you finish your main points.[^s5] If you do not know an answer, say so and follow up quickly; the Army lesson plan sets a normal limit of 24 hours.[^s2]

**Rehearse with a critic.** Both military sources recommend rehearsing in front of someone knowledgeable, ideally in the actual room with the visuals ready.[^s1][^s2]

A workplace example: a product manager has ten minutes with the COO about a delayed launch. *Before:* she opens with the history of vendor problems and reaches the request on slide nine. *After:* "I need a decision today: move the launch two weeks, or ship on time without the reporting module. I recommend the two-week delay; here is why." The history moves to backup slides. The leader can act on the first two sentences, and the discussion goes to the trade-off instead of the timeline.[^s2][^s5]

## Pitfalls

- **Confusing briefing types.** Delivering a fact-heavy information brief when you need a decision leaves the leader without a clear choice to make.[^s1][^s2]
- **Opinion-seeking recommendations.** "What do you think we should do?" hands the analysis back to the person with the least time to do it.[^s1]
- **Over-length.** Long sentences, jargon, acronyms and lists of more than about seven items make written briefings harder to use; the New Zealand guidance targets sentences of 15-20 words.[^s4]
- **Hedged language.** Words such as "hope", "try" or "aim" blur what you are actually proposing.[^s4]
- **Letting questions hijack the plan.** Answering everything at length, or never returning to deferred points, can run the slot out before the ask.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Complements** bottom line up front: decision briefs put the recommended action at the start, the same answer-first move this graph's BLUF article describes for writing.[^s2]
- **Complements** presentation rehearsal: briefing courses build rehearsal, from alone to a live critic to a dress rehearsal, into preparation.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Nicolini and Korica (2021), which documents the fragmented, filtered attention of senior executives that briefings must work within.[^s3]
- **Explained by** the attention-based view of the firm, which Nicolini and Korica extend; it explains why what reaches a leader, and in what form, shapes what gets acted on.[^s3]
- **Requires** audience analysis: briefers are told to study the audience's position, interests and existing knowledge first.[^s1][^s2] It is a kind of workplace meeting and relates to managing up, status reporting, issue selling, the pyramid principle, executive summaries, communicating uncertainty, words of estimative probability and natural frequencies, and information overload.[^s3][^s4]
