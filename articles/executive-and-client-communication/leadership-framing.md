steps:
  - Before you speak, write down the competing readings of the situation people already hold, including the rumour version and the worst case.
  - Write the frame in one sentence that defines the situation here and now in the listener's terms - what is happening, why, and what it asks of them.
  - Pick one or two framing devices that make that sentence vivid and repeatable - a metaphor, a contrast with the old way, or a short true story - and drop the rest.
  - Test the frame against the facts and against the toughest listener; cut any claim you could not defend if it were checked later.
  - Prime yourself for unplanned moments by rehearsing how you would answer the two or three questions you most expect, so hallway and one-to-one conversations carry the same frame.
  - Use the frame consistently across channels, then listen for the frames others play back and revise yours when they expose something you missed.
example:
  situation: A product director has to explain why a planned feature launch is slipping six weeks after a security review found problems.
  before: "Due to some unforeseen challenges identified during the review process, the launch timeline has been adjusted. We remain committed to delivering value and appreciate everyone's patience."
  after: "We're moving the launch six weeks because the security review found two flaws we would rather fix now than explain to customers later. Think of it as finishing the brakes before we sell the car. The work isn't wasted - the features are done; what changes is the hardening sprint, which Priya's team will scope by Friday. I made the call, and I'll update you every Monday until we ship."
  why: The rewrite replaces passive, blame-free fog with an active frame (a deliberate trade-off for customer safety), one memorable metaphor, a contrast between what is and isn't lost, and a named owner - so people repeat a defensible account instead of inventing one.
selfCheck:
  - Can I say in one sentence what this situation means for the people hearing it, in their words rather than mine?
  - Which competing frames are already circulating, and does mine answer them?
  - Have I chosen one or two devices (metaphor, contrast, story) rather than piling them up?
  - Would my frame still hold if someone checked it against what actually happens next?
  - Am I using active language that shows who decided and who owns the next step?

# Leadership framing

Leadership framing is the practice of shaping what a situation means for other people by choosing which aspects to include, leave out or emphasise when you talk about it. Gail Fairhurst distinguishes the frame - the mental picture a leader holds of "the situation here and now" - from framing, the act of communicating that picture so others can share it.[^s1] Because most workplace events are ambiguous, the interpretation that takes hold often matters more than the event itself: a delayed launch can become "a failure" or "a deliberate trade-off", and people act on whichever reading wins.[^s6]

## Origin

The technique was popularised for managers by communication scholar Gail Fairhurst and consultant Robert Sarr in *The Art of Framing: Managing the Language of Leadership*, whose core argument is that leaders cannot always control events but can influence how others see and interpret them. The book's table of contents names five framing tools - metaphor, jargon, contrast, spin and stories - and its synopsis adds visual images.[^s2] Fairhurst revised the work in 2011 as *The Power of Framing*, which organises the practice around rules such as controlling the context, defining the situation and applying ethics, and ties frames to the mental models people draw on when making sense of events.[^s1] Management researchers treat framing as a central mechanism of sensegiving: Peer Fiss and Edward Zajac's 2006 study of German corporations was titled, and designed, as an analysis of sensegiving via framing.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Framing is one of the most widely used ideas in management research, appearing in work on managerial cognition, strategic change, social movements and institutions; Cornelissen and Werner's 2014 review in the *Academy of Management Annals* reviews these streams, notes that they developed largely apart, and proposes links between them for studying framing at different levels.[^s4] For a manager, the practical point is that a decision announced without a frame does not stay neutral - rumours and worst-case readings supply one. Fiss and Zajac found that firms adapted their framing of strategic change to what different stakeholder groups preferred, and that markets responded more positively when firms used frames seen as institutionally appropriate.[^s3] Werner and Cornelissen's theory of institutional change proposes that actors shift understanding either by switching to an alternative frame or by blending old and new ones, and by building common ground around the new template - a useful reminder that a new frame lands more easily when it connects to one people already hold.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Start by listening for the frames already in circulation, then define the situation in one sentence in your listeners' terms.[^s1] Choose one or two devices from the Fairhurst and Sarr toolkit: a metaphor to make an abstract change concrete, a contrast that marks what is new against what came before, or a short true story that shows the frame in action.[^s2] Werner and Cornelissen's distinction is practical here - explicitly marking the contrast with the old way suits a clean break, while blending the new frame with familiar values suits a change people are wary of.[^s5] Fairhurst stresses that much framing is spontaneous, happening in hallway conversations and Q&A rather than in the launch deck, so rehearse your answers to the likely questions in advance; she calls this mental preparation priming.[^s1] Use active language that names who decided: in a 2024 interview, Fairhurst contrasted apologies phrased in passive, responsibility-shifting language with a chief executive who called an incident reprehensible and took direct responsibility, and argued that trust built through such choices is slow to gain and quick to lose.[^s6]

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is treating framing as spin. Fairhurst presents framing as persuasion that always involves ethical choices, and her case studies show credibility collapsing when a leader's frame is contradicted by what later comes to light.[^s1] Fiss and Zajac's finding that firms sometimes decoupled the change they announced from the change they implemented is a warning, not a model: a frame that outruns reality may win a short-term reaction but leaves an organisation exposed when the gap is noticed.[^s3] Other pitfalls are stacking too many metaphors, borrowing the strategy deck's jargon instead of the listener's language, and framing once at the announcement and then going quiet, which leaves the field to competing frames.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is strongest that framing shapes how audiences interpret organisational change: field studies such as Fiss and Zajac's link the frames firms choose to how stakeholders respond, and the broader framing literature is large and theoretically developed.[^s3][^s4] What has not been tested is the technique as taught to managers - there are no controlled trials showing that training leaders in Fairhurst and Sarr's tools improves team understanding or change outcomes, so the step-by-step guidance here rests on practitioner experience and theory, hence `practitioner` status.[^s1][^s4] Framing also works on a contested field: other people frame the same events, and a leader's frame competes with theirs rather than simply being received.[^s5]

## Connections

Leadership framing was introduced by Gail Fairhurst with Robert Sarr in *The Art of Framing*.[^s2] It is a practice that develops sensegiving, the skill of shaping how others interpret change, and Fiss and Zajac's 2006 study supports it as the main vehicle of sensegiving during strategic change.[^s3] It is applied in communicating organisational change, where Werner and Cornelissen's frame switching and blending describe how a new interpretation takes hold.[^s5] It complements narrative persuasion, because stories are one of the framing tools Fairhurst recommends alongside metaphor and argument.[^s1] It is related to gain-loss framing, a narrower decision-research sense of framing, and to sensemaking, the mental-model work that frames draw on.[^s4][^s1] It requires audience analysis, since a frame only works if it defines the situation in terms that connect with the listener.[^s1] Its ethics link it to credibility and apology, where active, responsibility-taking language protects trust.[^s6]
