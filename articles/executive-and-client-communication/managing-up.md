# Managing up

Managing up is the skill of consciously working with your manager - or anyone senior whose decisions shape your work, such as a client sponsor - so that you, they and the organisation all get better results.[^s1] Gabarro and Kotter, who framed the idea, were explicit that it does not mean office politics or flattering the boss; it means treating the relationship as one of mutual dependence between two fallible people and taking your share of responsibility for making it work.[^s1]

## Origin

The idea was set out by Harvard Business School professors John J. Gabarro and John P. Kotter in the 1980 *Harvard Business Review* article "Managing Your Boss", reprinted by HBR as a classic in 2005.[^s1] Their opening case follows a capable new executive who never worked out what his boss needed; a string of misunderstandings contributed to a failed product launch, and he was let go about fourteen months after joining.[^s1] Academic research on upward communication grew in parallel: Dutton and Ashford's 1993 *Academy of Management Review* paper described "issue selling" - how people below the top get senior leaders to pay attention to an issue - drawing on upward influence and impression management research.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Your manager controls much of what you need - priorities, resources, cover, information about the wider organisation - while depending on you to deliver; when the relationship is mishandled, both people and the business suffer.[^s1] Senior leaders' attention is scarce, so what reaches them and how it is framed shapes what the organisation acts on.[^s2] In consulting and client work the same logic applies to the client sponsor: they are your "boss" for the engagement, with their own pressures and their own superiors.

## What good looks like

Gabarro and Kotter describe three parts.[^s1] First, understand your manager: their goals, pressures, strengths, blind spots and preferred working style, checked rather than assumed.[^s1] Second, understand yourself, including your reflexes towards authority - some people instinctively resist a boss, others comply too readily and swallow disagreement, and both distort the relationship.[^s1] Third, build a relationship that fits both of you: match how they like to take in information (some prefer to read first, others to talk it through), agree mutual expectations out loud, keep information flowing - including bad news - be dependable and honest rather than optimistic, and spend their limited time and goodwill on what matters.[^s1]

When you need your manager or leadership to back an idea, the issue-selling research adds communication moves: package the issue in the logic of a business case and link it to goals leaders already care about, involve peers and other senior people, and judge timing and channel.[^s3][^s4] Ashford and Detert's later advice for selling ideas upward is similar: tailor the pitch to the audience's goals, frame it as an opportunity, keep emotions in check, pick the moment, follow the organisation's norms for evidence and format, and arrive with solutions rather than only problems.[^s5]

A workplace example: an analyst's manager keeps rewriting her weekly update at the last minute. Before, she sent a long narrative email on Friday afternoon and waited. After asking in their one-on-one, she learned the manager forwards it to a director on Friday mornings and only needs risks and decisions; she now sends three bullet points - decisions needed, risks, progress - by Thursday at 4 p.m., and flags any slipping date the day she knows it. The rewrite works because it fits the manager's real use of the update instead of the analyst's guess.[^s1]

## How to practise it

In your next one-on-one, ask what your manager's priorities and worries are this quarter and how they prefer to receive updates and bad news; write the answers down.[^s1] Draft a short note of what you think they expect from you - deliverables, decision rights, when to escalate - and ask them to correct it.[^s1] Each week, check that nothing important is being withheld, and before you take an issue upward, rehearse it as a business case tied to their goals with a proposed solution.[^s3][^s5] Review afterwards which requests used their time well and which did not.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Gabarro and Kotter's framework comes from case-based observation and has not been tested as a package, so its status here is practitioner.[^s1] Parts of it have research support. Dutton and colleagues' 2001 study of 82 issue-selling accounts from middle managers at a single hospital found that packaging, involvement and process moves were linked to whether issues gained senior attention, though the data were retrospective self-reports from one organisation.[^s3][^s4] A 2017 meta-analysis of 49 samples (about 9,000 people) found rational persuasion the only influence tactic consistently linked to good task and relationship outcomes, while pressure was negatively linked - consistent with leading with a reasoned case rather than force.[^s6] The skill also has limits: it cannot fix a manager who punishes honest information, and the line between adapting to a boss and ingratiating yourself is honesty - the authors exclude apple-polishing, and dependable candour is the core of the approach.[^s1]

## Connections

Managing up was introduced by John J. Gabarro and John P. Kotter in "Managing Your Boss", and is practised mostly in one-on-one meetings and through status reporting, where expectations and information flow are set.[^s1] It complements issue selling, the research tradition on getting senior leaders to attend to an issue, and is supported by Dutton and colleagues' 2001 study of issue-selling moves and, more loosely, by Lee and colleagues' 2017 meta-analysis of influence tactics.[^s2][^s3][^s6] It sits under organisational communication and requires audience analysis, since understanding the boss is audience analysis applied to one person; it is related to influence without authority (lateral and upward influence through exchange), bottom line up front and channel selection (matching how the boss takes in information), stakeholder mapping, and psychological safety, which governs whether candour upward is welcome.[^s1][^s2]
