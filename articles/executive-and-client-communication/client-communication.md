# Client communication

Client communication is everything a professional says and writes to the people they serve: the first scoping call, the kickoff, weekly updates, the email admitting a slipped date, the final readout. It covers external clients of consultancies, agencies, law and accounting firms, and internal clients of IT, HR, finance or analytics teams. Its defining feature is information asymmetry: the client usually cannot fully judge expert work, even after it is delivered, so the communication around the work becomes much of what they judge.[^s2]

## Origin

The topic grew out of services-marketing research in the 1980s and 1990s, which asked why customers end up satisfied or loyal. Large field studies modelled satisfaction as the gap between what customers expected and the quality they perceived.[^s3] Sharma and Patterson (1999) carried that work into "credence" professional services, where clients struggle to assess quality, and tested communication effectiveness alongside technical quality, functional quality and trust as drivers of client commitment.[^s1] Organizational psychologists studying consultants reached a related insight from the other side: providers and clients often weight different parts of the relationship.[^s6]

## Why it matters

In Sharma and Patterson's study of personal financial planning clients, communication effectiveness drove the other antecedents in the model and was the strongest single predictor of whether clients stayed committed to the relationship.[^s1] Patterson's 2016 review of the follow-up research reports that communication has since been linked to client comfort, co-production, engagement and value co-creation, and argues that first-time buyers in particular rely on it to judge whether the work and the fee were worth it.[^s2] Expectations matter because of an asymmetry: in a Swedish survey of 22,300 customers, quality that fell short of expectations hurt satisfaction and repurchase intent more than quality that beat expectations helped.[^s3] The practical upshot is that a client surprised by bad news costs more than a client pleased by a small over-delivery gains you.

## What works here

- **Turn vague expectations into explicit ones early.** Ojasalo describes three troublesome kinds of client expectation: fuzzy (the client wants "better" without knowing what), implicit (assumed and never said) and unrealistic. The remedy is to make the fuzzy precise, the implicit explicit and the unrealistic realistic before the work begins.[^s4] A short written charter listing goals, scope, assumptions, constraints, risks and what is still unknown, reviewed with the client and revised as you learn, gives that conversation a home.[^s5]
- **Map who the client actually is.** The sponsor who signs is rarely the only audience. Build a quick org chart and contact list, hold short informal conversations with key stakeholders, and note anyone whose concerns could derail the work.[^s5]
- **Keep a steady rhythm of updates.** Regular check-ins and written status reports prevent surprises and build credibility between milestones.[^s5]
- **Ask about fears, not just requirements.** Probing for what worries the client, then sharing how you will mitigate those risks, makes the relationship safer to be honest in.[^s5]
- **Talk about what the client values, not only what you value.** In one UK study, internal consultants rated relationship building as most important, while their clients put more weight on contract details and project logistics.[^s6] A good update covers both: the relationship and the plain facts of scope, dates and cost.

A worked example: an analytics team agrees to "improve the sales dashboard." Before building anything, the lead writes back: "To confirm, success means regional managers can see weekly pipeline by rep without exporting to Excel, live by 30 June. Out of scope: forecasting. We'll send a two-line update every Friday." A fuzzy request has become a checkable one, and the client knows when they will hear from the team.[^s4][^s5]

## Pitfalls

- **Promising to delight.** Because shortfalls weigh more than surpluses, inflated early promises are a poor trade; promise what you can reliably deliver.[^s3]
- **Silence until the deliverable.** Going quiet leaves the client to judge the work with nothing but the outcome, which they may be unable to evaluate.[^s2][^s5]
- **Hiding scope creep.** Letting unstated or unrealistic expectations persist sets up later dissatisfaction even when the work is technically sound.[^s4]
- **Taking all the credit.** Overstating your own contribution erodes the trust that client communication depends on; share credit with the client's team.[^s5]

Evidence limits: the strongest studies here are cross-sectional surveys in particular service settings (financial planning, Swedish consumer markets, one UK company), so they show strong associations rather than tested interventions, and effect sizes will vary by industry and relationship length.[^s1][^s3][^s6] Much how-to advice, such as charters and weekly updates, is practitioner guidance rather than experimentally tested.[^s5]

## Connections

Client communication is a workplace application of interpersonal communication inside organizational communication, and it requires audience analysis and active listening. Its empirical backbone is Sharma and Patterson's 1999 finding that communication effectiveness predicts relationship commitment in professional services.[^s1] Why expectation-setting matters is explained by the expectation-disconfirmation account of satisfaction, which Anderson and Sullivan's 1993 field study demonstrated at scale.[^s3] Day to day it complements status reporting and stakeholder mapping, both of which practitioner guidance treats as core habits of client-facing work.[^s5] It relates to the trusted advisor framework, managing up, executive briefings, credibility and rapport, and to delivering bad news, escalation and communicating uncertainty for its harder moments.
