# Executive presence

Executive presence is the impression that you can be trusted with consequential work: that you stay composed under pressure, speak and decide clearly, and carry yourself in a way that makes senior people and clients listen.[^s2] Dagley and Gaskin, who studied it empirically, define it in terms of the audience: a person has presence when others judge that they exert influence beyond their formal authority, and that judgement can change over time.[^s1] Treated as a skill, it is less about charisma than about a set of observable behaviours you can name, practise and get feedback on.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

The phrase circulated for decades in executive coaching and promotion discussions, usually as vague feedback ("you need more presence").[^s1] Economist Sylvia Ann Hewlett and the Center for Talent Innovation gave it a popular structure in a study fielded in early 2012 - a survey of nearly 4,000 professionals and 268 senior executives, plus focus groups and interviews - which grouped presence into three pillars: gravitas, communication and appearance.[^s2] Hewlett re-surveyed 73 executives in 2022 and reported in *Harvard Business Review* (2024) that the expected traits had shifted.[^s3] In parallel, Australian researchers Gavin Dagley and Cadeyrn Gaskin published a peer-reviewed qualitative study in 2014, interviewing 34 professionals who assess executives for a living.[^s1]

## Why it matters

In the 2012 survey, senior executives attributed about a quarter (26%) of what it takes to win the next promotion to presence - a self-reported opinion, not a measured effect, but a sign of how much weight decision makers give it.[^s2] Gravitas dominated: 67% of executives named it the core pillar, against 28% for communication and 5% for appearance.[^s2] For anyone briefing leaders or advising clients, presence shapes whether your analysis is heard before it is judged on its merits.[^s1]

## What good looks like

Dagley and Gaskin found ten characteristics. Five are judged in first contacts - status and reputation, physical appearance, projected confidence, communication ability and engagement skills - and five are judged over time: interpersonal integrity, values in action, intellect and expertise, delivering outcomes, and (negatively) coercive use of power.[^s1] Presence that starts strong can prove unsustainable when integrity or delivery fails, and people can have unexpected presence despite a quiet demeanour.[^s1]

Hewlett's surveys point the same way. Confidence under pressure and decisiveness lead gravitas; speaking skills, commanding a room and reading the audience lead communication.[^s2] By 2022, inclusiveness had joined the top gravitas traits, a listen-to-learn style was preferred over forcefulness, and authenticity had become valued in how people present themselves.[^s3]

Example: a project lead is asked by the chief operating officer whether a launch will slip. Before: she lists every open issue, apologises twice and ends with "it's hard to say". After: she pauses, gives her call - a two-week slip is likely - names the one decision she needs, asks what the COO is most worried about, and offers a written follow-up. The second version shows composure, decisiveness and listening, which are what observers read as presence.[^s2][^s3]

## How to practise it

Turn vague feedback into behaviours: ask the person who gave it what they saw, and pick two or three traits per pillar to work on, including at least one that is rising in importance, such as inclusiveness.[^s3] Many of the behaviours are trainable. In Antonakis and colleagues' experiments, managers and MBA students trained in charismatic leadership tactics - stories, metaphors, contrasts, three-part lists, expressing confidence in the goal, and animated voice, face and gestures - were rated as more charismatic and more leader-like, with a combined effect of about d = 0.62.[^s4] Record a short talk on a hard decision, review it against those tactics, and repeat.[^s4] Over time, protect the slow-built characteristics - keep commitments and deliver results - because those decide whether first impressions last.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The construct rests mainly on surveys, interviews and practitioner reports, so its status here is practitioner.[^s1][^s2] The 2012 survey is self-report from a sponsored study, and the 2022 re-survey sampled only 73 executives.[^s2][^s3] More presence is not always better: Vergauwe and colleagues found a curvilinear link between charismatic personality and rated leader effectiveness across several hundred leaders, with very high charisma rated as weaker on operational follow-through.[^s5]

Presence standards are also applied unevenly. In the 2012 data, 56% of professionals of colour felt held to a stricter standard (against 31% of white peers), and 81% of women and professionals of colour did not know how to act on the presence feedback they got.[^s2] Most people nominated as having presence in Dagley and Gaskin's study were men.[^s1] Hewlett notes the traditional ideal was modelled on white male executives.[^s3] If you give presence feedback, name the behaviour and check you would say the same to anyone.[^s2]

## Connections

The three-pillar model was popularised by Sylvia Ann Hewlett through the Center for Talent Innovation's 2012 study, and the construct was mapped empirically in Dagley and Gaskin's 2014 study.[^s1][^s2] It sits under impression management and requires audience analysis, since reading the room is one of its core communication behaviours; it is applied most visibly in presentations and executive briefings.[^s2] It complements credibility, because reputation, expertise and integrity are among its characteristics.[^s1] Its trainable core is supported by Antonakis and colleagues' charisma-training experiments, and the idea that more is always better is challenged by Vergauwe and colleagues' finding that very high charisma backfires.[^s4][^s5] It is related to managing up, paralanguage, silence and pauses, active listening, speaking anxiety, and power posing, a popular presence tip whose evidence is weighed in its own article.
