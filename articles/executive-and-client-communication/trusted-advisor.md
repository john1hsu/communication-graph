# Trusted advisor

A trusted advisor is the consultant, accountant, lawyer or internal expert whom a client or senior stakeholder turns to first - not just for technical answers but to think through a problem - and the framework of that name explains how that standing is earned.[^s1] Its best-known tool, the trust equation, breaks trustworthiness into rational parts (credibility and reliability) and an emotional part (intimacy), all divided by self-orientation: the more the advisor seems focused on themselves, the less they are trusted.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The framework comes from *The Trusted Advisor* (2000) by David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford, written for professional-services advisors and reissued in a 20th-anniversary edition in 2021 with material on communicating in a digital world.[^s1] Besides the trust equation, the book sets out a five-step process for building trust-based relationships: engage, listen, frame, envision and commit.[^s1] It has since spread well beyond consulting; the *Journal of Accountancy* described the equation in 2021 as a classic tool for CPA firms.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The book's premise is that technical expertise is necessary but not enough: clients act on advice, renew engagements and bring you into early conversations when they trust you, not merely when you are right.[^s1] Practitioners also see trust as protection against price competition - a client who trusts its advisor is less likely to switch to a cheaper or newer firm.[^s2] Inside organisations the same logic applies to anyone advising executives, such as finance partners, analysts and HR business partners.

## How to apply it

Use the four terms as a diagnostic before an important client or executive conversation.[^s1][^s2]

- **Credibility** - what you say is believable: expertise, sector knowledge and being candid about what you do not know.[^s2]
- **Reliability** - your actions are dependable: keeping small promises, sending the note when you said you would, and anticipating needs rather than only meeting them.[^s2]
- **Intimacy** - the client feels safe raising sensitive issues with you, which comes from conversations that go beyond the deliverable to their pressures and goals; accountants in the *Journal of Accountancy* piece found this the hardest element.[^s2]
- **Self-orientation** - the denominator: visible focus on your sale, your fee, your cleverness or being right; lower it by asking probing questions and keeping attention on the client's success.[^s2]

Then follow the five steps in order: engage the client on an issue that matters to them, listen until they feel understood, frame the real problem, envision with them what a good outcome looks like, and commit to concrete next steps.[^s1] Because each step builds on the one before, a common mistake is jumping from engage to a recommended solution and skipping the listening that earns the right to frame; another is trying to raise credibility alone - more slides, more credentials - when the weak term is reliability or intimacy.[^s1]

Channel and seniority change how the terms show up. In email and status reports, reliability is the most visible term - commitments met, needs anticipated, problems flagged early - while intimacy depends on conversations that go beyond the deliverable, which mostly happen live.[^s2] With executives, keeping the focus on the client's success often means a short answer that serves their decision rather than a tour of your analysis.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The trust equation is a practitioner model, presented in a business book for advisors rather than derived from measurement; its arithmetic (adding three terms and dividing by a fourth) is best read as a teaching device, not a measured relationship.[^s1][^s2] Its components do line up with the most-tested academic model of trust, Mayer, Davis and Schoorman's 1995 framework, which defines trust as willingness to be vulnerable to another party and traces it to their perceived ability, benevolence and integrity.[^s3] That model also proposes that integrity matters most early in a relationship and benevolence grows in importance as people interact.[^s3]

A 2007 meta-analysis of 132 independent samples found all three trustworthiness factors had unique links to trust (corrected correlations of roughly .62 to .67), so competence alone does not account for it.[^s4] In a field survey of 80 matched consultant-client pairs on enterprise-system projects, Ko (2010) found that clients' benevolent trust in consultants, not competence trust, predicted successful knowledge transfer - consistent with the framework's emphasis on intimacy and low self-orientation, though from a single study in one setting.[^s5] Treat the model as a checklist for being trustworthy, not a script for appearing so: intimacy performed to close a sale is still self-orientation, the term the framework says lowers trust.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

The framework was introduced by David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford in *The Trusted Advisor*, and is applied mainly in client communication across consulting, accounting and other professional services.[^s1][^s2] It is loosely supported by Colquitt, Scott and LePine's 2007 meta-analysis showing that integrity and benevolence add to ability in predicting trust, and by Ko's 2010 study of consultant-client pairs.[^s4][^s5] It sits under interpersonal communication and requires active listening, since the listen step carries the process; it relates to credibility (one of its terms), rapport and self-disclosure (routes to intimacy), perspective-taking, managing up (the same logic aimed at your own manager) and trust repair, which applies when reliability or integrity has failed.[^s1][^s3]
