# Crisis communication

Crisis communication is what an organization says, and how and when it says it, while something has gone badly wrong: a service outage, a data breach, a product recall, a safety incident, a public-health emergency. Two traditions inform it. Emergency and public-health practice, codified in the CDC's Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication (CERC) programme, focuses on helping people make good protective decisions under time pressure and imperfect information.[^s1] Reputation research in public relations focuses on how stakeholders assign blame and how the organization's response shapes their view of it afterwards.[^s3]

## Origin

The CDC's CERC framework condenses its guidance into six principles: be first, be right, be credible, express empathy, promote action and show respect.[^s1][^s2] On the reputation side, W. Timothy Coombs and Sherry Holladay set out situational crisis communication theory (SCCT), which sorts 13 crisis types into three clusters by how much responsibility the public assigns: victim crises (natural disasters, rumours, tampering), accidental crises (technical failures) and preventable crises (human error, misconduct).[^s3] Their 2002 initial tests, with 130 students rating real crisis cases, found that the more responsibility people attributed, the worse they rated the organization's reputation in all three clusters.[^s3]

## Why it matters

People in a crisis tend to simplify messages, cling to what they already believe, look for second opinions and trust the first message they hear, so whoever speaks first and clearly shapes the story.[^s1] Silence does not stop the story; it hands it to rumour, and the CDC's guidance treats rumours as more damaging than hard truths.[^s1] Inside a company the same dynamics apply: if staff first learn of a breach from a news site or a customer rather than from their own leaders, that outside version becomes the one they believe, and the organization is judged less trustworthy than if it had disclosed the problem itself.[^s1][^s5]

## What works here

- **Lead with protective instructions.** Coombs and Holladay argue that, whatever the crisis type, people first need to know what to do to protect themselves, before any reputational message.[^s3] CERC adds that action steps calm anxiety and restore a sense of control, so keep them simple and memorable.[^s1][^s2]
- **Say what is known, unknown and being done.** Be accurate, share facts as you confirm them, name the gaps and explain how you are closing them, and give a time for the next update.[^s1][^s2]
- **Acknowledge feelings in words, without over-reassuring.** Name the disruption, fear or anger people feel; even a small harm can feel unacceptable to those affected.[^s1][^s2]
- **Match responsibility to your part.** SCCT recommends more accommodating responses (corrective action, full apology) as responsibility rises, and instructing information alone for true victim crises.[^s3] In experiments, a rebuild response (apology, compensation) produced the best reputation outcome across crisis types.[^s4]
- **Disclose it yourself.** "Stealing thunder", breaking your own bad news before others do, made a communicator seem more trustworthy and credible than having a third party reveal it, and adding a brief note on why you are disclosing helped further.[^s5]
- **Plan the spokesperson and sign-off in advance**, and give every audience and outlet the same facts.[^s1][^s2]

**Example.** An internal status note after a payroll system failure. *Before:* "We are aware of reports of an issue affecting some payments and are investigating. We apologise for any inconvenience." *After:* "About 400 staff were not paid this morning because of a payroll system failure on our side. If you were affected, email payroll-help by 3 pm and we will send an emergency transfer today. We do not yet know whether other pay dates are affected; we will update you by 5 pm. We know this causes real stress and we are sorry." The second version states scope and cause, gives an action and deadline, names the unknown, sets the next update and owns the failure.[^s1][^s3]

## Pitfalls

- **Waiting for complete information.** If you cannot share details yet, say you are working on it and when you will update.[^s1]
- **Over-reassurance and speculation.** False calm and guesses cost credibility when facts change; avoid humour and condescension too.[^s1]
- **Repeating the accusation while rebutting it**, which spreads the claim further.[^s1]
- **Denial or minimising when you are at fault.** Defensive strategies suit only crises where the organization really is a victim.[^s3][^s4]
- **Inconsistent messages** across teams, channels or partners.[^s1]

## Evidence

The evidence is real but thinner and less clear-cut than the frameworks suggest. Coombs and Holladay's initial tests relied on student raters, did not test severity or performance history, and left open whether audiences perceive response strategies the way theorists classify them.[^s3] Claeys, Cauberghe and Vyncke's 2010 experiment with 316 consumers confirmed that preventable crises harm reputation most and that rebuild responses work best, but found no significant crisis-type-by-strategy interaction, so SCCT's core matching hypothesis was not supported.[^s4] The benefit of stealing thunder replicated across six experiments pooled in a 2025 integrative analysis (N = 1,573), but effects were small (r around .08 to .13) and based on hypothetical scenarios.[^s5] Lee and Fraustino's 2025 study suggests the effect depends on context, working through lower perceived severity and anger and interacting with the company's prior social-responsibility record.[^s6] The CERC principles are government best practice drawn from response experience, so treat them as sound practice rather than tested causal findings.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

- **Explained by** situational crisis communication theory, which links attributed responsibility to reputational threat and to the response that fits it.[^s3]
- **Demonstrated by** Coombs and Holladay's 2002 initial tests of SCCT.[^s3]
- **Challenged by** Claeys, Cauberghe and Vyncke's 2010 experiment, which did not support SCCT's matching hypothesis.[^s4]
- **Supported by** Vaughan-Johnston and colleagues' 2025 integrative analysis showing that self-disclosure ("stealing thunder") raises trustworthiness and credibility.[^s5]
- **Studied in** public relations research, where SCCT and stealing-thunder experiments are published.[^s4]
- **Related:** communicating uncertainty and plain language; delivering bad news, apology and trust repair (what follows a preventable crisis); denial (the defensive strategy that backfires when you are at fault); inoculation theory and passive voice; and change communication, a slower-moving neighbour.[^s1][^s3][^s4]
