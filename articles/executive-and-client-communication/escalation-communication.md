# Escalating a problem

Escalating a problem means taking an issue, risk or disagreement that cannot be settled at your level to someone with the authority to decide it. Done well, it is an ordinary governance route, not a complaint: project-management guidance treats it as a check on decisions that resolves problems early, cuts rework and makes priorities clear.[^s1] Formal delivery standards build it in, giving each manager tolerances on cost, time, scope, quality and risk and requiring anything beyond those limits, or that the owner cannot resolve, to go to the next level.[^s3]

## Origin

Escalation has no single inventor; it grew out of project governance. The UK government's project delivery standard, for example, sets out tolerances, delegated authority and the expectation that unresolved risks and issues move up the work hierarchy to the senior responsible owner and sponsoring body.[^s3] Practitioner writing in PMI's *PM Network* turned the idea into personal guidance - when to escalate, who starts it and how to behave while it is pending.[^s1] More recently, Hughes and Salvatore argued in *MIT Sloan Management Review* (2025) that organisations should formalise the procedure so disagreements are first worked on jointly by the people involved, instead of each side lobbying a sympathetic leader.[^s5]

## Why it matters

The main failure is not escalating badly but not escalating at all. Information-systems research calls the reluctance to pass bad news upward the "mum effect". Smith, Keil and Depledge (2001) built an explanatory model of it and tested it experimentally, finding that how serious staying silent seemed, and how much wrongdoing people observed, explained a significant share of reluctance to report.[^s6] A 2008 laboratory experiment by Park, Im and Keil found that who was at fault affected willingness to report, and that time urgency raised it indirectly - suggesting managers can encourage early escalation by stressing how narrow the window to fix a problem is.[^s2] Research on employee silence by Morrison, See and Pan (2014), across a lab study and two surveys, found that people who feel powerless at work are less likely to raise problems, an effect that weakens when the supervisor is seen as open to input.[^s4]

The opposite failure is the one-sided escalation. When colleagues take a disagreement separately to leaders likely to back them, a leader may endorse a poor decision on partial information, and trust between the parties suffers.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Escalate when the trigger is clear: the issue exceeds your tolerance or delegated authority, or a genuine attempt to resolve it directly has stalled.[^s1][^s3] Practitioner guidance suggests starting within about two working days of concluding it cannot be resolved at your level, telling your own manager first, and informing the other parties so the meeting stays factual.[^s1] Where possible, escalate jointly: both sides document how they tried to resolve it and bring the disagreement together.[^s5] Lead with the decision needed and the cost of delay, then the facts and options - the standard expects reports to highlight risks, issues and the decisions or direction required.[^s3] Keep working the current plan until a decision is made.[^s1]

Agree the route before you need it. Governance frameworks should set out authority limits and who decides what, and leaders should document and teach the escalation process so people know it is legitimate to use; with clients, agree that path at kickoff so using it later does not read as an accusation.[^s1][^s3]

## Common mistakes

Typical errors are waiting too long out of fear of conflict or looking bad, escalating before making a real effort to resolve the issue, making it personal, going over someone's head without telling them or your manager, arriving without facts, and halting work while a decision is pending.[^s1] Leaders make a matching mistake: if they meet escalations with irritation, they teach people to stay silent, since perceived openness is what weakens the link between powerlessness and silence.[^s4]

## Evidence

The evidence is stronger on the problem than on the technique. The mum effect has experimental support in IT project settings, including tests of a whistle-blowing model adapted to project reporting.[^s2][^s6] The silence research supports leader openness as a moderator.[^s4] The specific practices here - joint escalation, a two-day rule, answer-first framing - are practitioner guidance that has not been tested in controlled studies, so the evidence status is practitioner.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

Escalation is part of **managing up** and draws on **bottom line up front**, since the decision and deadline must come first.[^s3] It **addresses** the **mum effect**, the reluctance to report bad news studied by Park, Im and Keil and modelled by Smith, Keil and Depledge,[^s2][^s6] and **employee silence**, which Morrison and colleagues link to felt powerlessness.[^s4] It is **applied in** **project status reporting**, where reports should surface issues and decisions required.[^s3] It sits next to **selling issues to leadership** (proposing opportunities upward) and **delivering bad news**, and depends on the **psychological safety** that makes raising problems feel safe.[^s4] A **RACI matrix** helps by making clear in advance who decides.[^s3]
