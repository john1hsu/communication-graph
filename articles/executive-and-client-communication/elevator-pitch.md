steps:
  - Write down the one thing you want the listener to do next (a meeting, an introduction, a pilot slot) before you write anything else.
  - State the problem in the listener's terms, then what you propose or offer and the single strongest piece of evidence that it works.
  - Say why you are the person to do it, in one concrete accomplishment rather than adjectives.
  - End with a specific, small request the listener can say yes to on the spot.
  - Cut it to 90 seconds or less, then rehearse it aloud until it sounds conversational rather than memorised.
  - Stop and invite a question; treat what they ask as the start of the real conversation.

# Elevator pitch

An elevator pitch is a brief spoken case - usually 30 to 90 seconds - for an idea, a product, a project or yourself, designed to make the listener want to hear more.[^s6][^s5] Its goal is a next step, not a decision: a meeting, an introduction, a slot on an agenda. At work it shows up whenever you get a few minutes with someone senior or unfamiliar - a VP before a town hall, a prospective client at an event, a hiring manager at a career fair, an investor at a demo day.

## Origin

Where the name comes from is uncertain; the familiar stories involve a pitch delivered to a captive producer or executive during a lift ride.[^s6] Etymology research traces an early documented print use to quality-management author Philip Crosby, who in 1981 advised managers to prepare a one-minute "elevator speech" for the big boss, with "elevator pitch" in print by 1997 and spreading through venture-capital usage by the late 1990s.[^s7] Academic study came through entrepreneurship and creativity research, which treats the pitch as a high-stakes, first-impression judgment made under time pressure.[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

Listeners judge substance through delivery more than they realise. In Clark's (2008) study, 24 UK business angels rated three live pitches on 32 aspects; their investment interest tracked their ratings of presentation quality, with presentational factors such as delivery style weighing most, yet when asked for their reasons they cited the business, market and product rather than the presentation.[^s1] An analysis of 113 funded pitches on *Shark Tank* and *Dragons' Den* found that visible preparedness raised the venture's perceived legitimacy, and legitimacy in turn predicted how much money it raised.[^s4] Judgments also form fast: Hollywood executives in Elsbach and Kramer's study sorted writers into "creative" or "not creative" types within the first moments, and a pitcher who triggered early negative cues rarely recovered.[^s3]

## What works here

Structure the pitch around the listener's interest and a clear ask. Stanford GSB's career guidance recommends keeping it to 90 seconds or less, tailoring it to each audience, and building it from who you are, what you care about, and a relevant accomplishment that shows you can help the listener's organisation.[^s5] A common template adds an attention-getting opening, the problem you solve, and why a follow-up conversation would help this particular person.[^s6]

Engage rather than recite. Elsbach and Kramer found that executives rated pitches higher when the meeting felt like creative collaboration - questions, shared ideas, the listener adding to the concept - and lower when it felt like a lecture.[^s3] Stanford GSB likewise advises checking that the pitch sounds spontaneous rather than memorised, and that people who know you would recognise themselves in it, which keeps it credible.[^s5]

Prepare visibly. In the televised-pitch data, the signal investors rewarded was evidence of preparation - knowing your numbers and answering questions crisply - because it made the venture look like a legitimate, understandable business.[^s4]

## Pitfalls

Treating polish as a substitute for substance is the main trap. In a randomised field experiment, Clingingsmith and Shane (2018) gave about 30 minutes of video pitch training to aspiring entrepreneurs before 90-second pitches to 50 accredited investors at four competitions. Trained pitches contained more best-practice elements, but the average effect on investor interest was slightly negative and not significant; inexperienced investors marked trained pitches down by about 0.3 standard deviations, and the drop was concentrated among the lower-scoring pitches.[^s2] The authors read this as training making pitches more informative, which helps listeners screen out weaker ideas sooner, rather than making every idea more attractive.[^s2] A clear pitch exposes the idea; it does not rescue it.

Other pitfalls: a laundry list of ideas instead of one, which executives read as uncreative or desperate;[^s3] a pitch so rehearsed it sounds canned;[^s5][^s3] and no ask at all, which leaves the listener politely interested with nothing to do. Ethically, the line is the same as for any persuasion: make the real case clearly, and do not inflate evidence to fit the time.

## Evidence

The evidence is real but narrow. Clark's study is small and from one investor forum;[^s1] Pollack and colleagues studied only funded pitches on television;[^s4] Elsbach and Kramer's study is qualitative, from one industry, and partly based on re-enacted pitches.[^s3] The one randomised experiment found that training changed pitch content but not average investor interest, with effects depending on investor experience and pitch quality, in a single region with relatively few experienced investors.[^s2] Evidence for internal and career pitches is mostly practitioner guidance.[^s5][^s6] Hence `mixed`: delivery and preparation clearly shape first judgments, but no study shows that pitch technique alone makes a weak proposal succeed.

## Connections

The genre's evidence base is demonstrated by Clark (2008) on presentation skill and angel interest,[^s1] Clingingsmith and Shane (2018) on pitch training,[^s2] Elsbach and Kramer (2003) on how listeners categorise pitchers,[^s3] and Pollack, Rutherford and Nagy (2012), whose results are explained by cognitive legitimacy - preparation makes a venture look understandable and credible.[^s4] It is applied in networking, where career guidance frames it as a tailored self-introduction.[^s5] It is a short form of presentation and persuasion; it borrows its opening logic from bottom line up front, depends on audience analysis, and overlaps with issue selling and managing up when the pitch goes to your own leadership. Presentation rehearsal and narrative persuasion are the main tools for delivering it, and the curse of knowledge is the usual reason a pitch is full of jargon.
