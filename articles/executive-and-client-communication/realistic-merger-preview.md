  - Within days of the announcement, list the questions each affected group will ask (jobs, pay, reporting lines, location, systems, timing) and draft a plain answer to each.
  - Sort every answer into "decided", "not decided yet - expected by <date>" and "will not change", and include the unwelcome decisions rather than leaving them out.
  - Brief line managers first with the same facts and a short Q&A, so they can answer questions in their own team meetings the same day.
  - Deliver the preview in person or live where possible, then send the written version and a named place to send questions.

  situation: Telling a 40-person regional support team about the first month after their company is acquired.

Whether the two support teams merge is not decided yet; we expect a decision by 30 April, and you'll hear it from me first. Until then I'll update you every Thursday, even if the update is 'no change'."

# Realistic merger preview

A realistic merger preview is a deliberate programme of honest communication given to employees soon after a merger, acquisition or restructuring is announced. Instead of a single upbeat announcement followed by silence, people get specific, repeated information about what the change will mean for them, including the parts they will not like and the parts that are not yet decided.[^s1] The aim is to replace guesswork with facts while people are most anxious, so that the damage an announcement does to trust, commitment and job satisfaction is smaller and shorter-lived.[^s1]

## Origin

The technique comes from a 1991 field experiment by David Schweiger and Angelo DeNisi, published in the *Academy of Management Journal*. When a company announced a merger, employees at one plant received a realistic merger preview - a programme of realistic communications about the merger - while employees at a comparable plant received only limited information. Across four rounds of data collection, the preview plant showed fewer of the harmful after-effects of the announcement, the benefits lasted through the study, and on some measures attitudes returned close to their pre-announcement levels.[^s1] Its name and logic echo the realistic job preview from recruitment research, where giving candidates both the good and the bad news about a job is linked in a 1998 meta-analysis of 40 studies to lower turnover and higher performance.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Mergers and restructurings create uncertainty, and when official communication is thin, rumours fill the gap. In interviews with managers and communication staff at multinational firms, DiFonzo and Bordia found that poor change communication let rumours spread when uncertainty was high, and that successful campaigns chose openness over concealment and worked to build trust.[^s4] The rumours that do circulate tend to be negative: in a hospital going through change, negative rumours outnumbered positive ones, and employees who reported negative rumours also reported more change-related stress.[^s5] A realistic preview tackles both problems at the source by telling people early what is known, what is not and when they will hear more.[^s1][^s4]

## How to apply it

Start from the questions people are actually asking, not from the strategy deck: jobs, pay, managers, location, tools and timing.[^s4] Answer each one in plain terms and label it as decided, not yet decided (with a date), or unchanged. Include the losses people can already see coming; the point of a realistic preview is that it is not sanitised.[^s1] Brief line managers first so they can repeat the same facts in team meetings, deliver the preview live where you can, and then keep a fixed update rhythm.[^s4] Recruitment research on previews suggests that face-to-face delivery and longer, more substantive previews tend to do better than brief written ones, though those effects are modest.[^s3] Finally, treat the rumours you hear as a list of unanswered questions and answer them openly in the next update, since rumours often express employees' real concerns.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Cheerleading instead of informing.** "Exciting news" with no specifics is limited information by another name, the condition that did worse in the original experiment.[^s1]
- **Waiting until everything is decided.** Silence while details are settled is when rumours take hold; say what is open and when it will close.[^s4]
- **Leaving out the bad news.** A preview that hides known losses stops being realistic, and the honesty signal is what such previews depend on.[^s3]
- **One message, then nothing.** The original programme ran over time, alongside repeated measurement; a single briefing is not a preview.[^s1]
- **Ignoring the grapevine.** Negative rumours track stress, so dismissing them rather than answering them leaves the underlying worries in place.[^s5]

## Evidence

The evidence is promising but thin, so the status here is mixed. The core support is one longitudinal field experiment comparing two plants in a single company, a rare and strong design for this topic but not one that has been widely replicated.[^s1] The related realistic job preview literature is much larger, and a 1998 meta-analysis found reliable links to lower voluntary turnover and better performance, moderated by timing and medium.[^s2] A 2020 umbrella summary of the job-preview meta-analyses, though, describes the effects as consistently modest and attributes them partly to employees seeing the organisation as honest.[^s3] The rumour and uncertainty studies are interview, case-study and survey research rather than experiments.[^s4][^s5] The fair conclusion is that honest, specific, repeated communication is unlikely to hurt and probably helps, but it will not offset a change that people see as unfair.

## Connections

The realistic merger preview is a technique **applied in** communicating organisational change and sits under it as a narrower practice; it **requires** audience analysis, because each group needs its own answers.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by** Schweiger and DeNisi's 1991 field experiment.[^s1] It **addresses** workplace rumours, which spread when change communication is poor and uncertainty is high,[^s4] and which tend to be negative and stressful.[^s5] It **complements** communicating uncertainty, since much of a good preview is saying clearly what is not yet known and when it will be.[^s4] It is related to the **realistic job preview**, which applies the same honest-preview logic to recruitment,[^s2][^s3] and to delivering bad news and trust repair when the merger brings losses or follows a breach of trust.[^s1][^s4]
