# Expectation-disconfirmation theory

Expectation-disconfirmation theory explains satisfaction as a comparison. Before an experience, people hold expectations; afterwards, they judge how the result compared with them. Doing better than expected is *positive disconfirmation*, doing worse is *negative disconfirmation*, and satisfaction follows from that comparison together with the expectation itself.[^s1] For anyone who delivers work to a client, a sponsor or a boss, the theory's practical claim is that your communication before and during the work - the estimates, promises and caveats you give - becomes part of the yardstick your result is measured against.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Richard L. Oliver set out the model in a 1980 *Journal of Marketing Research* paper. He proposed that satisfaction is a function of expectation level and of disconfirmation, and that satisfaction in turn shapes later attitudes and purchase intentions; a two-stage field study of people who did and did not get a flu inoculation supported the model.[^s1] Marketing researchers adopted it as the dominant account of consumer satisfaction, and public-administration scholars later used it to explain citizens' satisfaction with public services.[^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

Workplace satisfaction is rarely judged against an absolute standard. A two-week delivery reads as fine if the client was told two weeks and as a failure if they heard "a few days" in a hallway. Meta-analytic evidence shows that the felt gap between expectation and result tracks satisfaction closely: across 168 studies and about 58,600 participants, disconfirmation correlated with satisfaction at about r = .61, almost as strongly as perceived performance itself (r = .63).[^s2] That is why explicit, accurate expectations - in a kickoff, a status report or a request to your manager - are part of the deliverable, not a courtesy.[^s2]

## How to use it

- **Set the expectation explicitly and early.** Give dates, scope and quality levels in writing before the work starts, so the comparison happens against what you said rather than what the client imagined.[^s1]
- **Re-set it as soon as it changes.** Because a shortfall against the expectation drives dissatisfaction, tell people when the forecast moves rather than letting them discover the gap at delivery.[^s2]
- **Promise what you can keep, then keep it.** In Gneezy and Epley's experiments, breaking a promise was judged clearly worse than keeping it, while exceeding a promise earned no extra credit, even when the extra effort was real.[^s4] Reliability, not over-delivery, is what the comparison rewards.[^s4][^s5]
- **Don't sandbag.** Deliberately low estimates are not a free win: in the 2025 meta-analysis, higher expectations went with *higher* satisfaction, not lower, so talking a client's expectations down can drag their judgement of the work down with it.[^s2]

**Example.** A team lead tells a client a report will arrive "end of next week, possibly Wednesday if the data is clean". The data is messy, Wednesday slips, and the client - anchored on Wednesday - feels let down by a Friday delivery that was on time. Saying "Friday 5pm; I'll tell you on Tuesday if that changes" gives one firm commitment to keep and a checkpoint for re-setting it.[^s2][^s4]

## Evidence

Support is solid for the core link between disconfirmation and satisfaction. Van Ryzin's 2013 experiment on citizen satisfaction has been replicated: Chen and colleagues ran two narrow experimental replications, in Hong Kong (2017) and Shenzhen (2021), and found support for the original results outside Western settings.[^s3]

The details of the mechanism are in dispute. Schiebler, Lee and Brodbeck's 2025 meta-analysis found that expectations correlated *positively* with satisfaction (r = .29 overall, r = .14 in experiments) and with disconfirmation, the opposite of the negative relation the theory predicts. They found no indirect effect of expectations on satisfaction through disconfirmation, noted that disconfirmation and satisfaction overlap so much that they may not be cleanly distinct measures, and reported little evidence for contrast effects.[^s2] Effects were stronger for services than for goods and in surveys than in experiments.[^s2] The popular corollary "under-promise and over-deliver" also outruns the evidence: Gneezy and Epley's studies suggest people overvalue kept promises rather than reward exceeded ones.[^s4] Most of this research concerns consumers and citizens, not long-running professional relationships, so treat it as a strong guide to *why* expectation-setting matters rather than a precise formula.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

The theory **was introduced by** Richard L. Oliver and **demonstrated by** his 1980 flu-inoculation study,[^s1] and is **studied in** services marketing.[^s2] It is **supported by** Chen and colleagues' 2022 replication,[^s3] and **challenged by** Schiebler, Lee and Brodbeck's 2025 meta-analysis on the mechanism and by Gneezy and Epley's 2014 promise experiments on the over-delivery corollary.[^s2][^s4] It **is applied in** client communication, where it explains why commitments shape how work is judged.[^s5] It sits next to service recovery (what to do after negative disconfirmation), status reporting and delivering bad news (re-setting expectations), communicating uncertainty and saying no (making commitments you can keep), managing up, Anderson and Sullivan's 1993 satisfaction study, and anchoring, which helps explain why stated expectations colour judgements of results.[^s2][^s4]
