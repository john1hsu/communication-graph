  - Finish the report first, then name the one decision or action you want from the reader.
  - Open with the recommendation or main finding in one or two sentences, before any background.
  - State the problem and what is at stake for this reader in concrete terms such as cost, risk, time or customers.

# Executive summary

An executive summary is the short section at the front of a report, proposal or business case that lets a busy decision maker grasp the problem, the findings and the recommended action without reading the full document.[^s3] Writing guides describe it as a condensed version of the whole that the reader may rely on alone, which is why its facts must be exact and its recommendation explicit.[^s4] Its sizing rule of thumb is about 5-10% of the full report, often one or two pages.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

The genre grew out of management and government reporting, where the people who decide are rarely the people who did the analysis. A well-known codification is the Canadian Health Services Research Foundation's "1:3:25" rule: one page of main messages, a three-page executive summary, and no more than 25 pages of findings, written for intelligent decision makers who are not researchers and have little time.[^s1] The Foundation told authors to write the summary like a news story, with the most important findings first and background after, and to keep methods to a line or two.[^s1] Health-policy researchers later built on this "graded-entry" idea, putting key messages on the front page and fuller detail behind them.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Senior readers triage. Many will read the summary and decide whether to read further, delegate or act, so the summary often *is* the report for its most important audience.[^s1][^s4] Government plain-language guidance makes the same point for any document of four or more pages: start with a summary and put the most important ideas first.[^s5] A summary that narrates what the team did, instead of stating findings and recommendations, fails the reader who needs to decide on a policy, action or expenditure.[^s3]

## What works here

- **Lead with the answer.** Put the recommendation or main finding in the opening lines; background and method follow, if at all.[^s1][^s3]
- **Write in the reader's currency.** Executives care about costs, productivity, risk and customers; frame the problem and benefit in those terms, with specific figures rather than generalities.[^s3][^s4]
- **Make it stand alone.** Use the same terms as the report, include milestones and dates, and don't introduce anything the report doesn't cover.[^s3][^s4]
- **End with a specific ask.** Close with a clear recommendation, what decision is needed and by when, justified by points already made in the summary.[^s4]
- **Write it last, test it with a real reader.** Draft after the report is finished, then try it on someone in the target audience to see what they find useful.[^s1][^s4]

## Pitfalls

- **Writing an academic abstract.** An abstract summarises purpose, method and results for peers; an executive summary is longer, practical and oriented to action, and should not be cryptic or method-heavy.[^s1][^s3]
- **Copy-pasting sentences from the report** instead of rewriting them for the summary's purpose.[^s3]
- **Vague claims** ("significant savings") where the reader needs the number.[^s3][^s4]
- **Hiding problems.** Glossing over limitations erodes trust when the reader finds them later; name them and say how they are handled.[^s3]
- **Jargon and technical detail.** In user testing of policy summaries, decision makers struggled with technical concepts and wanted shorter, more scannable text.[^s2]

**Channel and seniority.** In email, the summary becomes the first paragraph of the message; on slides, a single "summary" slide at the start; for a board or client, an even shorter key-messages box may sit above the summary, as in 1:3:25.[^s1][^s5]

**Evidence and limits.** Most guidance on executive summaries is practitioner consensus rather than experimental evidence. The closest formal testing is user research on graded-entry evidence summaries: in 2011, Rosenbaum and colleagues ran think-aloud tests with 18 policy makers in five countries (plus three pilots); participants liked having key messages first, and 16 said such a summary would help a real decision, mainly because it was concise.[^s2] Those tests were small, qualitative and in health policy, so they support the format's usefulness rather than measure its effect on decisions.[^s2]

## Connections

An executive summary is **part of** a longer business report; the 1:3:25 format treats it as the middle layer between key messages and the full findings.[^s1] It **contrasts with** the academic abstract, which is shorter, aimed at specialists and not built around a recommendation.[^s3] It **complements** plain language: the 1:3:25 guidance asks for language and examples that administrators and officials would recognise, not academic phrasing.[^s1] The graded-entry format it relies on was **demonstrated** in user testing by Rosenbaum et al. (2011).[^s2] Techniques such as bottom line up front and the pyramid principle give it structure, it is the written counterpart of an executive briefing, and a project status report often opens with one. Writing a good one requires audience analysis, and it is the main remedy for burying a report's conclusion in a wall of text.[^s3][^s5]
