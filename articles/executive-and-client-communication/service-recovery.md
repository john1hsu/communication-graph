steps:
  - Thank the client for raising it and listen to the whole complaint before defending, explaining or offering anything; then restate the problem and its impact on them in your own words.
  - Apologise for the specific failure if the complaint is legitimate, without arguing about fault in the first reply.
  - Ask what outcome would put them right, and offer a concrete fix that at least restores their position (redo, credit, refund or extra resource) - do not rely on courtesy alone.
  - Give a firm time for the next update or the fix, make it easy for them to reach one named person, and meet that time.
  - Explain plainly what went wrong and what you are changing so it does not happen again, once you actually know.
  - Close the loop - confirm with the client that the fix worked and log the cause so the team can prevent a repeat.

The assets will be live by Thursday 10am; I'll confirm personally when they are. We won't bill this month's management fee, and if you'd prefer a different remedy, tell me what would work. # Service recovery

Service recovery is what you say and do after you have let a client or customer down: a missed deadline, a wrong deliverable, a billing error, a rude exchange. Research on complaint handling treats the response as having several parts - how quickly and easily the complaint is handled, the tangible remedy, an apology, an explanation of what went wrong, and the courtesy and empathy shown along the way.[^s2] It applies as much to an internal service team answering a business unit, or a consultant answering a client sponsor, as to a shop answering a customer.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

The term was popularised for managers by Christopher Hart, James Heskett and Earl Sasser in their 1990 *Harvard Business Review* article "The Profitable Art of Service Recovery", which argued that service firms cannot prevent every mistake but can learn to recover, starting by spotting problems and acting fast, and that frontline staff need authority to fix errors even when that means bending standard rules.[^s5] Services-marketing researchers then tested the idea. Tax, Brown and Chandrashekaran (1998) drew on justice theory and showed that complainants judge the experience on three things: the outcome they get, the procedure used to get it, and how they are treated personally.[^s4] Davidow's 2003 review of about 60 studies organised the organisation's side into six response dimensions: timeliness, facilitation, redress, apology, credibility and attentiveness.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Tax and colleagues found that most customers who had complained were dissatisfied with how the complaint was handled, and that satisfaction with the handling directly shaped trust and commitment; a good history with the firm cushioned bad handling only a little.[^s4] For client work, that means a mishandled complaint can do more damage to the relationship than the original slip. Davidow's review also notes that staff attitudes often decide whether a failure turns into a second failure.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Cover all three things the client is judging. **Outcome:** offer a remedy that at least leaves them no worse off, and ask what they would consider fair, because more compensation does not reliably produce more satisfaction.[^s2] **Procedure:** make it easy to raise the problem, agree when they will hear back, and keep to it; Davidow found that a properly investigated answer matters more than an instant one.[^s2] **Treatment:** listen, show you understand the cost to them, apologise for a legitimate complaint, and explain what happened and what will change.[^s2] In Davidow's own model, attentiveness - courtesy, empathy and respect - had the largest effect on satisfaction and was the only dimension that reduced negative word of mouth, while attentiveness and credibility drove intentions to come back.[^s2]

The dimensions work together. A generous remedy delivered rudely, or warm treatment with no real fix, can both backfire.[^s2] A 2011 meta-analysis of 87 articles (95 samples, about 28,800 complainants) found that compensation drove satisfaction with the specific complaint most strongly, but that respectful employee behaviour (interactional justice) was slightly the stronger driver of overall satisfaction with the firm, and mattered most for non-monetary complaints and in service settings.[^s1] For professional services, where the failure is usually a missed commitment rather than a faulty product, the treatment and the explanation carry a lot of weight.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- **Leading with an explanation that shifts blame.** Explanations raise credibility only when they show what went wrong and what will prevent a repeat.[^s2]
- **Sympathy without a remedy, or a remedy without sympathy.** Both leave one justice dimension unmet.[^s1][^s2]
- **Over-compensating instead of asking.** Ask what would make the client whole; bigger is not automatically better.[^s2]
- **Vague follow-up.** "We'll update you soon" fails the timeliness test; give a time and meet it.[^s2]
- **Counting on the "recovery paradox".** The idea that a well-handled failure leaves clients more loyal than if nothing had gone wrong is widely repeated. A 2007 meta-analysis found a positive effect on satisfaction, moderated by study design and sample, but no significant effect on repurchase intentions, word of mouth or corporate image.[^s3] Recovery limits damage; it is not a growth strategy.

## Evidence and limits

The core finding - that remedy, fair process and respectful treatment each contribute to post-complaint satisfaction - comes from meta-analytic and review evidence.[^s1][^s2] But the justice dimensions are highly intercorrelated, many studies test only some dimensions, results on apology and timeliness are mixed, and much of the work uses scenario studies and consumer settings rather than long-running business relationships.[^s1][^s2] Treat the steps as well-supported defaults, not exact weights.

## Connections

Service recovery is a form of **trust repair** that **complements** an **apology**: apology is one of Davidow's six dimensions, but recovery also needs redress, timeliness and an explanation.[^s2] It is **explained by** **organisational justice** (outcome, procedure and interpersonal treatment) and **applied in** **client communication**.[^s4] It is **supported by** Gelbrich and Roschk's meta-analysis and Davidow's review,[^s1][^s2] **challenged by** de Matos and colleagues' meta-analysis of the recovery paradox,[^s3] and was **introduced** to managers in Hart, Heskett and Sasser's HBR article.[^s5] It sits next to **delivering bad news** and **escalation**, which come before a client complains; **expectation-disconfirmation theory**, which explains why a broken commitment feels like a failure; and **active listening**, which the first step requires.[^s4]
