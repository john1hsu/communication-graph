steps:
  - Before a client call or customer shift you expect to be hard, name the display the role needs (calm, warm, patient) and the feeling you are likely to have instead.
  - Write one honest reframe that would make the needed feeling real - what the other person is under, what is at stake for them, or what a good outcome looks like for both of you.
  - When the trigger arrives, take one breath and recall the reframe before you answer, rather than fixing your face first.
  - Shift attention to something in the interaction you can genuinely engage with - the problem to solve, a detail you are curious about, a part that is going well.
  - If the feeling will not shift, do not keep forcing a smile; say a short, neutral sentence that is true ("Let me make sure I understand the impact"), and change the situation if you can - a pause, a follow-up time, a handover.
  - Afterwards, note which reframes worked and which interactions still needed faking, and raise the recurring ones with your manager as a workload or process issue.
example:
  situation: An account manager joins a renewal call with a client who has escalated the same reporting defect three times and opens by saying the vendor "clearly doesn't care".
  before: "She fixes a bright smile, says \"We absolutely care - we're so sorry!\" in a cheerful voice, and keeps the smile while inwardly resenting the accusation; the client hears it as scripted, and she ends the call drained and unable to recall the deadline he set."

When he says it, she means it when she replies: \"Three escalations is too many, and I can hear it's put you in a tough spot internally. Tell me what your board needs to see by when.\" She writes the date down."

Reappraising why the client is angry makes the concern she shows real, so her warmth reads as sincere and she stays engaged enough to capture the commitment.

# Deep acting

Deep acting is meeting a role's expectations for how you come across by changing the feeling itself rather than only its outward signs. A support lead who stays calm with an abusive caller because she has reframed the call, or a consultant who is warm with a demanding client because he has focused on what the client is under, is deep acting. Its opposite, surface acting, means changing your facial expression and voice while the inner feeling stays the same - faking it.[^s3][^s6] Researchers describe deep acting as the route to displays that look sincere, and surface acting as the route that more often costs the employee.[^s6]

## Origin

Sociologist Arlie Russell Hochschild introduced the distinction in *The Managed Heart* (1983). She coined "emotional labor" for managing feeling as part of paid work, and separated surface acting from deep acting, which she also called emotion work. She warned that the resulting estrangement from one's own feelings was an occupational hazard.[^s1][^s5] In 2000 Alicia Grandey linked the two forms to James Gross's model of emotion regulation: deep acting resembles antecedent-focused regulation, which changes how a situation is perceived, and surface acting is response-focused, adjusting the expression after the emotion has formed.[^s4] Later meta-analysts used the same mapping. They define deep acting as reappraising the situation or redirecting attention, and surface acting as suppressing, amplifying or faking expressions.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Client- and customer-facing roles come with display rules: be pleasant, stay composed, look interested. How you meet them shows up in how others experience you. In Grandey's 2003 study, coworkers rated the service delivery of employees who used deep acting as better, and that of employees who used surface acting as worse. Surface acting, but not deep acting, was linked to emotional exhaustion.[^s3] A 2011 meta-analysis of 95 independent studies found the same split. Surface acting had substantial links to impaired well-being and poorer job attitudes and a small negative link to performance. Deep acting was only weakly related to well-being and attitudes, but positively related to emotional performance and customer satisfaction (corrected correlations of about .18 and .37).[^s2] Faking a smile also leaves the feeling in place, which is why the expressive-suppression costs of strain and lost attention follow it into the conversation.[^s4]

## How to apply it

The two strategies research treats as deep acting are cognitive change and attentional deployment.[^s2][^s5] In practice:

- **Reappraise before you react.** Decide in advance what a hard moment means: an angry client is worried about their own exposure, a blunt executive is short of time. Then the warmth or patience you show is something you actually feel.[^s2][^s5]
- **Take the other person's perspective.** Experience-sampling work counts perspective-taking and positive refocusing as deep-acting tactics, and both drop on unpleasant days, so plan them before a hard call rather than counting on them in the moment.[^s4]
- **Refocus attention** on something you can engage with honestly, such as the problem, a detail you are curious about, or the part that is working.[^s2]
- **Practise it as a skill.** In a 2015 study, German hairdressers trained in these strategies received significantly more tips than an untrained group. In a Dutch diary study of waiters and taxi drivers, days with more deep acting brought more tips.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as a better mask.** Deep acting changes the feeling. Smiling harder over resentment is still surface acting, and coworkers rate it as worse service.[^s3]
- **Assuming it is free.** Deep acting takes effort and draws on mental resources. The meta-analysts expected the drain and the gains to roughly cancel out for well-being, and found only weak effects.[^s2]
- **Using it to excuse a bad situation.** Grandey and Sayre caution that repeatedly deceiving yourself, without changing the situation, wears people down. Where clients are routinely abusive, the fix belongs in the work design (support, breaks, escalation routes), not only in each employee's head.[^s6]
- **Forcing it when it is not working.** If no honest reframe comes, a short true sentence and a change to the situation, such as a pause or a handover, beats a forced display.[^s6][^s4]

## Evidence

The performance benefits are the best-supported part: deep acting relates positively to customer satisfaction and rated service across many studies, and one field intervention increased tips.[^s2][^s3][^s5] The status here is **mixed** for three reasons. Most studies are cross-sectional and self-reported. The 2015 diary study had a sample of 34 employees and a 16% response rate, and an earlier reappraisal training study found no effects on exhaustion, job satisfaction or customer satisfaction.[^s5] Grandey and Gabriel also warn that common deep-acting scales are confounded with motivation, so a high score may partly mean an employee who cares rather than one who uses a particular strategy. And lab research suggests suppression can change visible displays more reliably than reappraisal does.[^s4] Well-being benefits are weak at best.[^s2]

## Connections

Deep acting is a form of emotion regulation (`cm:emotion-regulation`) within emotional labor (`cm:emotional-labor`), and it **contrasts with** surface acting (`cm:surface-acting`), the faking strategy measured beside it in Grandey's 2003 study (`cm:grandey-2003`), which it is **demonstrated by**.[^s3] It was **introduced by** Arlie Russell Hochschild (`cm:arlie-russell-hochschild`) **in** *The Managed Heart* (`cm:the-managed-heart`).[^s1] It is **explained by** the process model of emotion regulation (`cm:process-model-of-emotion-regulation`) as an antecedent-focused strategy, is **applied in** client communication (`cm:client-communication`), and is **supported by** the Hülsheger and Schewe meta-analysis (`cm:hulsheger-schewe-2011`).[^s2] It **addresses** expressive suppression (`cm:expressive-suppression`), which Grandey and Gabriel equate with surface acting, and their review also **challenges** how deep acting is measured (`cm:grandey-gabriel-2015`).[^s4] The tips diary and training studies (`cm:hulsheger-et-al-2015`) **demonstrate** its payoff in service work.[^s5] Cognitive reappraisal (`cm:cognitive-reappraisal`) and perspective-taking (`cm:perspective-taking`) are the strategies it draws on. Labeling emotions (`cm:labeling-emotions`), emotional contagion (`cm:emotional-contagion`), workplace stress (`cm:workplace-stress`) and the difficult conversation (`cm:difficult-conversation`) are neighbouring topics for anyone managing what they show at work.[^s4][^s6]
