# Sensegiving

Sensegiving is the deliberate work of influencing how other people understand a situation - a reorganisation, a new strategy, a crisis, a client's shifting priorities - by offering them a way to interpret it. It is the outward-facing partner of sensemaking: a leader first works out what a change means, then tries to shape the meaning others take from it.[^s1] Researchers treat it as something anyone can do, not only executives; stakeholders and middle managers give sense upward, sideways and to clients as well as down the line.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Dennis Gioia and Kumar Chittipeddi named the concept in a 1991 *Strategic Management Journal* study that followed the first year of a strategic change at a large public university using ethnographic methods. They described the president's role as cycles of making sense of the situation and giving sense to others, moving through four phases: envisioning, signalling, re-visioning and energising.[^s1] Sally Maitlis and Thomas Lawrence later asked when sensegiving happens at all. In a longitudinal study they found that both leaders and stakeholders start giving sense when they notice, or expect, a gap in how the organisation is making sense of an issue, and that two things let them do it: discursive ability (building and conveying a persuasive account) and process facilitators such as meetings, routines and structures that give them the time and opportunity.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Announcing a decision does not decide what it means. When leaders leave a gap, rumours and worst-case readings fill it; Kraft, Sparr and Peus's interviews with 55 leaders and employees who had lived through radical changes found that uncertainty and rumour dominate the early phase, and that employees make sense of change at different speeds and in different ways.[^s6] Much of the meaning-making happens in everyday conversations rather than in the launch deck: Linda Rouleau's 2005 study of middle managers implementing a strategic change at a fashion company showed them translating the new direction, explaining it to clients and justifying it in routine talk - work that shaped whether the change was accepted.[^s3] For anyone in an executive or client-facing role, this is the skill that turns a decision into something people can act on.

## What good looks like

Skilled sensegivers combine a clear frame with a concrete story. Logemann, Piekkari and Cornelissen's longitudinal case study of a European multinational found that managers were most effective when they used framing and narratives together, and that effective sensegiving gave staff a practical way to make sense of the change rather than a ready-made meaning to accept.[^s5] Rouleau and Balogun's study of middle managers adds that the talk itself is only half the work: they paired performing the conversation (choosing language and framing that connect to the listener's interests) with setting the scene (choosing who is in the room, which forum and which allies), using their knowledge of the organisation's codes, history and power relations.[^s4] Good sensegiving is also two-way. In Kraft and colleagues' interviews, leaders who helped most listened to concerns and traced rumours early, made themselves visibly available, opened joint discussions about what the change might offer, and later acknowledged progress and asked people what had gone well.[^s6]

## How to practise it

Before you speak, find out what sense people are already making: ask a few team members what they have heard and what worries them.[^s6] Then write the frame in one or two sentences - the problem the change solves, in the listener's terms - and attach one concrete story or example that people could retell.[^s5] Separate what is changing from what is not, and name what you do not yet know and when you will. Choose your forums deliberately: a team meeting for the headline, one-to-ones for individual concerns, and a follow-up session to revise the account once you have heard the pushback.[^s4][^s6] With clients, the same moves apply when you explain a reorganisation, a revised plan or a new contact person: Rouleau's managers rebuilt client relationships by translating the strategy into what it meant for each customer.[^s3] Over the following weeks, acknowledge progress publicly and ask what is working, because sensemaking continues long after the announcement.[^s6]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence base is mostly qualitative: ethnographies, longitudinal case studies and interview studies, often in a single organisation.[^s1][^s3][^s5] These studies are rich and consistent about the moves involved - perceived sensemaking gaps triggering sensegiving, discursive skill and forums enabling it, and framing plus narrative working better than either alone - but they cannot give effect sizes, and no meta-analysis tests whether trained sensegiving improves change outcomes.[^s2][^s5] Kraft and colleagues' framework comes from retrospective interviews.[^s6] There is also an ethical line. The most effective managers in Logemann and colleagues' study offered people a workable way to interpret the change rather than a finished meaning to swallow, which is a useful test against spin: an account that would not survive employees or clients comparing it with what actually happens is likely to cost you credibility rather than build shared understanding.[^s5]

## Connections

Sensegiving was introduced by Dennis Gioia with Kumar Chittipeddi in their 1991 study, which also defined its partner process, sensemaking; the two run in cycles, so each complements the other.[^s1] Maitlis and Lawrence's 2007 study demonstrates when and how leaders and stakeholders engage in it.[^s2] It sits under organisational communication and is a core skill within communicating organisational change, where Kraft and colleagues show it applied across phases of change and in manager one-on-one meetings.[^s6] It complements narrative persuasion, since the most effective sensegivers in Logemann and colleagues' study combined framing with stories, and it is related to gain-loss framing.[^s5] Middle managers' upward and lateral sensegiving overlaps with issue selling, managing up and stakeholder mapping.[^s3][^s4] It requires audience analysis, because a frame only works if it connects to what the listener cares about.[^s4]
