# Communicating organisational change

Communicating organisational change is the situation in which leaders tell people about a reorganisation, merger, new system, restructuring or downsizing and help them make sense of it. The message has to do more than announce: Armenakis and Harris argue that a change message builds readiness when it shows there is a real gap between where the organisation is and where it needs to be, that this particular change is the right response, that people can succeed at it, that leaders back it, and what is in it for those affected.[^s1] A 2011 review of 79 quantitative studies from 1948 to 2007 found that how a change is communicated is one of the process factors that shapes whether people accept or resist it.[^s3]

## Origin

Research on communicating change grew from organisational psychology and organisational communication. An early landmark is Schweiger and DeNisi's 1991 field experiment, in which one plant of a merging company received a "realistic merger preview" - realistic information about what the merger would mean for them - while another received limited information; the preview reduced the harmful after-effects of the announcement, and some attitudes returned to pre-announcement levels.[^s2] In 1995, John Kotter's *Harvard Business Review* article on why transformations fail listed under-communicating the vision, by roughly a factor of ten, among the common errors, which made frequent communication a staple of practitioner change models.[^s5] Armenakis and Harris later set out the five message components (discrepancy, appropriateness, efficacy, principal support and personal valence) and three ways to convey them: active participation, persuasive communication and managing information.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Change creates uncertainty, and silence fills with guesses. The 2011 review linked realistic, supportive communication to greater acceptance of change, lower anxiety and uncertainty, more trust in management and lower intentions to quit; participation in the change had similar benefits, and trust in management was among the strongest predictors of how people reacted.[^s3] The costs of getting it wrong are visible in a study of a health-care organisation that cut staff from 660 to 350 over two years: people who stayed and people who left lost trust in management to a similar degree, and non-managers and middle managers reported receiving less information than senior managers on every measure.[^s4]

## What works here

- **Answer "why" before "what".** Lead with the gap the change closes and why this option was chosen over others, then what happens and when.[^s1]
- **Make it personal.** Tell each group what changes for them, what does not, and what support they will get; this is the personal valence component.[^s1]
- **Be realistic, early and repeated.** A realistic preview of what the change means, rather than limited information, reduced harmful after-effects in a merger field experiment.[^s2] Plan to repeat the core message many times across channels.[^s5]
- **Say what is not yet known** and when the next update will come, since unaddressed uncertainty undermines implementation.[^s3]
- **Close the information gap down the hierarchy.** Brief middle managers before or alongside their teams, and check that front-line staff get the same substance senior leaders get.[^s4]
- **Invite participation**, not just questions: people who helped shape the details showed more readiness and acceptance.[^s3]

A workplace example: a finance director announcing a move to a shared-services centre might open with the cost and service gap the move closes, explain why the in-house options were rejected, say which roles move and on what date, admit that team locations will not be settled until March, and name the working group in which staff can shape the handover. The same plan announced as "exciting news about our new operating model" with details "to follow" leaves people to fill the gaps with rumour.[^s1][^s3]

## Pitfalls

Common failures are a single big announcement with no follow-up, spin that hides the losses people can already see, a vision that is never translated into what changes on Monday, and briefing senior managers well while everyone else learns from the grapevine.[^s4][^s5] More information is not automatically better: the 2011 review notes cases where more information went with more negative evaluations, so the content and honesty of the message matter as much as the volume.[^s3] Messages cannot make up for a change process that people see as unfair or managers they already distrust.[^s3]

## Evidence

The evidence base is broad but uneven, so the status here is mixed. The 2011 review covers six decades of studies, but its authors note that few studies collected data from more than one source and that effect sizes were not pooled into a meta-analysis.[^s3] Schweiger and DeNisi's realistic merger preview is a rare field experiment, but it compared just two plants in one company.[^s2] The downsizing study is a cross-sectional survey of one organisation.[^s4] Kotter's factor-of-ten claim comes from consulting experience rather than measurement.[^s5] The five-component message framework is drawn from case work and theory rather than controlled trials.[^s1]

## Connections

Communicating change sits under **organisational communication** and requires **audience analysis**, because each group affected needs its own answer to "what does this mean for me?".[^s1] It is **demonstrated by** Schweiger and DeNisi's 1991 realistic merger preview[^s2] and by Tourish, Hobman and Bordia's 2004 downsizing study of information gaps,[^s4] and **supported by** Oreg, Vakola and Armenakis's 2011 review linking communication and participation to acceptance of change.[^s3] It was **influenced by** John Kotter's account of why transformations fail.[^s5] It overlaps with **delivering bad news** and the **layoff conversation** when the change costs people their jobs,[^s4] with **communicating uncertainty** when details are not settled,[^s3] and with **trust repair** when trust in management has already been damaged.[^s3][^s4]
