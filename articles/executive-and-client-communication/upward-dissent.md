# Upward dissent

Upward dissent is disagreement voiced to the people above you - a manager, director or senior client sponsor - about a decision, policy or practice they can act on.[^s1] As a skill, it means raising that disagreement so it is actually considered: with evidence, a proposed alternative and a framing the listener cares about, rather than as a complaint, an end-run or a threat.[^s2][^s6]

## Origin

Communication scholar Jeffrey W. Kassing, at Arizona State University, built a research programme on employee dissent around the question of who hears it.[^s1] In a 2002 study with Todd Armstrong he distinguished three audiences: dissent expressed upward to supervisors and managers, laterally to coworkers, and outside the organisation to family and friends.[^s1] Only the first reaches someone with the power to change things. Kassing then compared the strategies people use upward: a 2005 survey asked employees how competent each one appeared, and a 2009 study of 190 employees followed what happens when the same issue has to be raised three or more times.[^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

Disagreement that never goes upward does not disappear; it moves sideways or out of the building, where it cannot fix anything.[^s1] Kassing and Armstrong found employees voiced dissent to supervisors more readily about coworker issues and decisions or change than about ethics or harm to employees, the very issues leaders most need to hear early.[^s1] Speaking up also carries real risk. In a field study and two experiments, Burris (2012) found that managers rated employees who challenged the status quo as weaker performers, and were less likely to endorse their ideas, than employees whose voice supported existing practice; perceived threat and perceived loyalty explained the difference.[^s4] The skill is therefore about being heard without being written off.

## What good looks like

Kassing's respondents ranked the upward strategies clearly. Presenting a solution was judged the most competent, followed by a direct, fact-based appeal, then repetition and circumvention (going over or around the boss); threatening to resign was judged least competent.[^s2] Good upward dissent leads with the first two. Detert's synthesis of research on workplace courage adds how to deliver it: connect the point to the organisation's priorities and the decision maker's concerns, back it with data and quantified alternatives, expect some defensiveness as a normal reaction, and keep returning calmly to the evidence.[^s6] It is also selective - reserved for issues that matter to your goals and values, raised when there is urgency or an opening for it to be heard.[^s6]

Escalation of tactics is the pattern to avoid. When supervisors delayed or responded badly to repeated dissent, Kassing's participants drifted from facts and solutions toward circumvention and resignation threats, and moved faster when the response was unfavourable.[^s3]

## How to practise it

1. **Choose the issue.** Ask whether it matters enough to spend credibility on; Detert describes that credibility as earned through competence and even-handedness over time.[^s6]
2. **Prepare the case.** Gather the facts, state the risk in the decision maker's terms, and bring at least one alternative.[^s2][^s6]
3. **Go to the right person first, privately.** Upward dissent targets whoever can act, before the issue spreads laterally.[^s1]
4. **Pick the moment.** Look for urgency, new allies or a shift in leadership attention; avoid moments right after a previous challenge.[^s6]
5. **Manage the room.** Acknowledge the other person's view, do not take emotional bait, and return to the data.[^s6]
6. **Repeat constructively.** If you must raise it again, change the evidence or the option rather than the pressure.[^s3]
7. **Follow up.** Thank supporters, share credit, and repair any relationship that felt attacked, whatever the decision.[^s6]

Managers have a part too: address concerns early, provide clear channels for disagreement, and step up efforts when an employee's way of raising an issue suddenly changes, because that change often signals frustration.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but bounded. Kassing's findings come from employee self-reports and perceptions (the 2009 sample was drawn from Arizona organisations), so they show how strategies are judged rather than which ones change decisions.[^s2][^s3] The broader voice literature points the same way with more scale: a 2017 meta-analysis found that promotive voice (proposing improvements) was positively associated with rated job performance while prohibitive voice (flagging harmful practices) showed a negative association, and that many antecedents differ between the two forms.[^s5] That fits Burris's experiments, and it means even skilful dissent can cost the speaker; framing a concern around a constructive alternative reduces but does not remove that risk.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Upward dissent is a specific form of managing up: disagreeing well is part of keeping a productive relationship with a boss.[^s6] It complements issue selling, since both depend on framing a concern in leaders' priorities, but issue selling seeks attention for a new issue while dissent contests a decision already made.[^s6] Circumvention differs from a joint, transparent escalation: Kassing found going around the boss was rated less competent than facts or solutions.[^s2] The response a dissenter gets shapes whether others keep speaking, linking it to psychological safety and to employee silence.[^s3][^s5] It requires audience analysis, because the case has to be framed in the decision maker's own priorities.[^s6] The research is grounded in organizational communication, where Kassing's studies appear.[^s1]
