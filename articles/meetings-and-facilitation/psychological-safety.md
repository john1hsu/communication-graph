# Psychological safety

Psychological safety is a team's shared belief that speaking up is safe: that nobody will be embarrassed, rejected or punished for asking a "basic" question, flagging an error or challenging the plan.[^s1] It is a property of the group rather than a personality trait, and it is built - or eroded - in ordinary interactions, which is why meetings are where it is most visible.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Edgar Schein and Warren Bennis wrote in 1965 that people need psychological safety before they can feel secure enough to change; Amy Edmondson credits them and Argyris and Schön's work on interpersonal threat as the roots of her construct.[^s1] Edmondson defined team psychological safety in a 1999 *Administrative Science Quarterly* study of 51 teams (427 members) at a 5,000-person office-furniture manufacturer, combining observation of team meetings, interviews and surveys of members and outside observers.[^s1] Google's Project Aristotle later brought the term into mainstream management after its study of 180 internal teams.[^s3]

## Why it matters

In low-safety teams people quietly calculate the cost of speaking: they hold back admissions of error or requests for help to avoid looking incompetent.[^s1] Edmondson's interviewees described colleagues guessing what the leader wanted to hear before voicing a view, and teams whose real discussion happened in the hallway after the meeting rather than in it.[^s1] That silence is a communication failure with business cost: doubts about a project's goal went unspoken in one product team, and problems surfaced late.[^s1] In Project Aristotle, how a team worked together mattered more than who was on it, and psychological safety ranked first of five team dynamics, ahead of dependability, structure and clarity, meaning and impact.[^s3]

## How to build it in meetings

The guidance below comes from Edmondson's findings that leader coaching and a supportive context predicted safety, and from Google's manager guidance; it is practitioner advice rather than separately tested interventions.[^s1][^s3]

- **Frame the work as a learning problem.** Open with what is uncertain and why every view is needed, not only with the plan to execute.[^s3][^s4]
- **Go first on fallibility.** Name something you got wrong or don't know; it lowers the price of the next admission.[^s3]
- **Ask, then wait.** Model curiosity with genuine questions and invite quieter members by name or round-robin before you give your own view.[^s3]
- **Respond productively.** Thank the person who brings bad news and move to the problem; a sigh or put-down teaches the whole room to stay quiet.[^s1][^s4]
- **Keep the standard.** Safety does not mean agreeing with every idea or dropping accountability; you can still decline a proposal or address poor work.[^s4]

**Example.** A project lead opens a status meeting with "We're on track, right?" and gets nods; the integration slip is mentioned to her afterwards in the corridor. The next week she opens with "Integration is our biggest unknown - what's one thing that could make us miss the date? I'll start: I underestimated the vendor's test cycle." Two engineers raise a data-mapping issue in the room, while there is still time to fix it.[^s1][^s3]

## Common misconceptions

Edmondson and Kerrissey (2025) argue the term is often misread as being nice, being comfortable, or being shielded from layoffs or consequences.[^s4] Psychological safety is about candour: productive disagreement can feel uncomfortable even in a safe team, and policies or mandates alone do not create it - groups build it interaction by interaction, at any level of the organisation.[^s4] Edmondson also separated it from group cohesiveness, which can suppress dissent in the way groupthink does; a psychologically safe team is one where people feel free to challenge each other.[^s1]

## Evidence

The core association is well documented. In Edmondson's 1999 study, psychological safety predicted team learning behaviour (seeking feedback, discussing errors, experimenting), both self-reported and observer-rated, and learning behaviour mediated its link to team performance; team efficacy did not predict learning once safety was controlled for.[^s1] A 2017 meta-analytic review by Frazier and colleagues pooled 136 independent samples (over 22,000 individuals and nearly 5,000 groups) to test its antecedents, whether it predicts task performance and citizenship behaviour beyond related constructs such as leader relations and engagement, and whether design and national culture moderate the results.[^s2] Limits remain: much of the evidence is correlational and survey-based, and Google's own write-up notes subjective ratings, heavy reliance on self-report and findings from a single company.[^s1][^s3] There is also a documented downside. Pearsall and Ellis (2011) found, in 126 student teams, that high psychological safety strengthened the link between a utilitarian team outlook and cheating; the authors note the low stakes of the task.[^s5][^s6] We therefore rate the evidence `mixed`: a real, consistently observed effect on speaking up and learning, whose payoff depends on the team's goals and norms.

## Connections

Psychological safety was introduced as a team-level construct by Amy Edmondson and demonstrated in her 1999 study, where it led to team learning behaviour.[^s1] It is often confused with group cohesiveness, which Edmondson explicitly distinguished from it.[^s1] Project Aristotle supports its importance for team effectiveness.[^s3] Pearsall and Ellis (2011) challenge any assumption that more safety is always better.[^s5] It sits under team climate and relates to defensiveness, feedback, difficult conversations and open-ended questions, the everyday behaviours through which a group learns whether candour is welcome.[^s1][^s4]
