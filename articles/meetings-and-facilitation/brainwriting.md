steps:
  - Write one specific question where everyone can see it, for example "What could cut the time it takes a new hire to ship their first change?", and say that you want quantity, not polished ideas.
  - Give each person a sheet, card stack or shared-document column and set a short timer (about five minutes) for writing ideas silently, one idea per card or line, with no talking.
  - When the timer ends, have everyone pass their sheet to the next person (or read the shared document), and ask them to add new ideas that build on, combine or twist what they read.
  - Repeat the write-and-pass rounds three to six times, stopping when the pace drops noticeably rather than at the first lull.
  - Give a few minutes of individual writing at the end so people can add ideas sparked by everything they read.
  - Post or display all ideas, cluster duplicates with the group, and only then open spoken discussion and selection (for example a private vote).
example:
  situation: A product manager has 30 minutes with eight engineers, designers and support staff to collect ideas for reducing customer onboarding time.
  before: "Let's just throw ideas out - anything goes. ... Great, Priya's idea about the setup wizard is a good one. Anyone want to build on that?"
  after: "For the next five minutes, write one idea per sticky note - no talking. Then pass your pad to your left and add to what you see. We'll do four rounds, put everything on the wall, and discuss only after that."
  why: In the open round people wait their turn, forget ideas, and the first confident idea steers the rest; silent written rounds let all eight people generate at once and still build on each other's ideas before discussion narrows the field.

  - Am I judging the session by how lively it felt, or by how many usable ideas we collected?

# Brainwriting

Brainwriting is idea generation on paper or screen instead of out loud: group members write ideas silently, then pass, post or read one another's lists and add more, and the group talks only once the ideas are collected.[^s3][^s7] It keeps the part of group brainstorming that works - exposure to other people's ideas - while removing the part that doesn't, the queue to speak.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

The best-known version, the 6-3-5 method, was introduced by German marketing specialist Bernd Rohrbach in the trade journal *Absatzwirtschaft* in the late 1960s (sources give 1968 or 1969) as an alternative to Alex Osborn's brainstorming.[^s6][^s7] In 6-3-5, six people each write three ideas in five minutes, pass the sheet on, and add three more to the sheet they receive; after six rounds the group has up to 108 ideas in about half an hour.[^s6] Psychologists later studied looser forms - members writing on slips and passing them round, or posting ideas to a shared space - under the general name brainwriting, notably Paul Paulus's group at the University of Texas at Arlington.[^s1][^s4]

## Why it matters

Spoken brainstorming feels productive but usually is not. Because only one person can talk at a time, people forget ideas or get distracted while they wait, and a few members tend to dominate.[^s4] Others hold back for fear of criticism (evaluation apprehension) or let colleagues carry the load (social loafing).[^s5] Brainwriting lets everyone generate at once, and in Paulus and Yang's 2000 experiments groups that exchanged written ideas produced more ideas than the same number of people working alone (nominal groups), both during the session and in a later solo session - the reverse of the usual finding for spoken brainstorming.[^s1] That makes it a practical default for any meeting whose job is to widen options before narrowing them.[^s3][^s4]

## How to apply it

Follow the steps above. Three design choices matter most:

- **Alternate solo and shared work.** In two studies at a high-technology company, employees who alternated short periods of individual writing with brief reviews of the group's ideas generated ideas faster than those who stayed in a group the whole time, a 71% higher rate per person per minute in the second study.[^s2][^s4] Starting with a group writing round and then switching to individual writing also worked well, because people could build on what they had read.[^s2]
- **Make people read the ideas.** Paulus and Yang found the benefit depends on members attending carefully to the shared ideas and having time to reflect on them afterwards.[^s1] Ask people to add to, combine or flip what they receive rather than just write in parallel.[^s1][^s6]
- **Match the medium to the room.** In person, use identical thick pens and sticky notes so ideas stay short, legible and anonymous; remotely, use a shared document or board with a column per person.[^s7] Because it is written and asynchronous by nature, the same rounds can run across time zones before a meeting.[^s2][^s7]

## Common mistakes

- **Talking during the writing rounds.** A comment like "ooh, good one" reintroduces evaluation and anchors the group.[^s5][^s7]
- **Stopping at the first lull.** Practitioners advise pushing past the first slowdown, which can produce unusual ideas, but ending before energy is gone.[^s7]
- **Measuring success by energy.** Participants often find silent sessions awkward at first and say they prefer talking, even though talking produces fewer ideas.[^s4] Judge the session by the ideas it produced, not by how lively it felt.[^s4]
- **Skipping convergence.** Brainwriting generates options; you still need clustering, discussion and a fair selection method.[^s7]

## Evidence and limits

The core lab result - idea exchange in writing beats both spoken groups and pooled individuals on quantity - comes from student samples.[^s1][^s5] The only workplace tests found so far are two small exploratory studies; in the first, a 37% advantage for one ordering was not statistically significant.[^s2][^s4] Heslin's 2009 review argued that lab results may not transfer automatically to organizations and listed likely boundary conditions - what the task is, who takes part, when and where it runs, and why the group is meeting - calling for field research.[^s3] He also flagged that extraverts used to verbal brainstorming may dislike the silence.[^s5] Most studies count ideas rather than judging their quality or whether they were implemented, so treat brainwriting as a well-supported way to get more ideas from more people, with mixed evidence beyond that.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

Brainwriting is an **alternative to brainstorming** (`cm:brainstorming`) designed to keep its idea sharing while avoiding its process losses.[^s3] It **addresses production blocking** (`cm:production-blocking`), because no one waits for a turn to speak, and **addresses unequal participation** (`cm:unequal-participation`), because a dominant talker cannot crowd out the page.[^s4] It was **demonstrated by Paulus and Yang (2000)** (`cm:paulus-yang-2000`) and is **supported by** the workplace studies of **Paulus et al. (2015)** (`cm:paulus-et-al-2015`), which also show it **applied in workplace meetings** (`cm:workplace-meetings`).[^s1][^s2] The 6-3-5 form was **introduced by Bernd Rohrbach** (`cm:bernd-rohrbach`).[^s6] It is a **facilitation** method for **group decision making**, related to the **nominal group technique** and **1-2-4-All**, which also start with silent individual writing, and it adapts well to **asynchronous** and **hybrid** settings.[^s2][^s7]
