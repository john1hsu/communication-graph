# Production blocking

Production blocking is the loss of ideas that happens when a group generates ideas out loud and has to take turns. Only one person can speak at a time, so everyone else is waiting: some ideas are forgotten before there is an opening, some are dropped because they no longer seem relevant, and people spend the wait listening rather than thinking.[^s1] It is the main reason a classic "call out your ideas" brainstorm usually produces fewer ideas than the same people would list working alone, and it is a format problem rather than a people problem, so the fix is to change how ideas are collected.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Social psychologists Michael Diehl and Wolfgang Stroebe, working in Germany, set out in 1987 to explain why real brainstorming groups kept losing to "nominal groups" - the pooled ideas of the same number of people working separately. They tested three candidate causes in four experiments: free riding, fear of being judged, and production blocking.[^s1] In the decisive experiment, people sat in separate rooms with a panel of lights showing who was talking; when they were told to speak only when no one else was, their output fell to about 38 to 46 ideas - even below ordinary face-to-face groups (about 56) - while people free to talk whenever they liked produced around 103, matching individuals working alone (106).[^s1] The blocking manipulation explained almost all the difference between conditions, whereas free riding had only a weak effect and evaluation apprehension did not account for the group deficit.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Most workplace idea sessions - product brainstorms, retrospectives, "any ideas?" moments in a meeting - are run as open-floor discussions, which is exactly the format that blocking hurts. In Diehl and Stroebe's first experiment, four-person groups produced only about 37-38% as many ideas as the same number of people working alone.[^s1] Hearing other people's ideas did not rescue the blocked groups, which suggests the queue itself, not distraction by others' contributions, does the damage.[^s1] A later cognitive model of idea generation explains why: producing ideas is a search through memory, and having to wait your turn interrupts both the step of calling up relevant knowledge and the step of turning it into ideas.[^s3]

## Evidence

The core experimental finding - that forced turn-taking cuts individual output sharply - comes from controlled studies and has been built into the leading theoretical account of group idea generation, which also holds that hearing others' ideas can stimulate thinking once blocking is removed.[^s1][^s3] How much of the overall brainstorming deficit blocking explains is less settled. A 1991 meta-analysis found productivity losses were largest in bigger groups, when an authority figure was present and when ideas were spoken rather than written, and judged the pattern most consistent with social explanations and only marginally consistent with procedural ones such as blocking.[^s2] The remedy most often proposed, electronic brainstorming, has its own debate: a 1992 study found that large groups typing ideas in parallel produced more unique, higher-quality ideas than they did verbally, while small groups showed little difference.[^s4] In a 1999 exchange, Dennis and Valacich argued that the gains are illusory for small groups but clear for large ones, and Pinsonneault and colleagues replied that the evidence did not yet show electronic brainstorming beats nominal groups even at large sizes.[^s5][^s6] Most of this research uses student groups and counts ideas in short sessions rather than tracking which ideas a team later adopts.[^s1][^s2]

## How to spot it

- People visibly waiting to speak, or saying "I had something, but it's gone" or "that was similar to mine".[^s1]
- A brainstorm where the captured list is short relative to the number and expertise of people in the room.[^s1][^s2]
- Larger groups producing proportionally less, and spoken sessions producing less than written ones.[^s2]
- Attendees spending the session tracking the conversation rather than developing their own ideas - the interference the cognitive model describes.[^s3]

## How to fix it

- **Remove the queue.** Start with a few minutes of silent individual writing, then pool ideas in parallel on sticky notes or a shared document; blocking disappears when everyone can contribute at once.[^s1]
- **Use the exchange after people have their own lists.** Once blocking is removed, reading others' ideas can stimulate new ones, so add a short round of reading and building on the pooled list.[^s3]
- **Go parallel and in writing for large groups.** Typed, simultaneous contribution helped large groups most in the electronic brainstorming studies; for small groups, separate individual lists merged afterwards are at least as good.[^s4][^s5]
- **Keep groups small or split them.** Losses grow with group size, so break a large room into smaller groups.[^s2]
- **Hold senior voices back.** Losses were larger with an authority figure present, so have everyone write before the most senior person shares.[^s2]

Example: a team lead running a 12-person retrospective says "What went wrong this sprint? Just shout it out" and gets six items, mostly from three people. Next sprint she gives everyone five minutes to type issues into a shared board, then has the group read the board and add to it; the list is far longer and includes items from people who rarely speak, because no one had to wait for airtime.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

Production blocking was demonstrated by Diehl and Stroebe's 1987 experiments and undermines classic spoken brainstorming.[^s1] It is explained by the search-for-ideas-in-associative-memory (SIAM) model, in which turn-taking interrupts the memory search behind idea generation.[^s3] Its role as the main cause of the brainstorming deficit is challenged in part by Mullen, Johnson and Salas's meta-analysis, which favoured social explanations.[^s2] Gallupe and colleagues' work on electronic brainstorming supports it, since removing the queue helped most where blocking should be worst.[^s4] It sits under group communication and is related to brainstorming, brainwriting, the nominal group technique and 1-2-4-All, which all begin with parallel individual work, to electronic brainstorming,[^s4][^s5] and to unequal participation, conversational turn-taking, facilitation and workplace meetings, where the same one-speaker-at-a-time constraint shapes who gets heard.[^s1][^s2]
