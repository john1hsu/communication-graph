steps:
  - Write down the leading proposal in one or two sentences, with the key assumptions and evidence it rests on, and share it with the group.
  - Name the devil's advocate (a person or small sub-team) openly, explain that the role is assigned, and rotate it so it never sticks to one person.
  - Give the advocate time before the meeting to pick the assumptions most open to challenge and to look for weak evidence, gaps and facts that point elsewhere.
  - Have the advocate present the critique to the group, and ask the proposal's owner to respond to each point on the merits rather than defend the plan as a whole.
  - Record which objections change the plan, which need more analysis, and which are answered, then decide, and label any written critique as a devil's-advocate exercise.
  - Invite anyone who genuinely disagrees to say so in their own name, because sincere dissent is more valuable than the assigned role.
example:
  situation: A product team of six is about to approve moving its customer-support tool to a new vendor; the head of support proposed it and everyone in the meeting has nodded along.
  before: "Sounds like we're all aligned on the switch. Any objections? No? Great, let's sign the contract this week."
  after: "Before we sign, Priya will play devil's advocate - that's an assigned role, not her personal view. Priya, take the three assumptions you think are weakest and make the best case against the switch on Thursday. Then Sam, as the owner, answers each point, and we decide."
  why: An open call for objections in a room that already seems agreed rarely surfaces doubts; assigning and labelling the critic role makes challenge expected rather than disloyal, and forces the owner to answer specific weaknesses before the commitment is made.

# Devil's advocacy

Devil's advocacy is a decision technique in which one person or a small sub-team is formally assigned to argue against the option a group is leaning towards: to probe its assumptions, question the evidence, expose faulty logic and point to alternatives that fit the same facts.[^s1] The aim is not to win the argument but to make the group test its plan before committing, so the proposal is either confirmed, sent back for more work, or changed and caveated.[^s1]

## Origin

The name comes from the Roman Catholic Church's Promoter of the Faith, an office Pope Sixtus V formally established in 1587, whose job was to find every unfavourable fact about a candidate for sainthood; its influence faded after canonization procedures were revised in 1983.[^s7] In everyday speech the phrase now means arguing an unpopular side for the sake of debate.[^s7] As a management technique it was studied in strategic decision-making research, usually alongside dialectical inquiry (where a full counter-plan is built and debated) and against an "expert approach" in which a single recommendation goes unchallenged; Charles Schwenk's 1990 meta-analysis pulled that literature together.[^s2] Intelligence analysts adopted it as a structured analytic technique for challenging a dominant line of thinking.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Teams that have worked on a problem for a long time tend to settle into a shared mindset in which contradictory evidence is quietly discounted.[^s1] In a meeting this shows up as a quick "any objections?" that nobody answers. Assigning the critic role turns challenge from a social risk into a job, and the evidence suggests it pays off: across studies, devil's advocacy produced better decisions than the expert approach, while neither devil's advocacy nor dialectical inquiry clearly beat the other.[^s2] In a laboratory experiment, devil's advocacy also produced higher-quality case recommendations than both dialectical inquiry and the expert approach, for groups as well as individuals.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Start by writing down the leading proposal, the key assumptions it rests on and the evidence behind it; the advocate then picks the assumptions most open to challenge, looks for weak or missing evidence, and highlights facts that point elsewhere before presenting the critique to the group.[^s1] Make the role explicit and label any written critique as a devil's-advocate exercise, so nobody mistakes it for the group's real view or the advocate's personal one.[^s1] Use it for important, hard-to-reverse calls - a vendor switch, a big hire, a launch date - rather than every routine choice.[^s1]

Channel matters. In one experiment, groups working through a computer-mediated system generated and considered more alternatives than face-to-face groups, though they needed more voting rounds to agree; groups using devil's advocacy also considered more alternatives.[^s4] For distributed teams, one practical option is to post the written critique in a shared document before the live discussion, so people can weigh it on their own first.

## Common mistakes

The biggest mistake is treating the assigned role as a substitute for real disagreement. In a two-factor experiment with 201 employees and managers, groups containing genuine dissenters were better than groups using a devil's advocate at curbing the tendency to look only for information that supports the preferred option.[^s5] A related study found that escalation of commitment to a failing choice fell when groups that already held differing preferences also used devil's advocacy - suggesting the technique works best on top of real diversity of view, not instead of it.[^s6] Other common mistakes are letting the advocate argue half-heartedly, giving the same person the role every time until they are seen as negative, and listening politely to the critique without changing, testing or caveating anything.[^s1]

## Evidence

The evidence is mixed rather than robust. Controlled experiments from the 1980s and 1990s consistently favour structured conflict over unchallenged recommendations,[^s2][^s3] but most used student or laboratory groups working on case problems, and later work on genuine versus contrived dissent shows that role-played challenge is a weaker form of the same benefit.[^s5] Treat it as a useful prompt, not a guarantee.

## Connections

Devil's advocacy is a facilitation technique applied in group decision making, where an unchallenged consensus is the common failure;[^s3] facilitation is a prerequisite for running it well. Schwenk's meta-analysis demonstrates its advantage over the expert approach and shows it is a close alternative to dialectical inquiry.[^s2] It addresses escalation of commitment, at least in groups that already hold differing views.[^s6] It is challenged by research showing that authentic dissent outperforms contrived dissent in curbing biased information search.[^s5] It is related to psychological safety and unequal participation, which decide whether sincere dissent gets voiced at all, to the nominal group technique as another structure for independent input, and to workplace meetings, where most of these decisions are made.
