# Group communication

Group communication (often called small group communication) is interaction among three or more people who are linked by a common purpose, influence one another and share a sense of being a group.[^s1] Two people form a dyad, so a group starts at three; there is no hard upper limit, but once a group passes roughly 15 to 20 members it usually stops behaving like a small group.[^s1] At work this is the communication of project teams, committees, stand-ups, steering groups and workshops. The term names both that everyday activity and the strand of communication studies that researches it.

## Origin

Systematic study of group talk took off in the late 1940s, when Robert F. Bales at Harvard built Interaction Process Analysis (IPA) and published it in a 1950 book.[^s5] IPA has trained observers sort each unit of talk into twelve categories: six task categories (giving and asking for information, opinions and suggestions) and six socio-emotional ones (positive reactions such as agreeing or relieving tension, and negative ones such as disagreeing or showing antagonism).[^s5] Bales hoped that coding many groups would reveal recurring interaction patterns that could help predict and manage how groups form and work.[^s5] The scheme is still taught in group-processes courses, where students code speech acts in live discussions and look for emerging task and socio-emotional leaders.[^s6] Today the topic is a standard unit of introductory communication textbooks, which treat it as one of the core forms of communication alongside interpersonal and public communication.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Groups are harder to communicate in than pairs, and the difficulty grows faster than the headcount: a six-person team has 15 possible one-to-one links, while a twelve-person team has 66.[^s1] Skills that work one to one still transfer, but groups add coordination problems: whose turn it is, who holds which role, and how information reaches everyone.[^s1] Structure matters too. Textbook summaries of communication-network research note that centralised patterns, such as a "wheel" in which everything goes through one hub, are faster on simple tasks, while decentralised patterns such as a "circle" cope better with complex problems.[^s1]

The most cited modern evidence that *how* a group talks affects what it achieves is Woolley and colleagues' 2010 *Science* paper. Across two studies of 699 people working in groups of two to five, a single "collective intelligence" factor predicted performance on varied tasks; it was only weakly linked to members' average or highest individual intelligence, but correlated with members' social sensitivity, how evenly conversational turns were spread, and the proportion of women in the group.[^s2] A 2021 meta-analysis by the same research network, covering 22 studies with 5,349 people in 1,356 online and face-to-face groups, reported that collaboration-process measures predicted collective intelligence about twice as well as individual member skill.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

These findings are promising but not settled. An independent 2021 meta-analysis by Rowe, Hattie and Hester found a modest link between the collective-intelligence factor and criterion performance (r = .26 across 857 groups), with very high heterogeneity, and judged the evidence mixed and preliminary: most studies were underpowered, many involved the original authors, and few controlled for members' individual intelligence.[^s3] They recommended not using the factor in practice until it is independently replicated and shown to add value beyond individual ability.[^s3] The safer practical reading is narrower: in the studies to date, groups whose conversation was more even and socially attentive tended to do better, but no one should treat a turn-taking count as a measure of a team's quality.[^s2][^s3]

## Using the field at work

The field's practical payoff is a set of questions a lead can ask about any recurring meeting or team. Is the group bigger than the task needs? The textbook advice is to limit membership to what the goal requires, because each extra person multiplies the links to manage.[^s1] Does the structure match the task? Routing everything through one person suits simple, fast work but starves complex problem-solving of cross-talk.[^s1] Is talk shared? Even if the collective-intelligence evidence is still developing, a few voices dominating cuts off information, which is why facilitators use rounds, silent writing and explicit invitations.[^s2][^s3] Is the balance between task talk and relationship talk right? Bales' split between task and socio-emotional acts is a simple lens for spotting a team that is all business and no repair, or the reverse.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Group communication sits under **communication studies**, next to **interpersonal communication**, which focuses on pairs, and **organizational communication**, which studies whole organisations.[^s1] Its central claim that interaction patterns shape outcomes is **demonstrated by Woolley et al. 2010** (`cm:woolley-et-al-2010`),[^s2] **supported by Riedl et al. 2021** (`cm:riedl-et-al-2021`),[^s4] and **challenged by the 2021 g-versus-c meta-analysis** (`cm:g-versus-c-meta-analysis-2021`), which questions how robust the collective-intelligence factor is.[^s3] The field was **influenced by Robert F. Bales** (`cm:robert-f-bales`), whose Interaction Process Analysis made group talk countable.[^s5] Narrower topics in this graph include **workplace meetings**, **facilitation**, **group decision-making**, **unequal participation** and **production blocking**, which apply these ideas to particular settings and problems.[^s1][^s2]
