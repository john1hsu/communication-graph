steps:
  - Ask the people who will attend what they need the meeting to settle, and include only items that need this whole group.
  - Write each item as a question the group must answer, not a topic label.
  - Mark each item as share, seek input or decide, and name who leads it and how many minutes it gets.
  - Use the questions to set the invite list - invite only the people needed to answer them - and cancel if you cannot write any questions.
  - Circulate the written agenda, with any pre-reading, far enough ahead for people to prepare.
  - Open the meeting by restating its purpose, follow the agenda, and end when the questions are answered.
example:
  situation: A team lead schedules a 45-minute meeting with six colleagues about next quarter's office move.
  before: "Agenda: 1. Office move update 2. Desk allocation 3. AOB"
  after: "Purpose: agree the move plan. 1. (Decide, Priya, 15 min) Which two weeks in March should we move? 2. (Seek input, Tom, 20 min) Under what conditions should we reallocate desks to hybrid staff? 3. (Share, me, 5 min) What changes for parking? Please read the floor plan before Tuesday."
  why: Questions show what counts as done, the labels tell people whether they are deciding or just listening, and the named owners and pre-reading let them arrive prepared instead of reacting cold.
selfCheck:
  - Could each attendee tell from the agenda alone what the meeting must decide and what they should prepare?
  - Is every item phrased as a question that needs this whole group, or could some be handled by email?
  - Did the agenda reach people early enough for them to actually prepare?
  - At the end, can I point to an answer for each question on the agenda?

# Meeting agendas

A meeting agenda is the written plan for a meeting: what the group needs to accomplish, in what order, led by whom. Meeting researchers classify a written agenda as a *procedural* design characteristic - one of the choices a meeting leader makes in advance that steers attendees' attention and effort toward the task.[^s3] As a technique, it is less about having a list and more about designing it well: items framed as questions, each with a clear purpose and owner, sent early enough that people can prepare.[^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Agendas are an old convention, but the evidence base is recent. In 2009 Desmond Leach, Steven Rogelberg, Peter Warr and Jennifer Burnfield surveyed 958 employees in the USA, UK and Australia, plus a second sample of 292 reporting on a single meeting, and tested which design features predicted how effective people judged their meetings.[^s1] Organisational psychologist Roger Schwarz popularised the practice of writing agenda items as questions in a 2015 *Harvard Business Review* piece,[^s5] and Rogelberg later built on it, arguing that the questions should also decide who attends and when the meeting ends.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Poorly planned meetings are a common cause of low meeting quality, and leaders can improve outcomes through how they design meetings before anyone arrives.[^s3] In Leach and colleagues' data, circulating a written agenda *before* the meeting was positively related to perceived effectiveness in both samples, and part of that link ran through attendees feeling more involved; an agenda handed out only at the meeting showed no significant relationship in the second sample.[^s1] A later review in *Current Directions in Psychological Science* turned these findings into a checklist: prepare an agenda, circulate it in advance, and follow it with clear goals and outcomes.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Start by asking attendees what they need from the meeting, and keep only items that affect everyone present and need the whole group's effort.[^s5] Write each item as a question - "Under what conditions should we reallocate desks?" rather than "Desk allocation" - which forces you to work out what outcome you want.[^s4][^s5] Tell people whether each item is to share information, seek input or make a decision, and name who leads it so they can prepare.[^s5] Let the questions set the invite list and the stopping point: the meeting is over when they are answered, and if you cannot write any, you may not need the meeting.[^s4] Send it ahead, open by stating the purpose, and use it to keep discussion on track.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

- **Recycling last week's agenda.** Rogelberg notes that many agendas are reused from meeting to meeting, which keeps stale items alive and hides what this meeting is for.[^s4]
- **Topic labels instead of questions.** A bare noun gives no finish line; a question tells the group what "done" looks like.[^s4][^s5]
- **Items for a subset of the room.** Topics that do not involve everyone waste the time of those who sit through them.[^s5]
- **Sending it at the door.** The benefit in the research is tied to circulating the agenda beforehand, not just having one in the room.[^s1]
- **Treating the agenda as the whole job.** Item relevance and how the leader facilitates discussion matter more than the document's existence.[^s4]

## Evidence

The effects are real but small. In Leach and colleagues' first sample, a written advance agenda kept a modest unique association with perceived effectiveness (β = .12) once other design features were controlled; correlations in both samples were around .2.[^s1] Both samples were cross-sectional self-reports, so they show association rather than causation.[^s1] Rogelberg himself summarises this line of work as showing little relationship between merely having an agenda and meeting quality, with the content of the items and the facilitation doing most of the work.[^s4] The question-based format is expert advice that has not been tested head-to-head against topic lists.[^s4][^s5] Hence `mixed`: circulate an agenda, but expect the gains to come from its design and use.

## Connections

The agenda technique's main evidence comes from the Leach et al. (2009) study of meeting design characteristics.[^s1] It is applied in workplace meetings of all kinds and sits under meeting design, the set of choices leaders make before a meeting starts.[^s2][^s3] It complements facilitation - the agenda sets the questions, facilitation gets them answered - and it complements meeting recaps, since reviews pair advance agendas with prompt minutes and action items.[^s2][^s4] By forcing a leader to write the questions first, it addresses unnecessary meetings: no questions, no meeting.[^s4] It is related to bottom line up front, because a stated purpose plays the same role for a meeting that a lead sentence plays for an email; to open-ended questions, the form agenda items take; and to the nominal group technique, a structured process you might assign to one agenda item.[^s4][^s5]
