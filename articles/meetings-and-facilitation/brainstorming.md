steps:
  - Frame one specific question in writing before the session, for example "What could cut our customer onboarding time from ten days to five?", and send it to participants a day ahead.
  - Open with five to ten minutes of silent, individual writing so everyone lists their own ideas before hearing anyone else's.
  - Pool the ideas in writing - sticky notes, a shared doc or passed cards - rather than having people call them out one at a time.
  - Give the group a short review round to read the pooled list and build on, combine or extend others' ideas, then send people back for another brief solo round.
  - State and enforce the ground rules - aim for quantity, welcome unusual ideas, build on others, and hold all criticism until the generation phase is over.
  - Evaluate and shortlist in a separate step - ideally after a break - using agreed criteria and a private or simultaneous vote.
example:
  situation: A product manager gathers seven colleagues, including a vice president, for an hour to find ways to reduce customer churn.
  before: "Let's just throw ideas out - no bad ideas! ... [VP] I think it's pricing. ... Good point. Anyone want to build on pricing?"
  after: "Spend eight minutes writing every churn idea you can, one per sticky note. We'll post them all, read them silently, add new notes that build on what you see, and only then cluster and vote with dots."
  why: Calling ideas out lets only one person speak at a time and lets the most senior voice anchor the room; writing first and pooling in parallel removes the queue and gets every person's ideas on the wall before status shapes the discussion.

  - Did I keep evaluation - including my own reactions - out of the generation phase?
  - Did I separate choosing ideas from producing them, with criteria agreed in advance?

# Brainstorming

Brainstorming is a meeting method for producing many options on a single question: participants offer ideas freely, aim for quantity, welcome unusual suggestions, build on one another's contributions and hold back criticism until later.[^s2] It is probably the most familiar idea-generation format at work, used for product ideas, process fixes and campaign concepts. The catch is that the classic version - people calling out ideas around a table - reliably produces fewer ideas than the same people working alone, so the practical skill is running a session that keeps brainstorming's spirit while avoiding its known losses.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Advertising executive Alex Osborn, a founding figure of the agency BBDO, first used the method in his agency in 1939, where staff called the meetings "brainstorm sessions"; he popularised it in his 1948 book *Your Creative Power* and expanded it in *Applied Imagination* (1953), which was widely translated.[^s1] Osborn went on to found the Creative Education Foundation in 1954 and launch the Creative Problem Solving Institute in 1955, roots of the creativity studies programme at Buffalo State.[^s1] He claimed that people could think up about twice as many ideas in a group as alone, and his rules - go for quantity, welcome wild ideas, combine and improve ideas, and withhold criticism - are still the standard list.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Getting many options on the table before choosing is the point of the method. But Osborn's group-advantage claim did not survive testing. Reviewing 22 experiments, Diehl and Stroebe (1987) found that 18 showed pooled individuals ("nominal groups") out-producing real groups, with no difference in the other four, all of which used pairs.[^s2] A meta-analysis by Mullen, Johnson and Salas (1991) confirmed that interacting brainstorming groups produce fewer and lower-quality ideas than nominal groups, with larger losses in bigger groups, when an authority figure is present and when ideas are spoken rather than written.[^s3] For a facilitator, that means an unstructured "throw ideas out" session wastes much of the room's thinking.

## How to apply it

The main fix is to remove the speaking queue. Diehl and Stroebe's experiments traced most of the loss to production blocking - only one person can talk at a time - rather than to free riding or fear of judgment; groups forced to wait their turn produced roughly half as many ideas as people who could contribute freely.[^s2] Later experiments showed why waiting hurts: long delays disrupt how people organise their ideas and unpredictable delays make their thinking less flexible.[^s4] So start with silent individual writing, pool ideas in parallel on notes or a shared document, and then let people read and build on each other's lists. In a field study at a high-technology company, alternating short solo writing periods with brief reviews of teammates' ideas produced the most ideas, and the group-then-individual sequence helped people build on shared ideas.[^s5] The Association for Psychological Science reported a 71% higher rate of ideas per person per minute for that alternating format compared with continuous group sharing.[^s6] Keep evaluation as a separate later step with agreed criteria and private or simultaneous voting.

## Common mistakes

- **Running it as an open-floor shout-out.** Spoken, one-at-a-time sharing is the format with the biggest productivity loss.[^s3]
- **Big groups.** Losses grow with group size, so split a large room into small groups or have everyone write.[^s3]
- **Letting the boss speak first.** Losses were larger when an experimenter was present, which the meta-analysis read as a social rather than procedural effect; have everyone write before senior people share.[^s3]
- **Treating "no bad ideas" as enough.** Fear of judgment was not the main cause of the loss in Diehl and Stroebe's experiments, so ground rules alone do not fix a blocked session.[^s2]
- **Judging success by ideas counted.** Much of this research, including the workplace studies, measured quantity rather than quality of ideas.[^s6]

## Evidence

The finding that interacting groups generate fewer ideas than pooled individuals has held across a review of more than twenty experiments and a meta-analysis, which found it fits social-psychological explanations better than procedural or economic ones.[^s2][^s3] That is why the popular claim that group brainstorming beats solo thinking is marked `contested` here. Writing-based variants that let people contribute in parallel are better supported, though the workplace evidence comes from a small number of field studies and mostly counts ideas.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Brainstorming was introduced by Alex Osborn and popularised in *Your Creative Power*.[^s1] Its central promise is challenged by Diehl and Stroebe's 1987 experiments and by the 1991 meta-analysis of Mullen, Johnson and Salas.[^s2][^s3] Brainwriting, in which ideas are written and passed rather than spoken, is the main alternative and the best-supported way to run group idea generation; it has been tested and applied in workplace meetings.[^s5][^s6] Brainstorming sits under facilitation and group decision making, and is related to the nominal group technique and 1-2-4-All, which both start with silent individual work; to production blocking and unequal participation, the problems those formats target; and to psychological safety, since people must feel safe to offer unusual ideas.[^s2][^s4]
