steps:
  - Hold it as soon as possible after the event or phase, with the people who did the work, and book 30-60 minutes rather than squeezing it into the end of another meeting.
  - Open by stating the ground rules - this is for learning, not grading; talk about roles and actions, not people; every view counts regardless of seniority.
  - Ask "What was supposed to happen?" and get agreement on the original goal, plan and success measures before going further.
  - Ask "What actually happened?" and build a factual timeline from several people's accounts and any data, without judging yet.
  - Ask "Why was there a difference?" for both what went well and what went badly, using open questions and drawing out quieter participants.
  - Ask "What will we do differently - and keep doing - next time?" and turn the answers into two or three specific actions with owners and dates.
  - Apply the actions in the very next piece of work, and open that work's kickoff by reviewing them.
example:
  situation: A marketing team has just shipped a product launch campaign two weeks late, and the manager calls the team together afterwards.
  before: "OK, the launch slipped. I've written up what went wrong - mainly design handoffs were late and nobody flagged it. Let's all be more proactive next time. Any questions?"
  after: "This isn't about blame - it's what we want to repeat and what to fix. First: what did we plan, and by when? Now let's build the timeline together. Priya, you were closest to design - what did you see? ... So the design brief changed twice after sign-off. What would have caught that earlier? Let's pick two changes, give each an owner, and use them on the Q3 campaign."
  why: The "before" is a one-way critique that names a vague culprit and ends with an exhortation, so people defend themselves or stay quiet and nothing changes. The "after" separates plan from reality, gathers several viewpoints, looks for causes in the process and ends with owned actions applied to the next project.
selfCheck:
  - Did we agree on what was supposed to happen before we argued about what went wrong?
  - Did the people who did the work find the lessons, or did I tell them?
  - Did we look at what went well as carefully as at what went badly?
  - Did we discuss roles and decisions rather than blaming individuals by name?
  - Which two or three actions came out of this, who owns them, and where will we use them next?

# After-action review

An after-action review (AAR) is a structured conversation, held soon after a piece of work, in which the people who did it compare what was supposed to happen with what actually happened, work out why the two differed, and decide what to sustain and what to change.[^s1] It is a discussion run by a facilitator rather than a verdict delivered by a boss: participants are guided to discover the lessons themselves, and the session is explicitly not a critique or a judgement of success or failure.[^s1][^s2] Researchers also call it a debrief or after-event review.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

The AAR comes from the U.S. Army. Its roots lie in the "interviews after combat" that the historian S. L. A. Marshall used in the Second World War to collect accurate accounts of battles, but the modern form was developed by the Army Research Institute in the mid-1970s.[^s2] It replaced the traditional critique, in which an umpire lectured the unit after an exercise, often on subjective estimates, in a tone that was frequently negative and that soldiers found hard to believe.[^s2] New engagement simulations gave trainers objective "ground truth" about what had happened, and the AAR was built to discuss that evidence two-way; it was later adopted at the National Training Center, where force-on-force training began in 1982.[^s2] The Army's current guide frames it around four questions: what was supposed to happen, what actually happened, what went right or wrong, and how to do the task differently next time.[^s1] Businesses adopted the practice from the 1990s, and Darling, Parry and Moore's 2005 *Harvard Business Review* article argued that companies mostly copied the meeting while missing the learning cycle around it.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Teams that finish a project and move straight on tend to repeat the same mistakes. Darling and colleagues describe a manufacturing executive who realised a project's review showed the team "discovering" problems that had already tripped it up twice.[^s5] The evidence that well-run debriefs help is fairly strong. Tannenbaum and Cerasoli's 2013 meta-analysis of 46 samples (2,136 people) found debriefs improved performance by about 20-25% over control groups, with similar effects for teams and individuals, in medical and non-medical settings, and in simulated and real work.[^s3] Because the AAR is also a meeting in which juniors are asked to speak about seniors' decisions, how it is run shapes whether people speak up at all.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Keep the format light and repeatable. Hold an informal AAR right after an event or at a natural break, and save the more prepared, formal version for big milestones.[^s1] Begin with ground rules: everyone with an observation contributes, nobody has all the answers whatever their rank, disagreement is fine if it is respectful, and the goal is improvement rather than grading.[^s1] Then work through the four questions in order. Settling "what was supposed to happen" first stops the group arguing about failure against different yardsticks, and building the "what happened" timeline from several people's accounts, plus data where you have it, gives everyone the same picture before anyone starts explaining it.[^s1][^s2]

Facilitate with open questions that draw out multiple perspectives, and let the group reach its own conclusions.[^s1][^s2] Review successes as well as failures: in Ellis and Davidi's field experiment, soldiers who debriefed both their successful and their failed navigation exercises improved more than those who reviewed only their failures, and their understanding of successful events became richer over time.[^s4] Finish with a small number of concrete changes, and apply them in the next piece of work. Darling and colleagues recommend pairing each AAR with a "before-action review" at the start of the next phase, asking what results are intended, what challenges are expected and what has been learned from similar work.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

The commonest failure is turning the AAR back into a critique, where the leader tells people what was good or bad and the conversation dwells only on what went wrong; that shuts down candour.[^s1] Other pitfalls in the Army's guide are asking yes/no questions, hearing only one viewpoint, glossing over real weaknesses, and naming individuals rather than duty positions when discussing failures.[^s1] In business settings, AARs often fail because they are treated as a one-off post-mortem, written up for a report nobody reads, and never connected to the next project.[^s5] A third trap is measuring the wrong thing: in the meta-analysis, debriefs that focused on team performance and were judged on team outcomes had the largest effects, while mismatched ones had much smaller ones.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The core benefit is supported by meta-analytic evidence, but with caveats.[^s3] Much of the underlying research is quasi-experimental, few unpublished studies were found, and studies with subjective outcome measures reported larger effects than those with objective ones.[^s3] Facilitation and structure appeared to help, yet so few studies tested unfacilitated or unstructured debriefs that those comparisons are weak.[^s3] Ellis and Davidi's study was conducted with soldiers in a single training setting, so its benefit for reviewing successes should be read as promising rather than settled for office work.[^s4] Much of the "how to" guidance, including the four questions and ground rules, rests on long military practice rather than controlled tests of each element.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

An AAR is a form of facilitated group feedback: Tannenbaum and Cerasoli define a debrief by active self-learning, developmental intent, focus on specific events and input from several sources.[^s3] That developmental, non-punitive purpose is what sets it apart from a performance review, which evaluates individuals.[^s3] It is the backward-looking partner of the premortem, which asks a team to imagine failure before work starts, and it typically ends in a meeting recap that records the owned actions. Its "everyone participates, regardless of rank" rule targets unequal participation, and it depends on psychological safety and on facilitators who ask open-ended questions.[^s1] The workplace version is usually run as a regular team meeting at project milestones.[^s5]
