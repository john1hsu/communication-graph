steps:
  - Write one specific, open question on the board or shared screen and read it aloud, for example "What are the biggest obstacles to closing month-end two days faster?"
  - Give everyone 10-20 minutes of silent, individual writing to list as many answers as they can, with no talking.
  - Run a round robin - each person reads out one idea per turn, which you record verbatim and number - and keep going until the lists are exhausted; allow no debate yet.
  - Walk through the numbered list for clarification only, letting anyone explain an item, and merge true duplicates with the agreement of the people who raised them.
  - Ask each person to privately pick and rank their top five items (5 points for the most important down to 1), then tally the scores in front of the group.
  - Discuss the top-ranked items and the surprises in the tally, and, if the scores are close, run a second private vote before you commit to next steps.
example:
  situation: An operations manager runs a one-hour meeting with eight team members, including two senior leads, to decide which process problems to fix first next quarter.
  before: "Okay, open floor - what should we fix first? ... Great point, Sam [senior lead], the ticketing tool is a mess. Anyone disagree? No? Let's go with that."
  after: "Take ten minutes and write down, on your own, every process problem that slows you down. Then we'll go round the table one idea at a time, I'll number them, and everyone ranks their top five privately before we talk priorities."
  why: The open floor lets the first senior voice anchor the room and blocks quieter people from offering ideas; silent writing and private ranking let every member contribute and weigh in equally before status and conversation shape the outcome.
selfCheck:
  - Is my question specific enough that people could write concrete answers to it on their own?
  - Did I protect the silent-writing and round-robin phases from early debate or evaluation, including my own?
  - Did each person rank privately, so seniors' preferences were not visible before others voted?
  - Is this really a single-question, prioritise-and-decide meeting, or does the topic need open exploration first?

# Nominal group technique

The nominal group technique (NGT) is a tightly structured way to run a meeting whose job is to generate options and agree priorities on one question. People are a "group in name only" for the first phase: each one writes ideas silently and alone. Ideas are then shared one per person per turn, clarified, and ranked privately, and the group's priorities are the summed individual scores rather than whatever the loudest discussion converged on.[^s2] It suits workplace moments such as choosing which process problems to fix, setting a project's top risks or gathering stakeholder priorities in a single session.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

NGT was designed by organisational researchers André Delbecq and Andrew Van de Ven, and set out in their program-planning work with David Gustafson in the 1970s.[^s2] In a 1974 *Academy of Management Journal* study, Van de Ven and Delbecq compared conventional interacting groups with nominal and Delphi groups on an applied fact-finding problem with no known answer; nominal and Delphi groups were about equally effective and both clearly beat the interacting groups on the number of ideas generated.[^s1] The technique has since spread widely, especially in health services research, where it is a standard consensus method alongside the Delphi technique.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Open discussion is a poor engine for producing ideas. Reviewing more than twenty studies, Diehl and Stroebe (1987) found that pooled ideas from people working alone almost always outnumbered those from interacting brainstorming groups of the same size, and their experiments traced most of the loss to production blocking: only one person can speak at a time, so others forget or suppress ideas while they wait.[^s3] NGT puts that "work alone first" finding into a meeting format.[^s3] It also balances participation: the round robin gives every member a turn and private voting means no one has to back a senior colleague's idea in public, which is why reviewers describe it as giving everyone a voice.[^s2]

## How to apply it

A typical session follows four stages - silent generation, round robin, clarification and private voting.[^s2] McMillan and colleagues suggest groups of no more than about seven, and time budgets of up to 20 minutes for silent writing, up to 30 minutes each for the round robin and clarification, and about 10 minutes for voting; a two-hour session for one question is common.[^s2] The facilitator's job is procedural: keep the round robin free of debate, record ideas in the contributor's own words, avoid steering during clarification, and remind people they need not agree with every item because they will vote their own view.[^s2] Recent adaptations add an individual pre-meeting activity, remote tools and more flexible discussion, which helped a 2024 study reach agreement across 28 mixed stakeholders quickly while participants reported they could speak freely.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Letting discussion leak into the early phases.** Commenting on ideas during the round robin reintroduces the blocking and evaluation the method is meant to remove.[^s3]
- **Mixing very different power levels without thought.** Status differences can still silence less powerful members during clarification, so groups are usually kept fairly homogeneous in status.[^s2]
- **Using it for the wrong meeting.** NGT handles a single, well-framed question; it limits open discussion, so it is a weak fit when the problem itself is still unclear.[^s2]
- **Treating the tally as the end.** Numerical consensus thresholds are rarely set in NGT studies; decide in advance what score spread counts as agreement and when to revote.[^s2]

## Evidence

The core idea-generation advantage of working alone first is well established in brainstorming research,[^s3] and early comparisons favoured nominal over interacting groups on idea output.[^s1] Evidence that NGT yields *better decisions*, rather than more ideas or more balanced input, is thinner: much of the literature is descriptive or applied, and reviewers note that practice varies and consensus criteria are seldom documented.[^s2] Hence `mixed` rather than `robust`.

## Connections

NGT sits under facilitation and group decision making. It was introduced by Delbecq and Van de Ven and demonstrated in their 1974 comparison study.[^s1][^s2] It addresses production blocking by separating idea generation from talk,[^s3] and addresses unequal participation through the round robin and private ranking.[^s2] It is an alternative to unstructured brainstorming[^s3] and to the Delphi technique, which uses anonymous multi-round questionnaires and takes weeks rather than hours.[^s2] It is applied in stakeholder consultation, where adapted versions help diverse groups reach timely agreement.[^s4] Because its private ranking lowers the social cost of disagreeing with senior colleagues, it is related to psychological safety, and it pairs with stakeholder mapping when deciding which groups should be represented.[^s2][^s4]
