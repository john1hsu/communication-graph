  - Before the meeting ends, read the decisions and action items aloud and get each owner to confirm their task and date in the room.
  - Within 24 hours, send the recap to everyone who attended and everyone affected who was absent.
  - Put the decisions first, each as one plain sentence stating what was agreed.
  - List each action as owner, verb-first task and due date - one named person per action, not a team.
  - Add a short "open questions" list naming who will resolve each one and where it lands next (a later agenda, a subgroup).
  - Ask readers to reply with corrections by a stated time, then treat the uncorrected recap as the record.
  - Start the next meeting by checking the previous recap's actions before new business.

  situation: A product manager follows up a 40-minute cross-team meeting about a delayed release.
  before: "Thanks all for a great discussion today! Lots of good ideas about the release. Let's keep the momentum going and circle back next week."
  after: "Decided: release moves to 18 March; the export feature ships in the April update. Actions: Ana - tell the three pilot customers about the new date by Thu 5 Mar. Raj - rebuild the test plan without export by Mon 9 Mar. Me - update the public roadmap by Fri 6 Mar. Open: whether support needs extra training (Lee to decide by our 12 Mar meeting). Reply by noon tomorrow if I've got anything wrong."
  why: The first version records a mood, not an outcome; the second lets anyone - including people who missed the meeting - see what changed, who owns each next step and when to expect it, and gives a deadline for correcting the record.

  - Does every action have exactly one named owner and a date, or did I write "we will" or "the team"?
  - Did each owner agree to their task out loud before we left, rather than discovering it in my email?

# Meeting recaps

A meeting recap is the short written note that follows a meeting and states what was decided, who owns each next step and by when, and what is still open.[^s1][^s3] Its job is not to transcribe the discussion but to turn a conversation that each person remembers slightly differently into one shared, checkable record that people can act on.[^s1][^s4] It is the after-meeting partner of the agenda: the agenda says what the meeting must settle, and the recap says what it did settle.[^s1]

## Origin

Formal minutes are an old convention of committees and boards, but the workplace recap is a lighter, action-focused descendant. Meeting-science reviews treat it as one of the core post-meeting practices: in 2018 Joseph Mroz, Joseph Allen, Dana Verhoeven and Marissa Shuffler distilled the research on workplace meetings into a checklist that tells organizers to send minutes and action items right after the meeting, drawing on Cohen, Rogelberg, Allen and Luong's 2011 work on staff-meeting design.[^s1] Health-professions guidance from the AAMC gives the same practice a concrete shape: a named recorder captures decisions, action plans and a summary, kept brief, with a responsible person for every item.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Meetings produce commitments, but commitments made out loud are easy to misremember or quietly drop. The 2018 review describes minutes as the record of decisions and of who is responsible for which next step, a record that both informs people who were absent and helps attendees follow through.[^s1] The AAMC guidance adds that recording who owns each action reduces confusion and that promptly distributed minutes double as a reminder and a way to see whether tasks are actually getting done.[^s3] University operational guidance makes the accountability point explicit: a summary within 24 hours, listing decisions, owners and deadlines, is what lets a team track and chase action items afterwards.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Do the recap's hard work before people leave. When each agenda item is resolved, say aloud who will act on it, and do not close a discussion without deciding how it will be acted on; unfinished items go to a later agenda or to a named subgroup.[^s3] Then write it up quickly - within a day - and send it to attendees and to the people affected who were not in the room.[^s1][^s4] Lead with decisions, then list actions as owner, task and due date, then open questions.[^s3][^s4] Keep it brief: decisions and actions, not a narrative of who said what.[^s3] Finally, close the loop: track the actions and review them at the next meeting so the recap becomes a working tool rather than an archive.[^s3][^s4]

In email or chat, the recap carries its weight in its first lines, so put the decisions there and keep any detail below. In a live meeting, the spoken read-back of decisions and owners matters as much as the written note, because it gives people the chance to object before the record is fixed.[^s3][^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **The transcript recap.** A blow-by-blow account of the discussion buries the few decisions and actions people need; guidance calls for brief minutes that list decisions and action items.[^s3]
- **Owners by implication.** "We'll look into it" or "marketing to follow up" assigns nobody; each action needs a responsible person and a deadline.[^s3][^s4]
- **Late recaps.** Sent days later, the note competes with people's own fading and diverging memories; reviews advise sending it immediately and practitioners within 24 hours.[^s1][^s4]
- **Surprise assignments.** Discovering a task for the first time in the recap invites pushback; confirm owners before the meeting ends.[^s3]
- **Write-only records.** A recap nobody revisits does little; reviewing last meeting's actions is what shows whether tasks are being completed.[^s3]

## Evidence

Direct tests are thin. The 2018 review recommends minutes and action items, but the studies it reports measure meeting design and attendee perceptions rather than comparing teams that do and do not send recaps, so the practice rests largely on expert consensus - hence `practitioner`.[^s1] The strongest indirect support comes from goal-setting research: a 2006 meta-analysis of 94 independent tests by Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran found that spelling out in advance when, where and how one will act had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = .65).[^s2] Those studies concern individuals' own if-then plans, not team action lists, so treat the link as a plausible mechanism for why a specific owner-task-date entry beats a vague "follow up", not as proof that recaps work.[^s2]

## Connections

Meeting recaps are applied in workplace meetings and sit under meeting design, the set of choices that shape a meeting before, during and after it.[^s1] They complement meeting agendas: reviews pair an advance agenda with prompt minutes and action items as two halves of the same checklist.[^s1] By naming one responsible person for each action, they address unclear ownership.[^s3] They are usually delivered by email within a day of the meeting.[^s4] Their emphasis on specific owner, task and date is plausibly explained by implementation intentions, the finding that concrete when-and-how plans improve follow-through.[^s2] They are related to spoken summarizing, which checks shared understanding during the meeting; to the RACI matrix, which clarifies roles for recurring work; to bottom line up front, which puts the decisions first; and to status reporting, which tracks the actions over time.[^s3][^s4]
