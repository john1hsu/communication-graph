steps:
  - Secure visible support from senior leaders (and labour partners where there is a union) and a commitment to stay with the work for at least six months.
  - Measure the starting point with a short civility survey of the unit so the group can see where respect, cooperation and conflict handling stand.
  - Train a facilitator from the unit, give them a toolkit of exercises, and schedule a regular, protected meeting for one intact team of about 10-15 people.
  - In the first sessions, have the group answer two questions in its own words - what civil behaviour looks like in this workplace, and what norms the group will hold itself to - and write the answers down, including norms for email and chat.
  - Use later sessions for short exercises and honest discussion of real friction points, turning each one into a specific change in how people greet, ask, reply and disagree.
  - Re-survey after the intervention and again later, share the results with the group, and keep reviewing the agreed norms so gains do not fade.
example:
  situation: A hospital outpatient team of 12 has a running problem with curt messages on its group chat, unanswered requests between shifts and public criticism on reply-all; staff say they dread opening the inbox.
  before: "The manager sends a memo to all staff: 'Reminder: please be professional and respectful in all communications. Unprofessional conduct will not be tolerated.' Nothing else changes, and within a week the same reply-all criticism resumes."
  after: "The team runs CREW sessions with a trained colleague as facilitator. In week two they agree, in their own words, that requests get an acknowledgement before the end of the shift, criticism goes to the person directly or into a call, and no one is copied just for visibility. Later sessions use real examples to practise rewriting abrupt messages, and the norms are revisited each month."
  why: A top-down reminder names a value but not behaviours and gives the team no ownership; CREW has the group define concrete norms itself, practise them together over months and review them, which is how the intervention produced lasting change in civility in controlled studies.

  - Do we have a regular, protected time to talk honestly about how we treat each other, rather than only when a complaint is filed?
  - Are leaders committed for months, and are they modelling the norms the group agreed?
  - Have we measured civility before and after, so we know whether anything actually changed?

# CREW (Civility, Respect and Engagement at Work)

CREW is a team-level programme for making a workplace more civil. Instead of issuing a conduct policy, it brings one intact work group together regularly over several months, with a facilitator drawn from the unit, to work out what civil behaviour means for them, agree norms they will hold each other to, and practise them.[^s3][^s4] The VA frames it around three linked ideas: civility as everyday courtesy and consideration, respect as listening and sensitivity that make honest conversation possible, and engagement as what grows from respectful, trusting relationships.[^s3]

## Origin

CREW was built inside the US Veterans Health Administration as a national initiative grounded in all-employee survey results and staff interviews; the VA required senior-leader and labour-partner support and a commitment of at least six months before a workgroup could start.[^s3] Michael Leiter and colleagues at Acadia University then adapted the process for Canadian hospitals and tested it against comparison units, publishing the first controlled evaluation in 2011.[^s1][^s4]

## Why it matters

In Leiter's 2011 study across 41 health-care units, the eight units that ran CREW for six months improved significantly more than 33 contrast units on coworker civility, supervisor incivility, respect, cynicism, job satisfaction, management trust and absences, and gains in civility explained the gains in attitudes.[^s1] A one-year follow-up with 1,957 providers found civility and distress kept improving after the programme ended and attitude gains held, although reductions in absence faded back to baseline.[^s2] The same norm-setting logic applies to digital channels: researchers on email incivility recommend clear team norms for acceptable email, modelled by leaders, and folding email incivility into civility training.[^s5]

## How to apply it

- **Get commitment first.** Leaders sign up for months, not a workshop, and the effort runs alongside normal work.[^s3]
- **Work with real teams.** In the Canadian version, groups of 10-15 people from the same unit met weekly for six months with a facilitator using a toolkit, and facilitators and leaders met regionally at three and six months.[^s4]
- **Let the group define civility.** Early sessions set ground rules and answer what civil behaviour is in this workplace and which norms the group will keep.[^s4] Include how people write: response windows, reply-all and copying, and moving conflict out of email.[^s5]
- **Practise, do not lecture.** Toolkit exercises are small and behavioural; one gave staff a code word that prompted them to pause and greet each other properly.[^s4] Meetings are a place to raise real frustrations honestly and fix them.[^s3]
- **Measure and sustain.** Survey before and after; plan for follow-up, because some gains fade without it.[^s1][^s2]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as a one-off training.** The evidence comes from months-long, group-based work; the broader intervention literature finds multi-component, organisation-level approaches more promising than one-off individual training.[^s2][^s6]
- **Writing the norms for the team.** CREW's distinctive move is that each group sets its own norms; imposed rules lose that ownership.[^s4]
- **Using it to handle bullying.** CREW targets everyday incivility across a group; serious individual misconduct still needs management or HR action.[^s3][^s6]
- **Ignoring digital channels.** Teams that only discuss face-to-face behaviour leave email and chat, where passive incivility such as ignored requests is common, unaddressed.[^s5]

## Evidence

The core studies are quasi-experiments with comparison groups rather than randomised trials, run in health-care settings by the researchers who adapted the programme, with self-reported civility as the main outcome.[^s1][^s2] A 2014 systematic review of bullying and incivility interventions appraised only 12 studies, and rated only three of them as moderate quality, so the field's evidence base is thin.[^s6] Whether CREW reduces email or chat incivility specifically has not been tested; that link rests on researchers' recommendations.[^s5]

## Connections

CREW is a structured way of building **team communication norms** (`cm:team-communication-norms`), its broader idea.[^s4] It **addresses workplace incivility** (`cm:workplace-incivility`), as **demonstrated by** Leiter et al. 2011 (`cm:leiter-et-al-2011`),[^s1] with durability **supported by** the one-year follow-up, Leiter et al. 2012 (`cm:leiter-et-al-2012`).[^s2] It plausibly **addresses cyber incivility** (`cm:cyber-incivility`) when the agreed norms cover email and chat, as email-incivility researchers recommend, but that remains untested (low confidence).[^s5] Related practices include **chat etiquette** and **meeting norms** for the specific rules a group may adopt, **facilitation** for running the sessions, **psychological safety** for the climate that honest discussion needs, and **interpersonal conflict** for the friction the sessions surface.[^s3][^s4][^s5]
