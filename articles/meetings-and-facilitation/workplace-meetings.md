# Workplace meetings

A workplace meeting is a planned gathering of colleagues, face to face or on a call, held to share information, work a problem or reach a decision together. Meetings are where much of an organization's talking actually happens: Lehmann-Willenbrock, Rogelberg, Allen and Kello, citing earlier surveys, put typical employee time at about six hours a week and managers' at roughly 23, and estimate that meetings absorb 7-15% of personnel budgets.[^s1] The same review reports estimates that as many as half of meetings are rated poor by attendees.[^s1] Because the situation recurs so often, small improvements in how meetings are run compound across a team's year.

## Origin

Meetings are as old as organizations, but treating them as an object of study is recent. Industrial-organizational psychologists such as Steven Rogelberg, Joseph Allen and Nale Lehmann-Willenbrock built a body of "meeting science" in the 2000s and 2010s and collected it in *The Cambridge Handbook of Meeting Science* (2015).[^s6] A landmark early study was Rogelberg, Leach and Burnfield's 2006 *Journal of Applied Psychology* survey of 980 employees, described by their university as the first international study of meeting time and well-being.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Meetings affect more than the hour they occupy. Kauffeld and Lehmann-Willenbrock videotaped and coded 92 regular team meetings; teams whose talk was more problem-solving and action-planning reported higher meeting satisfaction and productivity, and constructive meeting interaction was related to the organization's success about two and a half years later.[^s2] Dysfunctional talk, such as criticizing others or complaining, was negatively related to those outcomes, and its harm outweighed the benefit of good interaction.[^s2] Rogelberg also describes "meeting recovery syndrome", the cooling-off period after a bad meeting in which frustration spills into colleagues' conversations and other work.[^s3]

The effect of meeting load depends on the person and the meeting. In the 2006 study, people high in accomplishment striving felt worse with more meetings, and many short meetings hurt more than a few longer ones of the same total length; people lower in that trait reported better well-being with more meeting time, and whether meetings were run efficiently moderated the effect.[^s4]

## What works here

- **Decide whether a meeting is needed.** The review recommends a simple decision tree before calling one, and organisation-wide meeting-free time, so updates that need no discussion go to a written, asynchronous channel instead.[^s1]
- **Invite only people with a stake**, prepare an agenda, and start and end on time.[^s1] Rogelberg and Allen's unpublished data, reported by the APA, suggested that a start delayed by five to ten minutes sharply lowered attendees' ratings of satisfaction and effectiveness.[^s3]
- **Run the process, not just the content.** Set a time limit per item, balance participation by inviting quieter people in and checking people who dominate, and make room for people to take part in decisions, which Allen's research links to more satisfied attendees and more effective meetings.[^s3]
- **Make it safe to speak up and ask for feedback.** Leaders tend to rate their own meetings more favourably than attendees do, so the review recommends anonymous attendee feedback and meeting-skills training.[^s1]
- **Close with decisions, owners and dates** so the meeting produces outputs the team can act on.[^s1]

**Example.** A product team holds a 60-minute weekly meeting where eight people take turns reading status aloud and decisions slip to "next week". The lead moves status to a written update posted the day before, cuts the meeting to 25 minutes for the two or three items that need a decision, invites only the people who own those items, and ends each item by naming an owner and a date. The time saved comes from the updates nobody needed to hear live, and the remaining minutes go to the problem-solving and action-planning talk that the coding study linked to better outcomes.[^s1][^s2]

## Pitfalls

- **Meetings by default.** Recurring meetings that outlive their purpose drain time; firms in an *MIT Sloan Management Review* survey of 76 large companies reported higher productivity and lower stress after introducing one meeting-free day a week, though the data were self-reported and uncontrolled.[^s5]
- **Complaining spirals and side conversations.** Negative remarks tend to pull others in and outweigh the good the rest of the meeting does.[^s2][^s3]
- **Assuming one size fits all.** Meeting load suits some colleagues better than others, so asking the team how many meetings work for them beats imposing a single rule.[^s4]
- **Judging your own meeting from the chair.** Organisers overrate their meetings; ask attendees.[^s1]

The evidence base is real but uneven: the coding study is correlational, the well-being study relies on surveys, and the meeting-free-day findings come from a practitioner survey rather than a controlled trial.[^s2][^s4][^s5] The review also recommends leaving time for informal pre-meeting conversation, which on video calls has to be planned deliberately.[^s1]

## Connections

Workplace meetings are a setting for **group communication** within **organizational communication** (`cm:organizational-communication`), and they are the field of study of **meeting science**, the research programme summarised by Lehmann-Willenbrock and colleagues.[^s1][^s6] The Kauffeld and Lehmann-Willenbrock coding study (`cm:kauffeld-lehmann-willenbrock-2012`) and the Rogelberg, Leach and Burnfield well-being study (`cm:rogelberg-leach-burnfield-2006`) are the landmark studies that show what meetings do to teams and people.[^s2][^s4] Bad meetings can cause **meeting recovery syndrome** (`cm:meeting-recovery-syndrome`).[^s3] **Asynchronous communication** (`cm:asynchronous-communication`) is the alternative for updates that need no live discussion, in line with the review's advice to test whether a meeting is needed at all.[^s1] Practices that improve meetings include **meeting agendas** (`cm:meeting-agendas`), **facilitation** (`cm:facilitation`), **meeting recaps**, **psychological safety** (`cm:psychological-safety`), the **RACI matrix** (`cm:raci-matrix`) and the **nominal group technique** (`cm:nominal-group-technique`); **manager one-on-one meetings** (`cm:one-on-one-meetings`) are a specific kind of meeting, and **Zoom fatigue** (`cm:zoom-fatigue`) and the **camera-optional norm** (`cm:camera-optional-norm`) cover the video-call version.[^s1][^s3]
