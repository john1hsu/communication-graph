# Unequal participation

Unequal participation is what happens when a few people do most of the talking in a group and the rest say little. It is a barrier rather than a personality flaw: the meeting's format, the people in the room and the way turns are handed out decide who speaks, and the group then decides with only part of the knowledge it has.[^s1][^s6] Kellogg professor Leigh Thompson illustrates it with a widely repeated figure: in a typical six-person meeting, two people do more than 60 percent of the talking, and larger groups are worse.[^s5]

## Origin

The problem gained wide workplace attention in 2010, when Anita Woolley and colleagues reported that groups whose members took conversational turns more evenly tended to score higher on a general "collective intelligence" factor, which was not strongly tied to the average or highest IQ of members.[^s1] Facilitation guides had already treated over-talking and under-participation as standard problems for anyone running a discussion; the Open University's facilitation course, for example, warns against letting high participants monopolise the conversation.[^s6]

## Why it matters

When talk is lopsided, the group loses ideas and objections that quieter members hold, and the people who speak most can be mistaken for the people who know most. A 2020 study of leaderless small groups found that the time a member spent speaking predicted whether others later saw them as a leader, and the effect held after the researchers controlled for intelligence, personality and gender.[^s4] In other words, sheer airtime buys influence, which rewards whoever talks first and longest. Imbalance also tracks social position: in an experiment with many deliberating groups, Karpowitz, Mendelberg and Shaker found a substantial gap in women's voice and authority, which closed or widened depending on how many women were present and whether the group decided by majority or unanimous rule.[^s3] 

## Evidence

The link between even turn-taking and group performance comes mainly from Woolley et al. (2010), two studies with 699 people working in groups of two to five; the collective intelligence factor they found correlated with equality of conversational turns, members' average social sensitivity and the proportion of women in the group.[^s1] That broader claim is not settled. A 2021 meta-analysis by Rowe, Hattie and Hester found that the collective intelligence factor correlated only moderately with group performance (r = .26 across 857 groups), that results varied widely between studies, that independent replication attempts had partly or fully failed while those by the original team succeeded, and that most studies were underpowered.[^s2] The same review flagged that the studies did not control for individual participation rates, so a few dominant members could be driving the group scores.[^s2] The evidence status is therefore **mixed**: lopsided airtime is well documented, and it reliably shapes influence and leader perceptions,[^s4] but the popular claim that evening out turns will make any team smarter outruns the data.[^s2] Note too that the "two people, 60 percent" figure is presented on Kellogg's page without a named study, so treat it as an illustration, not a measured norm.[^s5]

## How to spot it

Watch turn counts, not volume. Signs include the same two or three voices opening every topic; quieter people speaking only when called on; decisions that mirror the first opinion voiced, often the most senior person's; and people who share objections in the corridor or in private chat after the meeting rather than during it.[^s6] On video calls, check who has not unmuted. When a group's composition puts some members in a small minority, expect their share of talk and influence to drop unless the process deliberately protects it.[^s3]

## How to fix it

Change the format so that contributing does not depend on grabbing the floor:

- **Collect views before discussion.** Have everyone write ideas on cards or in a shared document first (brainwriting), then discuss; Thompson recommends this specifically to blunt the loudest voices.[^s5]
- **Use pairs and small groups.** Let people talk in twos or small groups before the full group, which feels safer for quiet members.[^s6]
- **Agree ground rules.** Ask the group at the start how everyone can take part without one person dominating, and tell people no one will be put on the spot.[^s6]
- **Invite by name, gently.** When someone's body language suggests they have something to add, invite them and thank them for it.[^s6]
- **Let the senior person speak last.** Since early and frequent speakers gain influence regardless of the quality of their input, hold the leader's view until others have spoken.[^s4][^s6]
- **Fit the decision rule to the room.** Karpowitz and colleagues found that the decision rule changed how much an under-represented group was heard, so think about whether majority voting or consensus better protects minority voices in your group.[^s3]

A workplace example: a product team's weekly review was routinely settled by the engineering lead and the most senior designer within ten minutes. The manager switched to five minutes of silent writing in a shared document, then went round the table reading each person's top concern before open discussion, with the manager speaking last. Risks that used to surface only in private chats now came up in the meeting.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Unequal participation is a problem of **group communication** (`cm:group-communication`) and **undermines workplace meetings** (`cm:workplace-meetings`), because decisions rest on a narrow set of views.[^s1][^s6] It is **demonstrated by Woolley et al. 2010** (`cm:woolley-et-al-2010`), which tied uneven turn-taking to weaker group performance,[^s1] and that link is **challenged by the 2021 g-versus-c meta-analysis** (`cm:g-versus-c-meta-analysis-2021`), which questions how well the collective intelligence factor replicates.[^s2] It is **demonstrated by Karpowitz, Mendelberg and Shaker 2012** (`cm:karpowitz-mendelberg-shaker-2012`) for gender gaps in voice and authority,[^s3] and partly **explained by MacLaren et al. 2020** (`cm:maclaren-et-al-2020`), the babble-hypothesis study showing that speaking time itself earns leader status.[^s4] Existing articles describe remedies that address it: **facilitation** (`cm:facilitation`), the **nominal group technique** (`cm:nominal-group-technique`), **1-2-4-All** (`cm:1-2-4-all`) and **brainwriting** (`cm:brainwriting`), all of which give people time to think and a guaranteed turn.[^s5][^s6] It is closely related to **conversational turn-taking**, **interrupting**, **wait time**, **brainstorming** and **psychological safety**.[^s1][^s6]
