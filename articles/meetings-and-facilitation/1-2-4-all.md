steps:
  - Write one open, specific invitation the group can act on, such as "What opportunities do you see to cut our release lead time?", and show it to everyone.
  - Give one minute of silent, individual reflection in which each person writes their own ideas; protect the silence and do not talk over it.
  - Have people pair up for two minutes to share and build on each other's ideas, keeping different ideas distinct rather than forcing agreement.
  - Merge pairs into groups of four for about five minutes to compare ideas and pick out the ones that stand out.
  - Bring everyone back and ask each group for one important idea that is not yet on the board, recording each one, until the strongest ideas are visible.
  - Close by naming what the group will do with the ideas - prioritise them, assign owners or feed them into a decision - and debrief briefly with a co-host on how the process went.
example:
  situation: A team lead runs a 30-minute retrospective with ten engineers, two of whom are senior and usually speak first, to find ways to reduce late-night production incidents.
  before: "So, what should we change to cut down on incidents? ... Good point, Priya [senior engineer], more alerting makes sense. Anyone else? No? Let's take that as the action."
  after: "Take one minute on your own and jot down every change you think would cut late-night incidents. Now share with the person next to you for two minutes, then join another pair for five. Each group of four, give me the one idea we haven't heard yet."
  why: The open question lets the first senior voice set the answer and the quiet engineers never speak; reflecting alone and then in pairs means everyone has an idea and a low-risk audience before the whole room hears anything, so more and more varied options surface.
selfCheck:
  - Was my invitation specific enough that someone could write concrete answers to it in one minute?
  - Did I protect the silent reflection, or did someone (including me) start talking before everyone had written?
  - In the all-group share, did I ask for new ideas from each group rather than letting the most confident group report everything?
  - Did I steer the content of the small-group conversations, or did I limit myself to running time and process?

# 1-2-4-All

1-2-4-All is a short, tightly timed way to get ideas from everyone in a meeting. People reflect on a question alone, then talk in pairs, then in groups of four, and only then share with the whole group.[^s1] Its designers describe it as a way to surface ideas people would otherwise keep to themselves, flatten differences in rank and build shared ownership of what comes out, at any group size, in roughly 15 minutes.[^s1]

## Origin

The method was developed by Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless as part of Liberating Structures, a free, openly licensed collection of small group formats meant to replace conventional presentations and open discussions.[^s1] Each structure is defined by five design elements: the invitation, the use of space and materials, how participation is distributed, how groups are configured, and the sequence of steps with timings.[^s1] Its alone-then-pair sequence matches think-pair-share, which Frank Lyman proposed for classrooms in 1981.[^s3][^s4]

## Why it matters

In an open discussion only one person can talk at a time, so ideas are lost while people wait and a few voices dominate; research on group brainstorming has repeatedly found that groups talking freely produce fewer ideas than the same people working separately.[^s5] 1-2-4-All attacks both problems: everyone writes before anyone speaks, and in the pair round half the room can talk at once.[^s1][^s4] Classroom research on the analogous think-pair-share reports that thinking time improves the quality of answers and willingness to speak, and that pair talk gives people a low-risk rehearsal before speaking in front of everyone.[^s3][^s4]

The technique also builds the facilitator. Liberating Structures frames the host's job as moving from expert on stage to guide on the side, and advises newcomers to start with simple structures, debrief honestly with a co-host and try again, recommending 1-2-4-All as the way to generate options with a group.[^s2] Running it repeatedly is low-risk practice in the core facilitation moves: framing a question, holding time, protecting silence and staying out of the content.[^s1][^s2]

## How to apply it

Use it when you need many ideas, questions or concerns quickly - for example to prioritise tasks, gather feedback after a presentation, restart a stalled discussion or involve more voices in a difficult change.[^s1] Follow the steps above: invitation (1 minute), silent reflection (1), pairs (2), quartets (5) and whole-group share (about 7).[^s1] Online, if moving pairs into quartets is awkward, go straight from individual reflection to trios (1-3-All), or have everyone type one idea in the chat and press Enter at the same moment so no single comment anchors the rest.[^s1] You can repeat a round to go deeper or reverse the order of the steps.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping or shortening the silent minute.** The structure depends on everyone having their own idea before conversation starts, so keep to the timing.[^s1]
- **Over-helping.** Hosts who wander between pairs steering the content undo the point of the method.[^s1]
- **Trios that let one person dominate.** If you use groups of three, build in time for each person to speak.[^s1]
- **A share that only hears the bold.** In think-pair-share research, the whole-class share was the weakest phase: in one study men dominated whole-group sharing even though women contributed high-quality ideas at similar rates in small groups, and being called on cold raised anxiety for some students.[^s3] Ask each group for one new idea, collect ideas in writing, or let groups share with a neighbouring group instead.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

1-2-4-All itself has little formal testing; its support is practitioner experience plus evidence on its component moves.[^s1][^s2] Studies of think-pair-share and peer discussion, mostly in university classrooms, report more participation, longer explanations and gains in understanding, but these summaries come largely from education settings rather than workplace meetings.[^s4] Brainwriting studies with employees suggest writing before or between group sessions can raise idea output, though they were small and measured quantity, not quality.[^s5] Treat it as a well-reasoned default for inclusive idea generation, not a proven way to better decisions.[^s3][^s5]

## Connections

1-2-4-All is **part of Liberating Structures** (`cm:liberating-structures`) and was **introduced by Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless**.[^s1] It **develops facilitation** (`cm:facilitation`) because the Liberating Structures path for new hosts starts with simple structures like this one, practised and debriefed with a co-host.[^s2] It **addresses unequal participation** (`cm:unequal-participation`) by giving everyone time to think and a turn in a small group, and, more tentatively, **addresses production blocking** (`cm:production-blocking`) since people write ideas before they have to wait to speak.[^s1][^s5] It is **applied in workplace meetings** (`cm:workplace-meetings`).[^s1] It sits alongside the **nominal group technique**, **brainstorming**, **wait time** and **think-pair-share**, and supports **psychological safety** by lowering the risk of speaking up.[^s1][^s3]
