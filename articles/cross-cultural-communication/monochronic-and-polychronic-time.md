# Monochronic and polychronic time

Monochronic and polychronic time describe two ways of organising activity in time. In monochronic (M-time) settings people schedule and do one thing at a time, expect punctuality, and end meetings on time even if the agenda is unfinished; in polychronic (P-time) settings people handle several things at once, treat schedules as flexible, and let relationships and the business at hand take priority over the clock.[^s1] Researchers now usually call the individual-level version *polychronicity*: how much a person prefers to engage in two or more tasks or events at the same time.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

The anthropologist Edward T. Hall introduced the terms in *The Silent Language* (1959) as a cultural dimension, and in later co-authored work (Hall and Hall, 1987) described monochronic time as linear, tangible and divisible into blocks.[^s2][^s3] Textbooks still follow Hall in placing Northern Europe and North America towards the monochronic end and Latin America, the Mediterranean and the Middle East towards the polychronic end.[^s1] From the early 1990s, management and consumer researchers such as Allen Bluedorn, Carol Kaufman(-Scarborough), Paul Lane and Jay Lindquist turned the idea into individual attitude scales, notably the Polychronic Attitude Index and the Inventory of Polychronic Values.[^s2][^s3] Hall also bundled other traits with P-time, such as relationship orientation and high-context communication; later reviewers argue these should be tested rather than assumed to come together.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Different time habits show up as communication problems rather than as abstract values. A colleague who arrives ten minutes late, takes a call mid-meeting or lets a discussion overrun can look disrespectful to a monochronic teammate, while the polychronic colleague may find a hard stop with the main issue unresolved rigid or even rude.[^s1] Textbooks note that monochronic managers often find polychronic scheduling hard to manage and that polychronic staff can feel constrained by clock-driven plans, so mixed teams do better when they agree explicitly on deadlines, meeting length and what happens to unfinished agenda items.[^s1] Within one country the same clash appears between individuals: in a 1999 Philadelphia-area survey, people who scored as polychronic said they changed activities more and worked best under pressure, while monochronic people planned in more detail and were more upset by schedule changes.[^s2]

## Using it at work

1. **Make time norms explicit.** At the start of a project, agree what "on time" means for meetings and deadlines, whether meetings end at the scheduled time, and where overflow items go, rather than assuming everyone shares your default.[^s1]
2. **Ask about the person, not the passport.** Polychronicity varies a lot between individuals and jobs, so ask how a colleague likes to plan and handle interruptions instead of inferring it from nationality.[^s3]
3. **Match the work to the style where you can.** Polychronic people report a better fit with jobs full of switching, and research suggests the style helps mainly when a role genuinely demands juggling; protect focused blocks for people and tasks that need them.[^s2][^s3][^s4]
4. **Do not treat juggling as productivity.** In a diary study of German employees, days with more multitasking brought lower evening well-being and lower self-rated performance, especially for people low in polychronicity.[^s4]

## Evidence

The individual-level construct is measurable: short scales such as the three-item Polychronic Attitude Index reached reliabilities around .8, and a 1999 survey of 112 respondents explained about 54% of variance in polychronic attitude with planning, pressure and schedule-change items.[^s2] In a 2015 diary study of 93 employees from 12 German firms over five workdays, polychronicity predicted how much people multitasked each day, and it moderated how multitasking related to self-rated performance.[^s4] Both studies rely on self-reports from single regions, so their authors call the results exploratory or limited in generality.[^s2][^s4]

The cultural claims and the link to ability are weaker. König and Waller's 2010 review found that cross-national comparisons are mixed, with some studies finding no differences between French, Japanese, German and U.S. samples, and noted that measurement equivalence across cultures had not been tested; they concluded that culture is an overestimated cause of polychronicity.[^s3] They also found little evidence that polychronic people are better at multitasking, inconsistent correlations with job performance, and a definition muddled by mixing preference with behaviour.[^s3] Treat national M-time/P-time rankings as rough hypotheses, and the individual preference as a real but modest difference in style rather than skill.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

- Narrower case of **intercultural communication**: it is one of Hall's cultural dimensions taught alongside contexting and proxemics.[^s1]
- **Introduced by Edward T. Hall** in **The Silent Language** (1959).[^s2]
- **Challenged by König & Waller (2010)**, who questioned the definition, the cultural antecedents and the performance claims.[^s3]
- **Supported by Kirchberg, Roe & Van Eerde (2015)**, whose diary study linked polychronicity to everyday multitasking at work.[^s4]
- **Confused with multitasking**: polychronicity is a preference, multitasking is the behaviour, and polychronic people are not reliably better at it.[^s1][^s3]
- Related to **high- and low-context cultures** and **Hofstede's dimensions** as rival ways of describing cultural difference, and to **meeting agendas**, **setting reply-time expectations** and **multicultural teams**, where time norms need to be agreed out loud.[^s1][^s3]
