# Ethnocentrism

Ethnocentrism is placing your own culture at the centre: treating its beliefs, values and ways of behaving as normal and right, and judging other cultures by that yardstick.[^s5] Pride in your own culture is not the problem; the barrier appears when "how we do it" silently becomes "how it should be done", so that a different style is read as a defect.[^s5] In communication it works as a filter on both the message and the messenger: it shapes how you interpret what an out-group colleague says and how competent and credible you judge them to be.[^s2]

## Origin

Psychology and the social sciences have long credited the American sociologist William Graham Sumner with coining the term in 1906,[^s4] and his description of one's own group as the centre of everything is still the standard reference point.[^s3] A 2014 review of German, Polish and English sources by Boris Bizumic found the concept in print decades earlier, for example in Ludwig Gumplowicz's work of 1879 and 1881, and argued that Sumner popularised rather than coined it.[^s4] Communication researchers James Neuliep and James McCroskey brought the idea into intercultural communication as a measurable trait with their Generalized Ethnocentrism (GENE) scale in 1997: 22 agree/disagree items, 15 of them scored, with reliability typically around .80 to .90.[^s1] They argued that ethnocentrism has uses, such as group loyalty, but acts as a barrier to communication because people who see another culture as inferior tend to avoid contact with it.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Ethnocentrism rarely sounds like open prejudice at work. More often it is a manager calling an indirect colleague "evasive", a reviewer marking down a report that leads with context, or a team that keeps routing questions around the member whose English sounds different.[^s5][^s2] Because people tend to talk with those they find attractive and credible, an ethnocentric lens reduces contact, which removes the very conversations that would correct the misreading.[^s3]

## Evidence

- **Hiring and managing.** In two video studies, Neuliep, Hintz and McCroskey (2005) found that US students higher in ethnocentrism rated a Korean job applicant lower on social attraction, competence and character and were less likely to recommend hiring him.[^s2] When a manager reprimanded a worker, ethnocentrism was linked to lower ratings of an Asian manager's attractiveness, competence and general attitudes but not of an otherwise identical white manager; it was not linked to ratings of managerial effectiveness.[^s2]
- **Willingness to talk.** In a 2016 survey of 438 New Zealand management students, higher GENE scores went with lower intercultural willingness to communicate, a modest correlation of about r = -.24.[^s3]
- **Limits.** These are mostly correlational studies with student samples and self-report scales, and effects are small to moderate, so the finding is better treated as consistent but modest than as a large effect.[^s3][^s2] The intervention evidence is thinner still: the 2016 review could point to only one study that measured a drop in ethnocentrism after an intercultural service-learning project.[^s3]

## How to spot it

- **Labels that hide a norm.** Words like "unprofessional", "rambling", "blunt" or "evasive" applied to a colleague from another culture often describe distance from your own default rather than a fault.[^s5]
- **Central-position language.** Phrases that only make sense from your vantage point, such as calling a region the "Far East", show where you have put the centre.[^s5]
- **Uneven credibility.** You trust the same idea more when a same-culture colleague proposes it, or rate an interviewee's competence on accent and style.[^s2]
- **One-way fixes.** Your plan for a cross-cultural problem is that the other side should adapt; textbooks call the extreme form of this cultural imperialism.[^s5]

## How to fix it

- **Name your standpoint.** Before judging a message, say to yourself which norm you are using and where you learned it.[^s5]
- **Ask what it means before deciding what it means.** Treat an unexpected style as a question ("How would this usually be read on your team?") rather than as evidence of poor skill.[^s5]
- **Separate content from style in evaluations.** In interviews and reviews, score the substance against written criteria before forming an overall impression, since ethnocentrism mainly distorts attraction, competence and character ratings.[^s2]
- **Build real contact.** Pair people across cultures on shared work rather than relying on awareness training. A meta-analysis of 515 studies found that contact reduced prejudice in 94% of them, with a mean r of about .21, and stronger effects under Allport's optimal conditions.[^s6] It seems to work mainly by lowering anxiety and building empathy rather than through facts.[^s6]
- **Make mixing the default.** Deliberately designed mixed-culture teams, and contact that is expected rather than optional, are the recommended levers.[^s3]

**Example.** A UK-based project lead reviews a status update from a colleague in another office that opens with background and only hints at a delay. Her first draft reply says the update is "unclear and evasive". Rewritten, it says: "Thanks — I read this as the March date being at risk. Is that right, and what would you need to hold it?" The second version checks her reading and keeps the colleague talking instead of labelling a style.[^s5][^s3]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a core barrier studied in intercultural communication, where it is treated as a filter on how messages and sources are perceived.[^s2][^s5]
- **Demonstrated by Neuliep, Hintz and McCroskey (2005),** whose video studies linked ethnocentrism to lower ratings of out-group interviewees and managers.[^s2]
- **Undermines intercultural willingness to communicate,** with which it correlates negatively in student samples.[^s3]
- **Undermines credibility:** it lowers the competence and character people attribute to out-group speakers.[^s2]
- **Introduced by Ludwig Gumplowicz,** according to Bizumic's history; Sumner later popularised the term.[^s4]
- **Related:** intergroup contact is the best-evidenced route to reducing prejudice of this kind;[^s6] high- and low-context differences and direct versus indirect feedback are common styles that ethnocentric listeners misread; humble inquiry and *The Culture Map* offer ways to ask about and map those differences instead of judging them.
