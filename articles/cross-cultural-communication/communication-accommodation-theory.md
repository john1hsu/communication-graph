# Communication accommodation theory

Communication accommodation theory (CAT) explains why people change how they talk depending on who they are talking to, and what those changes do to the relationship. Speakers *converge* when they become more like their partner in accent, speed, wording or style, usually to reduce social distance, show respect or win approval; they *diverge* when they become less like their partner, to keep distance or to regulate the exchange, for example by slowing down a fast talker.[^s2] The theory's central practical point is that the adjustment itself matters less than how the listener reads it: the same move can feel courteous, condescending or cold depending on the motive the listener attributes to it.[^s2]

## Origin

The theory began in 1973 as speech accommodation theory, developed by Howard Giles to explain choices of language, accent and dialect.[^s2][^s6] In an early study with Donald Taylor and Richard Bourhis, 80 bilingual English-Canadians heard a French-Canadian man describe a picture in French, mixed French and English, fluent English or halting English, knowing he could choose his language.[^s1] The authors reported that listeners judged him more favourably, and accommodated more in their own descriptions, when he appeared to make more effort to accommodate to them.[^s1] Over the following decades the theory broadened from speech to "communication", covering nonverbal behaviour, discourse and written and online exchanges; its 50th anniversary review in 2023 traces its spread across fields, languages and cultures and into computer-mediated and human-machine interaction.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Every cross-cultural or cross-level conversation involves accommodation choices, often unconscious ones. Gasiorek, Giles and Soliz summarise the theory in four principles: people accommodate more when they want to affiliate or be understood, and less when they want to disaffiliate or be hard to follow; perceived accommodation tends to reduce social distance and raise satisfaction, and perceived non-accommodation does the reverse, in both cases depending on the motives listeners attribute.[^s2] Two failure modes follow. *Under-accommodation* means not adjusting enough, such as keeping idioms and full speed with a second-language colleague. *Over-accommodation* means adjusting in a way that feels patronising, such as exaggerated simplicity, and it generally draws more negative judgements than under-accommodation.[^s2] Simply holding your usual style (maintenance) is itself read as a form of non-accommodation.[^s2]

Status shapes who is expected to move. Lower-status speakers tend to converge towards higher-status ones, although the authors stress this is a tendency rather than a rule.[^s2] In 144 face-to-face pairs in which one person held a high-power role and the other a low-power role, Muir and colleagues found that low-power participants with certain personality profiles accommodated their language style more, and that their partners then rated rapport and their attractiveness more positively.[^s4]

## Evidence

The research base is large. Soliz and Giles systematically reviewed 149 CAT articles published from 1973 to 2010 and meta-analysed 76 of them, with a combined sample of 18,382, estimating effects for behaviours such as non-accommodation and reluctant accommodation and for outcomes such as quality of contact and relational solidarity.[^s3] More than half of all CAT research up to that point had appeared since 2000, spreading from ethnolinguistic and intergenerational settings into family, legal, healthcare, classroom and mediated contexts.[^s2]

The status here is `mixed` rather than `robust` because the effects are strongly context-dependent. Accommodation is not uniformly good: in two instant-messaging experiments where "judges" could award money to "workers" pitching business ideas, judges who matched their workers' language style more were rated lower on rapport and attractiveness, while workers' accommodation made no significant difference to judges' ratings.[^s5] The authors explain this through expectations that high- and low-power speakers behave differently, but the samples were small (54 and 30 participants), so the finding is suggestive.[^s5] The theory's own principles also make outcomes hinge on attributed motives, which are hard to predict in advance, and the 2015 authors note that the principles describe single interactions and do not yet explain how repeated exchanges accumulate.[^s2]

## Putting it to work

Treat accommodation as a deliberate choice rather than a reflex.[^s2] Converge first on what helps understanding, such as pace and vocabulary, since the theory links accommodation to being understood as well as to affiliation.[^s2] For example, a project lead briefing a new offshore team might slow down, drop idioms and pause for questions, instead of either racing through the usual slang-heavy stand-up (under-accommodation) or switching to loud, clipped "simple English" (over-accommodation).[^s2] If you hold the senior role, be careful about copying a junior colleague's casual chat style, since high-power accommodation backfired in the messaging experiments.[^s5] Afterwards, judge success by the listener's reaction, because perceived accommodation, not your intention, drives the outcome.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Howard Giles, who developed speech accommodation theory in 1973.[^s2][^s6]
- **Demonstrated by** Giles, Taylor and Bourhis (1973), the Canadian bilingual study in which more effortful accommodation drew more favourable judgements.[^s1]
- **Supported by** Soliz and Giles (2014), the systematic and meta-analytic review of four decades of CAT research.[^s3]
- **Demonstrated by** Muir and colleagues (2017), whose messaging experiments show accommodation by the higher-power party can backfire; it is therefore **applied in** instant messaging and other text-based channels.[^s5]
- **Studied in** communication studies, where it is one of the most influential behavioural theories, and sits within intercultural communication through its roots in language and accent choice.[^s2]
- **Related to** audience design and register and tone, which describe tailoring style to the listener; communicating across languages, whose adjustments it explains; mirroring and rapport; politeness theory and power distance, which concern the same status dynamics; and the multicultural team, where these choices happen daily.[^s2][^s4]
