# Intercultural competence

Intercultural competence is the ability to function effectively across cultures; organisational researchers broadly agree on that core, even though they model it in many different ways.[^s1] In Darla Deardorff's widely used formulation, the visible result is communication and behaviour that is both *effective* (you achieve your goals to some degree) and *appropriate* (the other side sees your conduct as acceptable), resting on underlying attitudes, knowledge and skills.[^s3][^s4] Deardorff also argues that, since every person carries several cultures, nearly any interaction can be treated as intercultural, which makes this a general workplace skill rather than an expatriate specialism.[^s4]

## Origin

Interest in the idea grew out of study abroad, expatriate management and intercultural training, and by 2014 reviewers counted more than 30 models and over 300 related constructs.[^s1] To cut through that variety, Deardorff ran a three-round Delphi study by email with leading intercultural scholars for her 2004 doctoral dissertation at North Carolina State University, and asked administrators at 24 internationalising US universities to accept or reject the experts' conclusions.[^s2] The panels preferred a general definition and agreed that degrees of competence can be assessed, best through a mix of methods including interviews, observation and judgments by self and others.[^s2] The resulting process model, published in 2006, is often described as the first research-based definition of the concept.[^s3][^s4]

## Why it matters

Leung, Ang and Tan's 2014 review sorts the many constructs into three families: enduring **traits** such as open-mindedness and tolerance of ambiguity, **attitudes and worldviews** that run from ethnocentric to ethnorelative, and **capabilities** such as cultural intelligence and language skill.[^s1] Across that literature, measures in the trait and capability families predict psychological adjustment, and capability measures show the most consistent links to task performance, leader effectiveness, trust and cohesion in teams, and joint gains in negotiation.[^s1] For a communicator, the practical upshot is that good intentions and country facts are not enough; the behaviour in the room is what predicts results.[^s1]

## What good looks like

Deardorff's process model lays out the pieces in the order they tend to build:[^s3]

- **Attitudes:** respect (valuing other cultures), openness (holding back judgment) and curiosity (tolerating ambiguity).[^s3]
- **Knowledge:** cultural self-awareness, deeper knowledge of the other context, and sociolinguistic awareness of how language is used.[^s3]
- **Skills:** listening, observing and evaluating; analysing, interpreting and relating.[^s3]
- **Internal outcome:** a shift in frame of reference toward adaptability, flexibility, an ethnorelative view and empathy.[^s3]
- **External outcome:** effective and appropriate communication and behaviour in the situation.[^s3]

**Example.** *Situation:* a project manager in Toronto leads a vendor team in another country. *Before:* when a vendor lead answers a deadline request with "we will try our best," she logs the date as confirmed and is surprised when it slips. *After:* she notes what was actually said, asks an open question ("What would need to be true for the 14th to work?"), and agrees with the lead on a way to flag risk privately. *Why:* she separated observation from interpretation and adjusted the channel, so the message that the date was at risk could be delivered without loss of face.[^s3][^s4]

## How to practise it

1. **Start with yourself.** Write down two or three of your own defaults about directness, hierarchy and time; cultural self-awareness is the first knowledge element in the model.[^s3]
2. **Observe before you evaluate.** In a puzzling exchange, record what was said and done, then list more than one plausible meaning before acting on any.[^s3]
3. **Set a small, specific goal.** Break "be better across cultures" into one observable behaviour, such as checking understanding at the end of calls with a partner office.[^s4]
4. **Get others' views.** Self-report shows only half the picture; ask a colleague from the other context how your approach landed.[^s4]
5. **Learn on the job and reflect.** Development takes years, and reviewers stress experiential learning in real global teams with deliberate reflection over one-off courses; a 2001 meta-analysis found only small positive links between training and adjustment (r = 0.12) or performance (r = 0.23).[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The status here is `mixed`. The construct predicts meaningful outcomes, but validation is uneven across models, few studies pit models against each other, most samples are elite expatriates, and measurement leans heavily on self-report that may be faked in selection.[^s1] A 2020 study of the three best-validated tests (the Multicultural Personality Questionnaire, Cultural Intelligence Scale and Intercultural Adjustment Potential Scale) found them substantially intercorrelated, possibly overlapping with personality, with some low-reliability scales; it noted that self-reported abilities often diverge from actual behaviour, and two authors work for the company selling one of the tests.[^s5] Deardorff stresses that competence is never finished and that language fluency is necessary but not sufficient.[^s4] Treat any score as a starting point for feedback, not a verdict.

## Connections

Leung, Ang and Tan's review, `cm:leung-ang-tan-2014`, supports the construct's links to adjustment and performance and is the best map of its competing models.[^s1] `cm:cultural-intelligence` is the capability model with the strongest evidence within that family, and Milton Bennett's developmental model of intercultural sensitivity (`cm:milton-bennett`) shaped its worldview strand.[^s1] Its internal outcome, an ethnorelative view, is the direct counter to `cm:ethnocentrism`.[^s3] It pays off in a `cm:multicultural-team`, where reviewed studies link it to trust, cohesion and creativity.[^s1] Deardorff frames it as a matter of communication and behaviour, which places it within `cm:intercultural-communication`.[^s4] `cm:matsumoto-hwang-2020` challenges how cleanly current tests measure it.[^s5] Practices such as `cm:describe-analyze-evaluate`, `cm:culture-assimilator`, `cm:active-listening` and `cm:perspective-taking` exercise the listening, observing and interpreting skills in the model.[^s3]
