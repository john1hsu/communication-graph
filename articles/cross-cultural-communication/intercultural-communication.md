# Intercultural communication

Intercultural communication is the field that studies what happens when people with different cultural identities try to understand each other. Textbook definitions, following Lustig and Koester, place it where cultural differences are large enough that the people involved hold different interpretations and expectations about how to communicate competently.[^s2] It is not limited to international business: the same dynamics show up in one-to-one conversations, teams, public speaking and mass media, and between groups inside one country.[^s2] For a working professional, the field is the source of most of the vocabulary used to diagnose cross-cultural friction, from high- and low-context styles to ethnocentrism, and of the training methods meant to reduce it.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

The anthropologist Edward T. Hall is usually credited as the field's first explicit writer, and his 1959 book *The Silent Language* is generally treated as its founding work.[^s1] Communication historian Wendy Leeds-Hurwitz showed in 1990 that Hall's ideas grew out of a team effort: between 1946 and 1956, linguists and anthropologists at the U.S. Foreign Service Institute developed them while preparing American diplomats for postings abroad.[^s1] That origin explains the field's practical bent. It began as training for people who had to get work done with counterparts whose unspoken rules about time, space and directness differed from their own.[^s1] Hall's line that culture is communication, and communication is culture, is still the field's standard starting point.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Most workplace misunderstandings across cultures are not about vocabulary but about expectations: how direct a refusal should be, how much context a request needs, who speaks first in a meeting. The field gives names to these differences so they can be discussed rather than taken personally, and it treats stereotyping and ethnocentrism as barriers to be managed, not as moral failings to be denied.[^s2] It also offers a working definition of the goal. Intercultural competence, in Darla Deardorff's widely used research-based formulation, is communication and behaviour that is both effective and appropriate in intercultural interactions, and she notes that in a broad sense every interaction is intercultural.[^s4]

In practice, the field's findings translate into a few habits:

- **Treat culture as a hypothesis, not a label.** Use a framework to generate a guess about why a colleague's email felt abrupt, then check it with that person.[^s3]
- **Judge appropriateness from the other side.** Deardorff warns that self-ratings show only half the picture; whether your behaviour was appropriate is best judged by the people on the receiving end.[^s4]
- **Look beyond passports.** Occupation, socio-economic background and the economic environment can predict work values better than nationality does.[^s3]

A typical case: a project lead in a distributed product team reads a supplier's vague "we will try our best" as agreement, misses a deadline, and blames the supplier. An intercultural lens prompts a different move before the deadline: ask an open question about what could get in the way, and confirm commitments in writing.[^s2][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The field rests partly on interpretive work and partly on large comparative studies, and the second strand has been sharply questioned. A 2016 meta-analysis by Vas Taras, Piers Steel and Bradley Kirkman pooled 558 studies that used Hofstede's cultural-values dimensions across 32 countries and found that roughly 80% of the variation in those values lay within countries rather than between them.[^s3][^s6] For power distance, occupation accounted for about half of the variance, against roughly a fifth for country.[^s3][^s6] The authors concluded that country is often a poor proxy for culture, a warning against applying national scores to individual colleagues.[^s3] Their analysis covers values only, not language, customs or communication style, and it is correlational.[^s3]

The applied side has more encouraging but modest evidence. A 2021 meta-analysis of 37 samples covering 7,040 trainees found that cross-cultural training had a medium positive effect on adjustment and small positive effects on cultural intelligence and job performance, with training method and rating source moderating the results.[^s5] Assessment remains a weak point: Deardorff advises combining several methods and perspectives, such as observation, critical-incident analysis and others' ratings, rather than relying on self-report surveys.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Edward T. Hall, in *The Silent Language*,** building on Foreign Service Institute work that drew on linguistics and anthropology.[^s1]
- **Narrower concepts** studied in the field include high- and low-context cultures, proxemics, direct versus indirect feedback, and ethnocentrism as a barrier; *The Culture Map* is a popular managerial synthesis.[^s1][^s2]
- **Intercultural competence** is the field's name for the goal, and exercises such as describe-analyse-evaluate are its classroom tools.[^s4]
- **Challenged by Taras, Steel and Kirkman (2016),** whose findings undercut the habit of equating country with culture in Hofstede-style research.[^s3]
- **Supported by the 2021 training meta-analysis,** which links cross-cultural training to better adjustment and, more weakly, to performance.[^s5]
