# Politeness theory

Politeness theory is the best-known account of why people soften what they say. Brown and Levinson proposed that every competent adult has two kinds of face: positive face, the want to be liked and approved of, and negative face, the want to act without being imposed upon.[^s6] Many everyday acts threaten one or both. A request or a deadline imposes on the hearer's freedom; criticism, disagreement or turning down an invitation threatens their wish to be valued.[^s2] Politeness is the set of linguistic choices speakers use to redress those face-threatening acts (FTAs).[^s1]

## Origin

Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson first published the model as a long essay in 1978 and reissued it as the book *Politeness: Some universals in language usage* in 1987, with a new introduction reviewing the research it had prompted.[^s1] They built it from detailed comparison of Tamil, Tzeltal and English and argued that shared principles of interaction sit beneath the surface variety of polite behaviour across societies.[^s1] Their face concept draws on the sociologist Erving Goffman's earlier work on facework, but splits it into a want for approval and a want for freedom from imposition; some critics argue this dichotomy narrows Goffman's original idea and leans too heavily on negative face.[^s6]

## Why it matters

The theory gives you a vocabulary for choosing how to phrase anything that costs the other person something. It names a ladder of strategies, from most to least direct: say it baldly ("Send me the deck"), use positive politeness that stresses closeness and shared goals ("You know this material best, could you send the deck?"), use negative politeness that respects the hearer's autonomy with hedges, questions and apologies ("Sorry to ask on short notice, would you be able to send the deck?"), go off record with a hint ("I still don't have the deck"), or not perform the act at all.[^s2] The model predicts that people pick more redressive strategies as three things grow: the social distance between speaker and hearer, the hearer's power over the speaker, and the size of the imposition.[^s3]

These choices also signal status. Less polite request forms make listeners infer that the speaker holds more power, and powerless-sounding speech can reduce a speaker's credibility.[^s2] In a laboratory study of superior-subordinate exchanges, David Morand found that power was communicated through specific politeness moves that bosses and subordinates used differently, which he linked to distorted upward communication in hierarchies.[^s4] For a manager, that means an unhedged instruction can land as a reminder of rank; for an employee, heavy hedging can make an important warning easy to dismiss.

## Using it at work

Before a hard request, estimate the three variables. A small favour from a close peer can go almost bald on record; a large ask of a senior stakeholder you barely know calls for redress such as acknowledging their time and giving them a real option to decline.[^s3] Match the redress to the face under threat: experiments suggest a strategy is judged most polite when it addresses the specific kind of face the act endangers, so an apology suits an imposition while praise suits a criticism.[^s2] In writing, small wording shifts carry a lot of weight: in a large study of online requests, sentence-medial "please" and counterfactual modals ("could you", "would you") read as polite, while direct imperatives and statements of what the reader must do read as impolite.[^s5] Beware of over-hinting with people of higher status or from more direct cultures: an off-record request can simply be missed, so make the actual ask recoverable.[^s2]

## Evidence

Experimental tests give the theory partial support. Holtgraves and Yang (1992) found that politeness rose with the size of the request, the addressee's status and social distance, and coding of videotaped interactions found similar patterns in American and Korean samples.[^s3] Reviews of the experimental literature report that imposition reliably increases politeness, power generally does, but distance effects are mixed, the variables interact rather than simply adding up, and the proposed ranking of strategies holds only in part: for requests, negative politeness is often rated more polite than hinting.[^s2] Field data back the power prediction: Wikipedia editors were more polite before winning administrator elections and less polite afterwards, and lower-reputation Stack Exchange users were more polite than high-reputation ones.[^s5] Stephan, Liberman and Trope's eight studies (2010) suggest that politeness is tied to psychological distance more broadly, rising when the addressee is spatially or temporally far away.[^s3]

The universality claim is the most contested part. Matsumoto (1988) and Ide (1989) argued that Japanese politeness rests on socially prescribed discernment and relative rank rather than on the individual's wish to be unimpeded, so negative face looked culturally specific.[^s6] Pizziconi's re-analysis concludes that the positive and negative face constructs survive this critique, but she agrees the model over-emphasises strategic calculation relative to fixed social norms and under-develops how impositions are weighed; the real cultural differences lie in what counts as face and how overtly it must be attended to.[^s6] Treat the model as a strong heuristic for diagnosing a phrasing choice, not a formula that predicts what will feel polite in every culture.

## Connections

Politeness theory rests on face and facework, so that article is a prerequisite; it was introduced by Penelope Brown and Stephen C. Levinson in *Politeness: Some universals in language usage*.[^s1] It is studied within pragmatics, where the debate over its universality has run for decades.[^s6] Its predictions are demonstrated by the Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil et al. (2013) analysis of politeness and power online[^s5] and by Holtgraves and Yang's (1992) experiments on status, distance and imposition,[^s3] and it is challenged by Matsumoto's (1988) critique of negative face in Japanese.[^s6] It underpins choices about register and tone, direct versus indirect feedback, and how you phrase requests when managing up or writing email across a hierarchy.[^s4]
