# Hofstede's dimensions of national culture

Hofstede's model describes national cultures as positions on a small set of value dimensions: power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualism versus collectivism, masculinity versus femininity, long- versus short-term orientation, and indulgence versus restraint.[^s1] Each country receives a score on each dimension, derived from averaging survey answers across many respondents, so the scores describe a society relative to other societies rather than any single person in it.[^s1][^s2] It is the most cited comparative framework in cross-cultural management, and the most argued about.[^s3][^s5]

## Origin

Geert Hofstede joined IBM in 1965 as a manager of personnel research and ran an employee opinion survey across more than 70 national subsidiaries, eventually holding well over 100,000 questionnaires on pay, working conditions, job security and relations with bosses and colleagues.[^s2] Averaging the answers by country revealed recurring patterns, which became the first four dimensions and were published in *Culture's Consequences* in 1980.[^s2] A fifth, long-term orientation, came from work with Michael Harris Bond around 1980, and a sixth, indulgence versus restraint, from Michael Minkov's analysis of World Values Survey data; only the 2010 edition of *Cultures and Organizations* contains all six.[^s2] In his own retrospective, Hofstede stresses that dimensions depend on the level of aggregation and that national-level dimensions should not be confused with differences between individuals.[^s1]

## Why it matters

In plain workplace terms, the dimensions name differences that otherwise surface as friction:[^s2]

- **Power distance** — how far people accept unequal power and deference to bosses; it shapes whether a junior analyst will openly correct a director in a meeting.[^s2]
- **Uncertainty avoidance** — how a group copes with ambiguity and anxiety; Hofstede's team notes it is not simply about rules or risk.[^s2]
- **Individualism–collectivism** — whether personal initiative or the group comes first, which affects how praise and credit land.[^s2]
- **Masculinity–femininity** — an emphasis on pay, recognition and status versus cooperation and good relationships; despite the label, the survey asked nothing about gender roles.[^s2]
- **Long- vs. short-term orientation** — thrift and perseverance versus respect for tradition and quick results.[^s2]
- **Indulgence–restraint** — how freely people pursue enjoyment and report life satisfaction.[^s2]

Used well, the model gives a team shared vocabulary for discussing expectations — "we seem to differ on how much hierarchy we expect" — instead of reading a colleague's caution or bluntness as a character flaw.[^s1][^s2] Used badly, it turns a country average into a prediction about the person on the call, which is exactly the inference the research warns against.[^s4]

### Using it at work without stereotyping

1. Treat a country score as a hypothesis about the room, not a fact about a person; confirm by asking how this team likes to disagree, decide and give feedback.[^s4]
2. Check occupation and seniority before nationality: a meta-analysis found occupation explained about half of the clustering in power-distance values, far more than country did.[^s4]
3. Compare relatively. Scores locate one society against another, so the useful question is how your own habits differ from your counterpart's, not where they sit on a scale.[^s1]
4. Expect the strongest signal in feelings and attitudes — commitment, team attitudes, feedback seeking — and a weaker one in individual job performance.[^s3]

## Evidence

The model has broad but modest support. A 2010 meta-analysis of 598 studies and more than 200,000 people found that individuals' cultural values predicted organisational outcomes with an average absolute correlation of about 0.18.[^s3] Values were weaker predictors than personality and demographics for job performance, absenteeism and turnover, but stronger for organisational commitment, citizenship behaviour, team-related attitudes and feedback seeking, and effects were larger in culturally "tight" countries and among managers than students.[^s3]

The central dispute is whether nations are the right unit. A 2016 meta-analysis of 558 studies across 32 countries found that roughly 80% of the variance in Hofstede-type values lay within countries and only about 16–21% between them, concluding that country is a poor proxy for an individual's culture.[^s4] Occupation, socio-economic status and economic conditions such as wealth and freedom often grouped values better than borders did, although country did better for uncertainty avoidance and masculinity.[^s4] Earlier, Brendan McSweeney challenged the core assumptions behind the model, questioning whether national cultures can plausibly act as systematic causes.[^s5] Course summaries of his critique add that one company's employees cannot typify a nation and that a questionnaire cannot capture culture's main dimensions.[^s6] Hofstede himself agreed that national scores should not be applied to individuals.[^s1] The status here is therefore `contested`: the dimensions are a reasonable map of group-level tendencies, but popular use as a guide to individual behaviour outruns the evidence.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a framework within `cm:intercultural-communication`, the field that studies how cultural background shapes messages and their reception.[^s1]
- **Introduced by / in:** Geert Hofstede, from the IBM survey reported in *Culture's Consequences* (1980).[^s2]
- **Supported by:** the Taras, Kirkman and Steel (2010) meta-analysis, which found consistent though modest predictive validity.[^s3]
- **Challenged by:** Taras, Steel and Kirkman (2016) on within-country variance, and McSweeney (2002) on the model's assumptions about national culture.[^s4][^s5]
- **Related:** Hall's `cm:high-and-low-context-cultures` and Meyer's `cm:the-culture-map` offer alternative country maps with the same stereotyping risk; power distance in particular bears on `cm:direct-vs-indirect-feedback` and on how `cm:multicultural-team` members speak up, and over-reading national scores feeds `cm:ethnocentrism`.[^s1][^s4]
