# Face and facework

*Face* is the positive social value a person claims for themselves in an encounter: the image of a competent, decent, likeable self that they expect the others present to honour.[^s1] Goffman stressed that this image is only on loan from the people we are with, which is why it can be lost in a moment. *Facework* is what people do to keep their conduct consistent with that image, to avoid threatening other people's, and to repair the damage when an incident happens.[^s1][^s4] At work, facework is the reason a manager says "could we take another look at this section?" rather than "this section is wrong", and the reason a public correction in a meeting can derail a conversation that a private one would have settled.[^s2]

## Origin

Sociologist Erving Goffman introduced the idea in his 1955 essay "On Face-Work", published in *Psychiatry* and later collected in *Interaction Ritual*.[^s1] He described two kinds of facework. *Avoidance* keeps threats from arising: staying away from risky topics, hedging claims, and tactfully overlooking another person's slip. The *corrective* process repairs face once an incident is acknowledged, in a ritual sequence: someone flags the problem, the offender makes an offering (an explanation, a reframing, compensation or an apology), the others accept it, and the forgiven person shows gratitude.[^s1]

Linguists Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson built a universal model of politeness on Goffman's idea, first as a 1978 essay and then as the 1987 book *Politeness*, drawing on English, Tamil and Tzeltal.[^s6] They split face in two: *positive face*, the wish to be approved of and liked, and *negative face*, the wish not to be imposed on.[^s2] Communication scholar Stella Ting-Toomey later developed face-negotiation theory, which ties face concerns to culture and conflict style and distinguishes concern for one's own face, the other person's face and mutual face.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Many routine workplace messages are what politeness researchers call face-threatening acts. Criticism, interrupting and raising a sensitive topic threaten the listener's positive face; requests, advice and orders threaten their negative face by imposing on their time or autonomy.[^s2] Apologising and admitting a mistake threaten the speaker's own face, which is part of why they are hard to do.[^s2] When face is threatened carelessly, people defend their image instead of engaging with the substance; when it is handled well, the same hard message can land as help.[^s1][^s3]

Face also explains a large share of cross-cultural friction. In a survey of 768 students in China, Germany, Japan and the United States, concern for one's own face predicted a dominating conflict style, while concern for the other person's face predicted avoiding and integrating styles; face concerns carried much of the effect of culture on how people handled conflict.[^s3] The collectivist samples reported more concern for the other person's face.[^s3]

## Using it at work

Treat face as part of the message, not decoration around it. Before a request or criticism, ask whose face is at stake (yours, theirs or the team's) and whether the issue concerns their wish to be respected or their wish not to be imposed on.[^s2] Choose the setting first: a private conversation removes the audience that makes face loss costly.[^s1][^s2] To protect positive face, show interest in the person's goals and frame the problem as shared; to protect negative face, phrase requests as questions and acknowledge the imposition.[^s2] When something goes wrong, use Goffman's corrective sequence deliberately: name the issue, make a real offering such as an explanation or apology, and accept the other person's offering gracefully rather than extracting more.[^s1]

Example: a project lead notices that a senior colleague's figures in a client deck are out of date. Saying so in the client rehearsal threatens the colleague's competence in front of peers. Messaging beforehand ("I think the Q3 numbers updated last night; want me to swap them in, or would you rather?") delivers the same correction while leaving the colleague in control of the fix.[^s1][^s2] Facework should never become a way to hide the problem: the correction still has to be clear enough to act on.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

Face is a theoretical construct rather than a single testable effect, and its reach is debated. Matsumoto (1988) and other scholars of Japanese argued that negative face, the wish to guard individual territory, reflects a Western, individualist self and fits poorly in cultures where people see themselves mainly as members of a group.[^s5] Pizziconi's re-examination of the Japanese evidence counters that both kinds of face are recognisable there, and that what varies is what counts as face and how much it needs overt attention.[^s5] Empirical tests of face-negotiation theory lean heavily on self-reported, cross-sectional data from college students; Oetzel and Ting-Toomey also could not confirm a separate mutual-face factor, and several of their results contradicted earlier findings.[^s3][^s4] Use cultural tendencies as a prompt to ask, not a stereotype to apply.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Erving Goffman, whose 1955 essay defined face and the avoidance and corrective processes.[^s1]
- **Studied in** pragmatics, where Brown and Levinson's politeness model and its critics treat face as the motive behind polite language.[^s5][^s6]
- **Demonstrated by** Oetzel and Ting-Toomey (2003), whose four-country study linked self- and other-face concerns to conflict styles.[^s3]
- **Challenged by** Matsumoto's (1988) argument that negative face does not carry over to Japanese social life, as discussed by Pizziconi.[^s5]
- **Applied in** difficult conversations, where face concerns shape whether people dominate, avoid or integrate, and in multicultural teams, where face norms differ.[^s3][^s4]
- **Broader / related:** face sits within interpersonal and intercultural communication; politeness theory and face-negotiation theory build on it; it helps explain direct vs. indirect feedback; apologies and social accounts are corrective facework; and defensiveness is a common reaction to face threat.[^s1][^s2][^s4]
