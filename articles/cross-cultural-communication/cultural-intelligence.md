# Cultural intelligence

Cultural intelligence (CQ) is the capability to function effectively in culturally diverse settings.[^s1] Rather than a list of facts about countries, it is a set of four capabilities: **motivational CQ**, the drive to direct and sustain effort in intercultural situations; **cognitive CQ**, knowledge of cultures and how they differ; **metacognitive CQ**, the awareness and strategy you use to acquire, check and revise that knowledge; and **behavioural CQ**, the flexibility to adjust what you say and do in the moment.[^s2] For a communicator, the practical upshot is that knowing a norm is not enough: you also have to want to engage, notice when your interpretation might be wrong, and change your behaviour.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The construct was proposed by P. Christopher Earley and Soon Ang in 2003, and Ang and colleagues later set out the four-factor model, drawing on Sternberg's idea that intelligence has multiple loci.[^s2] In 2007 Ang, Van Dyne and co-authors published the Cultural Intelligence Scale (CQS), cross-validated it with 1,360 respondents across samples, time periods and countries, and tested it in three studies with 794 participants in the USA and Singapore.[^s1] A rival conceptualisation by Thomas and colleagues (2008) also exists, and reviewers note that most research has followed the Earley-Ang version.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Ang and colleagues found a consistent pattern: metacognitive and cognitive CQ predicted cultural judgment and decision making, motivational and behavioural CQ predicted cultural adaptation, and metacognitive and behavioural CQ predicted task performance.[^s1] A 2018 meta-analysis of 167 studies (199 samples, N = 44,155) supported a general CQ factor plus four specific factors, with behavioural and metacognitive CQ linked to observer-rated task performance and motivational CQ to sociocultural adjustment.[^s2] It also found that CQ explained outcomes beyond Big Five personality, cognitive ability and emotional intelligence, which is why it is treated as a distinct, learnable capability rather than a personality trait.[^s2] A separate meta-analysis summarised by the University of Southern Denmark (74 studies, 20,642 people) links CQ to job satisfaction, cross-cultural adjustment, transformational leadership and job performance in cross-cultural work.[^s6]

## What good looks like

High-CQ communicators treat their first reading of a colleague as a hypothesis. Metacognitive CQ is the part that does this checking, and the meta-analysis found it strengthened the payoff of the other three capabilities; at low metacognitive CQ, cultural knowledge alone was even negatively related to task performance.[^s2] In practice that looks like:

- planning before a cross-cultural meeting which norms (directness, hierarchy, turn-taking, time) might differ, then watching for evidence rather than assuming;[^s2]
- adapting concrete behaviour, such as speech rate, how firmly a request is phrased, or whether to raise disagreement in the room or one-to-one, because behavioural CQ is the factor most directly tied to performance;[^s1][^s2]
- staying engaged when an exchange feels awkward, since motivational CQ is what predicts adjustment over time.[^s1][^s2]

**Example.** A UK-based product lead runs a design review with an engineering team in another country. *Before:* she asks "Any concerns?", hears silence, and records the design as agreed; two weeks later the team reports a blocking issue. *After:* before the meeting she notes that open challenge to a lead may feel risky for this group, so she sends the design a day early, asks each engineer in a short message for one risk they see, and opens the meeting by thanking people for the risks raised. *Why:* she treated silence as ambiguous (metacognitive CQ) and changed the channel and turn-taking (behavioural CQ), so concerns surfaced while they were cheap to fix.[^s1][^s2]

## How to practise it

1. **Name your defaults.** Before an important exchange, list two or three of your own norms that could be read differently (for example, how you say no). This builds the metacognitive habit.[^s2]
2. **Learn the specific context, not just the country.** Ask a colleague or read about this team's working norms; treat national-culture frameworks as starting hypotheses.[^s2][^s3]
3. **Check, then adapt.** Use a clarifying question when a reaction surprises you, and change one concrete behaviour based on the answer.[^s1]
4. **Debrief.** Afterwards, note what you expected, what happened and what you will try next time.[^s2]
5. **Use training as a start, not a finish.** A 2021 meta-analysis of 37 samples (7,040 trainees) found cross-cultural training had a medium effect on adjustment but only small effects on CQ and job performance, with training method moderating the CQ effect, so pair courses with practice and feedback.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The status here is `mixed`. The predictive evidence is large and consistent, but a 2018 review of 142 empirical articles asked whether CQ is universal or culture-specific, why objective measures are lacking, and how far it can be developed.[^s4] Most studies rely on the self-report CQS; the meta-analysis authors also caution that self-rated adaptation may capture a feeling of fitting in more than observed adaptation, and that heterogeneity across studies was substantial.[^s2] A systematic review of 73 articles flags a broader construct-clarity problem in intercultural competence research and the field's heavy reliance on one conceptualisation.[^s3] Treat a CQ score as a conversation starter, not a verdict on someone's ability.

## Connections

CQ is one model within the wider family of `cm:intercultural-competence` and draws on `cm:intercultural-communication` research.[^s3] It was introduced by `cm:soon-ang` with Earley and first validated in `cm:ang-et-al-2007`.[^s1][^s2] `cm:rockstuhl-van-dyne-2018` supports the four-factor structure, while `cm:fang-schei-selart-2018` challenges how well CQ is measured and how far it can be developed.[^s2][^s4] Its metacognitive checking, which treats first impressions as hypotheses, is a natural counterweight to `cm:ethnocentrism`, judging others by your own group's norms.[^s2] Its payoff shows up most in a `cm:multicultural-team`, where the SDU meta-analysis links CQ to adjustment and performance.[^s6] Knowledge frameworks such as `cm:high-and-low-context-cultures`, `cm:hofstede-cultural-dimensions` and `cm:direct-vs-indirect-feedback` feed cognitive CQ, and `cm:describe-analyze-evaluate` and `cm:perspective-taking` are concrete ways to exercise its metacognitive side.[^s2] CQ's demonstrated incremental validity over `cm:emotional-intelligence` marks it as a separate construct.[^s2]
