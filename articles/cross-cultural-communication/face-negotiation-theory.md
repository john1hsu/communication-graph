# Face-negotiation theory

Face-negotiation theory explains why the same disagreement can be handled bluntly by one colleague and quietly sidestepped by another. Its starting point is that people everywhere care about face, the public self-image they want others to respect, and that conflict puts face at risk.[^s4] What differs is whose face a person protects first: their own (self-face), the other party's (other-face), or both (mutual face). The theory links those face concerns to culture and to how individuals see themselves, and then links face concerns to conflict behaviour.[^s1]

## Origin

Stella Ting-Toomey began building the theory in the early 1980s because mainstream conflict research, mostly American, treated open confrontation and self-disclosure as the healthy default.[^s1] It first appeared in a 1985 chapter on conflict and culture, was set out in full in 1988, was expanded with Atsuko Kurogi in 1998 into a version on intercultural facework competence with seven assumptions and 32 propositions, and was revised again in 2005 as "the matrix of face".[^s1][^s2] It draws on Erving Goffman's account of face and on Brown and Levinson's politeness theory, but shifts the focus from polite phrasing to conflict.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The theory makes three linked predictions. First, members of individualistic cultures, and people with an independent self-construal, lean toward self-face concern, while members of collectivistic cultures and people with an interdependent self-construal lean toward other-face or mutual-face concern; power distance shapes whether facework is informal and horizontal or formal and vertical.[^s1] Second, those concerns map onto clusters of facework: self-face onto dominating moves such as defending a position, other-face onto avoiding moves such as giving in, pretending nothing is wrong or seeking a third party, and mutual face onto integrating moves such as apologising, talking privately, staying calm and problem-solving.[^s4] Third, relational and situational factors, such as whether the other person is in-group or out-group and higher or lower in status, change which facework is expected.[^s1]

Later versions name the kinds of face that can be threatened, including autonomy, status, inclusion, competence and reliability.[^s6] That list is useful at work because it tells you what a conflict may really be about: a dispute over a deadline can turn out to be a threat to someone's competence face, or to their wish to be included in the decision.[^s6]

## Using it at work

Suppose a project lead in one office sends a public channel message saying a partner team's estimate is wrong, and the partner team goes silent. Read through the theory, the lead acted from self-face and treated directness as efficient, while the partner team read a public challenge to their competence and chose avoidance rather than a public counter-argument.[^s4] A face-aware repair moves the conversation private, acknowledges the partner team's expertise before raising the numbers, and asks a joint question about how to close the gap, which is the integrating cluster the theory associates with mutual face.[^s4] Conflict practitioners give similar advice for negotiations: leave the other side a dignified way to change position, avoid public rebuffs, pair any hard demand with a respectful gesture, and use a respected third party when a direct concession would cost too much face.[^s5] Ting-Toomey's own prescription is competence built from culture-specific knowledge, mindful attention to your own gut reactions, and skills such as mindful listening, reframing and mutual-face dialogue.[^s1]

## Evidence

The core path has empirical support. In a survey of 768 students from China, Germany, Japan and the United States, Oetzel and Ting-Toomey (2003) found that independent self-construal went with self-face and interdependent self-construal with other-face, that self-face predicted a dominating style and other-face predicted avoiding and integrating, and that face concerns carried much of culture's effect on conflict style.[^s2] Not every proposition held: self-face did not predict integrating, and the authors note cross-sectional self-report data, recalled rather than observed conflicts, student samples and some unreliable scales in particular countries.[^s2] A textbook review of the research describes the support as partial and names reliance on self-reports from college students as the theory's main weakness.[^s4]

Other work shows that context can outweigh culture. Cingöz-Ulu and Lalonde (2007) found that the type of relationship mattered more than nationality in Turkish and Canadian conflict strategies, and that several results ran against the collectivist-avoids, individualist-forces pattern reported in Holt and DeVore's 2005 meta-analysis.[^s3] Qualitative work on real email exchanges also argues that survey methods say little about what actually happens in a conflict, while still finding face needs at the heart of the dispute.[^s6] Treat the theory as a well-supported lens for diagnosing what is at stake for each person, not as a rule that a given nationality will avoid or confront.

## Connections

Face-negotiation theory was introduced by Stella Ting-Toomey[^s1] and builds on Erving Goffman's idea of face and on politeness theory.[^s2] It requires the concepts of face and facework and of individualism and collectivism, and it sits within intercultural communication, where communication-theory textbooks place it.[^s4] Its main path is demonstrated by Oetzel and Ting-Toomey (2003),[^s2] and its culture-level predictions are challenged by Cingöz-Ulu and Lalonde (2007), who found relationship context mattered more.[^s3] It is applied in negotiation, where face-saving exits and face-giving gestures can matter as much as the substance,[^s5] and in difficult conversations and workplace conflict, where it helps explain why a direct challenge triggers silence rather than debate.[^s1] It complements politeness theory, power distance, direct versus indirect feedback and conflict-mode frameworks such as Thomas-Kilmann, and it informs intercultural competence in multicultural teams.[^s1]
