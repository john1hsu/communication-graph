steps:
  - Book a qualified, independent interpreter early - not a bilingual colleague, the client's relative or the other side's interpreter - and check for any conflict of interest.
  - Choose the mode with the interpreter (consecutive for small meetings, simultaneous with a team of two for long or large ones) and plan for the meeting to take up to twice as long.
  - Brief the interpreter beforehand on the purpose, who will attend, sensitive topics and technical terms, and send the agenda, slides or documents in advance.
  - At the start, introduce the interpreter, explain that they will render everything faithfully and confidentially, and make clear that you, not the interpreter, run the meeting.
  - Look at and speak to the other person in the first person ("What do you need from us?"), never "Ask him what he needs".
  - Speak one or two complete sentences at a time, pause, and avoid idioms, jargon, acronyms and jokes that do not translate.
  - Assume every word in the room will be interpreted, including asides to your own team, and ask for the other side's side-discussions to be interpreted too.
  - Check understanding by asking the other person to restate key decisions or next steps, then debrief the interpreter afterwards on anything they noticed or found difficult.
example:
  situation: A UK operations manager meets a supplier's production director in Japan to agree a corrective-action plan, with an interpreter the supplier arranged.

(pause) What would you need from us to reach that? ... Could you tell me the three actions we have agreed, so I am sure I have understood them?"

# Working with interpreters

Working with interpreters is the set of habits that lets you hold a real conversation when you and the other party do not share a language and a third person carries the meaning between you. It covers choosing the interpreter, briefing them, how you speak while they work, and how you check that the message arrived. The interpreter's job is to render everything faithfully, without adding, omitting, changing or advising; your job is to make that possible and to keep running the conversation yourself.[^s1][^s6]

## Origin

The guidance comes from two professional traditions. Interpreting bodies and public-service providers, such as Australia's national accreditation authority NAATI and the interpreting provider VITS, publish protocols for appointments and meetings: brief the interpreter, introduce their role, speak directly in the first person, use short segments and allow extra time.[^s1][^s6] In international business, negotiation scholar Jeswald Salacuse's *The Global Negotiator* (2003) argued that executives should bring their own vetted interpreter, brief them on the deal and the translation style wanted, and watch for interpreters who steer talks for their own interests.[^s2] Planning professor Sanda Kaufman, writing on the interpreter as an intervener, adds that interpreters are never perfectly neutral: their word choices in the gap between the parties can tilt meaning.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Interpreting errors are frequent and consequential. In an audiotaped study of 13 paediatric clinic visits, Flores and colleagues counted 396 interpreter errors, an average of 31 per visit; most were omissions, and errors by ad hoc interpreters (nurses, a social worker and an 11-year-old sibling) were more likely to carry potential clinical consequences than those by hospital interpreters (77% vs. 53%).[^s5] A 2007 systematic review of 28 studies found that professional interpreters were associated with better communication, comprehension, use of services, outcomes and satisfaction than ad hoc interpreters, bringing care close to that of patients without a language barrier.[^s4] In negotiations the stakes are commercial: a mistranslation can sink a request, and a team that must wait for interpretation has less time to think than a side that already understood.[^s2][^s3]

## How to apply it

Choose and brief well before the meeting. Use a qualified, independent interpreter, and in a negotiation bring your own rather than relying on the other side's, so you can check accuracy and follow their side-conversations.[^s2][^s3] Decide on consecutive interpreting (pauses after each segment, roughly double the time) or simultaneous interpreting (real time, usually two interpreters to manage fatigue), and send the agenda, documents and specialist terms in advance.[^s1][^s6]

During the meeting, introduce the interpreter, explain their role and confidentiality, and keep control of the agenda yourself.[^s6] Look at and speak to the other person in the first person, in one or two complete sentences at a time, without slang, idioms or acronyms, and assume everything said in the room will be interpreted.[^s1] Afterwards, check that decisions were understood and offer the interpreter a debrief, especially after a tense or emotional session.[^s6]

## Common mistakes

The most damaging mistake is the convenient ad hoc interpreter: a bilingual colleague, a family member or the counterpart's staff, whose errors are more likely to matter and whose loyalties may be divided.[^s5][^s2] Others are talking to the interpreter about the other party ("tell her that..."), delivering long monologues that force the interpreter to summarise, making private remarks to your own team that are then interpreted, asking the interpreter for their opinion, and treating a nod or a "yes" as proof of agreement.[^s1][^s3] Kaufman also cautions against substituting machine translation for a skilled human interpreter in high-stakes talks.[^s3]

## Evidence

The strongest evidence comes from healthcare. It consistently favours professional over ad hoc interpreters, but it is largely observational, and the review's included studies vary in design and quality.[^s4] The Flores study is small (13 encounters in one clinic, Spanish only) and measures potential rather than confirmed harm.[^s5] Delivery advice such as first-person address and short segments rests on professional consensus rather than controlled trials, and its transfer from clinics to business meetings and negotiations is reasoned rather than tested; hence `mixed`.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Working with interpreters addresses language barriers, which professional interpreting reduces in the systematic-review evidence, and it is supported by that review and demonstrated by the Flores error study.[^s4][^s5] It is applied in negotiation, where Salacuse and Kaufman tailor it to protect your side's accuracy and thinking time, and in client appointments, where NAATI's protocol is written for service providers meeting clients.[^s2][^s3][^s1] It complements communicating across languages: the same plain, idiom-free, short-segment delivery that helps second-language speakers also makes an interpreter's work accurate.[^s1] Related practices include teach-back for checking understanding, negotiation preparation, plain language and deliberate pauses.
