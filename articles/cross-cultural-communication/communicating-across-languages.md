steps:
  - Before the meeting or message, find out which participants are working in a second language and how fluent they are, rather than guessing from accent or job title.
  - Speak a little slower than feels natural, articulate word endings and pause between ideas; do not raise your volume.
  - Swap idioms, sports metaphors, slang and phrasal verbs for plain single words ("cancel", not "call off"; "start", not "kick off").
  - Pick one term for each key thing and keep using it, spell out acronyms on first use, and put numbers, dates and decisions in writing as well as saying them.
  - Check understanding by inviting people to restate the next step or decision in their own words, not by asking "Does that make sense?".
  - Manage airtime - leave pauses after questions, invite quieter second-language speakers by name or in the chat, and judge contributions on substance rather than fluency.
  - For important written content, test it with a reader who has limited English or run it through a translation tool and back to spot phrases that break.
example:
  situation: A UK-based project lead updates a video call of engineers in Brazil, Poland and Japan about a vendor delay.
  before: "Quick heads-up - the vendor's dropped the ball again, so we'll have to push back the go-live and play it by ear. Let's touch base Thursday and circle back once we've ironed out the kinks. Make sense?"
  after: "The vendor delivered the test server late. So we will move the launch from 3 March to 17 March. I will put both dates in the chat. On Thursday we will meet again to fix the remaining problems. Ana, could you say back what changes for your team?"

# Communicating across languages

Communicating across languages means deliberately adapting your speech and writing when some of the people you work with are using a shared working language - usually English - that is not their first. It covers the speaker's side (pace, articulation, word choice), the writer's side (short sentences, consistent terms, no idioms) and the facilitator's side (checking understanding and sharing airtime), and it applies to second-language speakers addressing each other as much as to native speakers.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

English has become the main lingua franca of global business, mostly within the last three decades, and Harvard Business School professor Tsedal Neeley has argued that companies therefore need a deliberate language strategy that prepares native and non-native speakers alike to work in multilingual teams.[^s1] The adjustments themselves are described in linguistics as accommodation: research tracing back to speech and communication accommodation theory treats adapting your speech to a listener as convergence towards them, and warns that overdoing it becomes condescension or over-accommodation.[^s3] Government content designers have turned similar adjustments into concrete writing rules for audiences with limited English.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Language gaps do more than slow a meeting down. In a study based on 90 interviews across 15 multinational teams in three German automotive firms, Tenzer, Pudelko and Harzing found that members' cognitive and emotional reactions to language barriers shaped how trustworthy colleagues seemed and whether people intended to trust them; unlike other kinds of diversity, the effects of language were uniformly negative.[^s2] Neeley reports that highly skilled professionals often feel reduced to a childlike level when forced to work in a second language and worry about being judged on language rather than job skill, which changes how much they contribute.[^s1] Managers can also misjudge people in the other direction, promoting the most fluent speaker rather than the most capable one.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Start with your listeners: learn who is working in a second language and how comfortable they are, and adjust to that level instead of to an assumption.[^s3] When speaking, slow down slightly, enunciate and pause between ideas; native speakers who report accommodating most often use clearer enunciation, fewer idioms and colloquialisms and a slower pace, while very few think shouting helps.[^s3] When writing, aim for sentences averaging about 15 words in the active voice, replace idioms and phrasal verbs with single plain words, keep to simple tenses, avoid contractions and spell out acronyms; running important text through translation tools and back, and testing it with readers who have limited English, catches phrases that break.[^s4] In meetings, put dates, numbers and decisions in writing as well, check understanding through restatement, and watch who gets airtime so that quieter second-language speakers are not crowded out.[^s1][^s3]

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is not adjusting at all, because fluent speakers rarely notice their own idioms and speed. The opposite mistake is over-accommodation: speaking very loudly, using broken "foreigner talk" or simplifying content until it feels patronising; about a fifth of native speakers surveyed by Drljača Margić worried that accommodation can come across this way or lower the quality of the conversation.[^s3] Asking "Does that make sense?" invites a polite yes rather than revealing confusion, which is why restatement is safer.[^s3] Treating fluency as a proxy for competence or leadership potential is another trap.[^s1] Finally, plain English is not automatically plain for second-language readers: some everyday words such as "deadline" or "get in touch" translate badly.[^s4]

## Evidence

The evidence that language barriers damage trust and participation in multinational teams comes from qualitative field research rather than controlled tests of the remedies, and the best-known accommodation survey relies on native speakers' self-reports, with the author noting that how second-language listeners perceive the adjustments still needs study.[^s2][^s3] The specific techniques are therefore best treated as practitioner and government consensus.[^s4] One popular related claim - that a foreign accent makes statements sound less credible, from Lev-Ari and Keysar's 2010 experiments - has a mixed replication record: some direct replications found no effect, others partial effects, and a 2024 study with 60 listeners found no credibility penalty in truth judgements even though accented speakers were rated lower on several social traits.[^s5] Judge second-language colleagues on substance, and do not assume listeners will be fair by default.
