# Individualism and collectivism

Individualism and collectivism describe where people place the self. In individualist settings a person is defined mainly by their own goals, freedoms and achievements; in collectivist settings identity rests on membership of a group, and the group's goals come first, which one workplace textbook sums up as "I" versus "We".[^s1] At work the difference shows up in who decides, how openly people say what they think, and whose reputation a remark is felt to affect.[^s1]

## Origin

The contrast entered management research as one of the four original value dimensions in Geert Hofstede's *Culture's Consequences*, the IBM-survey framework whose influence a 2010 meta-analysis reviewed across three decades of studies.[^s5] Harry Triandis and colleagues then refined the idea at the level of individuals. In a 1998 paper, Triandis and Michele Gelfand argued that each orientation comes in a horizontal form that stresses equality and a vertical form that stresses rank, giving four patterns: horizontal individualism (self-reliance without status-seeking), vertical individualism (competing for distinction), horizontal collectivism (interdependence among equals) and vertical collectivism (deferring to the in-group and its authorities).[^s2] They also found that most existing scales captured mainly the horizontal forms.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The orientation changes what a "normal" message sounds like. Workplace guidance links individualism with independent decisions, prizing personal initiative and readily speaking one's mind, and collectivism with group decisions, valuing stability and weighing how one's words reflect on the team.[^s1] It also shapes face: drawing on Ting-Toomey's face-negotiation theory, the same textbook notes that individualists mostly guard their own face, while collectivists also guard their group's face and the face of the person they are talking to, which favours indirect, relationship-preserving phrasing.[^s1]

Consider a regional manager who announces a quarterly win on an all-hands call by naming one engineer as "the hero who saved the launch." In a team leaning towards horizontal collectivism, the engineer may feel exposed and colleagues may feel erased; a version more likely to land credits the squad first, names specific contributions in writing afterwards, and asks the engineer privately how they prefer to be recognised.[^s1][^s2] The same habit can misfire the other way: a manager who only ever praises "the team" may leave colleagues who define themselves by their own goals and achievements feeling unseen.[^s1]

### Working with it without stereotyping

1. Treat a country label as a weak guess; ask people how they prefer to receive credit, feedback and decisions, and watch what they do.[^s4][^s6]
2. Distinguish horizontal from vertical forms: someone can be group-minded yet dislike hierarchy, or individualistic yet uninterested in status.[^s2]
3. When a colleague's indirect reply puzzles you, consider that they may be protecting the group's or your face rather than hiding information.[^s1][^s3]
4. Offer both individual and group channels for input and recognition so the process does not reward only one orientation.[^s1][^s5]

## Evidence

The link to communication style holds best at the individual level. A 1996 cross-cultural survey study by William Gudykunst, Stella Ting-Toomey and colleagues found that cultural individualism-collectivism affected low- and high-context communication both directly and through people's self-construals and values, but individual self-construals and values predicted communication style better than the culture-level label did.[^s3]

Large reviews cut the popular picture down to size, which is why the status here is `mixed`. A 2002 meta-analysis by Daphna Oyserman and colleagues found European Americans more individualistic and less collectivistic than other groups on average, yet not more individualistic than African Americans or Latinos, and not less collectivistic than Japanese or Koreans; among Asian samples, only Chinese participants differed strongly.[^s4] Effects were moderate on self-concept and relationality and larger on attribution and cognitive style.[^s4] A 2010 meta-analysis of 598 studies and over 200,000 people found that Hofstede-type values predicted workplace outcomes with an average absolute correlation of about 0.18; they did better than personality on outcomes such as team-related attitudes and feedback seeking, and worse on job performance.[^s5] A companion meta-analysis by Piers Steel and Vasyl Taras estimated that only about 3% of the variance in individualism scores lay between countries, with individual characteristics such as socioeconomic status and age carrying more of it, and national wealth linked to higher country-level individualism.[^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a construct within `cm:intercultural-communication`, and one dimension (`partOf`) of `cm:hofstede-cultural-dimensions`, introduced by Geert Hofstede in *Culture's Consequences*.[^s5]
- **Demonstrated by:** Gudykunst et al. (1996), who traced how individualism-collectivism and self-construals shape low- versus high-context speaking styles.[^s3]
- **Supported by:** Triandis and Gelfand (1998), whose measures confirmed four distinct horizontal and vertical patterns.[^s2]
- **Challenged by:** Oyserman, Coon and Kemmelmeier (2002), whose meta-analysis contradicted several familiar country contrasts, and Steel and Taras (2010), who found little of the variance in individualism at country level.[^s4][^s6]
