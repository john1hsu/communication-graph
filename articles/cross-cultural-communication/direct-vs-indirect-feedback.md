# Direct vs. indirect feedback

Direct feedback states a problem plainly - "this draft isn't ready" - and in some cultures is sharpened with upgraders such as "absolutely" or "totally". Indirect feedback cushions the same message with downgraders such as "kind of" or "a little", with understatement ("not quite there yet" for work that is far from done), or by praising what worked and leaving the listener to infer the rest.[^s1][^s2] Neither style is wrong; the risk is that a message encoded in one style is decoded in the other, so a warning is heard as a suggestion or a routine correction is heard as an attack.[^s1]

## Origin

The contrast between explicit and implicit messages has long roots in intercultural communication research, and it was popularised for managers by INSEAD professor Erin Meyer, who sorted cultures by how plainly they deliver negative feedback and borrowed the linguists' terms upgraders and downgraders to describe how they do it.[^s1][^s2] Meyer places managers from countries such as the Netherlands, Germany, Denmark, Israel, Russia and France toward the direct end, and notes a twist: Americans, often seen as direct, tend to cushion criticism with several positives and superlatives.[^s1] Her examples are drawn from executives' experiences rather than systematic measurement.[^s1][^s2] The distinction appears as the Evaluating scale in her 2014 book *The Culture Map*, which treats directness in criticism separately from directness in general.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Miscalibrated directness causes real workplace damage in both directions. In Meyer's accounts, a German finance director read his British boss's polite "suggestion" as optional when it was a warning, and a Korean manager in the Netherlands who copied local bluntness was seen as aggressive by Dutch colleagues.[^s2] She also describes an American whose video critique of Thai colleagues was treated as bullying by the Bangkok HR team, and a Ukrainian manager reprimanded in the U.S. for copying others on a blunt criticism.[^s1] Because feedback that stings for the wrong reason pulls attention toward the relationship and away from the work, getting the encoding right is part of making feedback usable at all.[^s1][^s5]

## How to apply it

When receiving, decode before reacting: mentally strip out the upgraders and downgraders and ask what the core message is.[^s2] When giving, listen to the softeners or intensifiers your counterpart uses and match your wording to their norm rather than yours; Meyer recasts the golden rule as treating others the way they would treat themselves.[^s1] Do not simply imitate a more direct culture, because a newcomer's bluntness often lands harder than a native speaker's.[^s2] Deliver criticism privately, give positive feedback regularly rather than only alongside criticism, and in more indirect settings name what you value before what must change.[^s1][^s2] Channel matters too: Meyer warns that recapping criticism in writing can read as distrust to some recipients, so in those settings say it live and keep any written follow-up to agreed actions.[^s1]

A practical test: after giving feedback across a style gap, ask the person what they will do differently. If their answer is smaller (or bigger) than the problem you meant, your message was decoded in the other style.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

The research supports real but conditional differences. Across four studies, Sanchez-Burks and colleagues (2003) found that Americans became less indirect, and paid less attention to indirect cues, at work than in social settings, while East Asian participants did not; at work Americans were less indirect than Koreans and Chinese, but the groups did not differ outside work.[^s3] A Thai-English bilingual sample showed the same work-specific drop in indirectness only when answering in English, which suggests the pattern is a cultural schema that context can switch on.[^s3] Brew and Cairns (2004), surveying 102 Australian expatriates and East Asian host-nationals in Singapore and Bangkok, found East Asians handled workplace conflict more indirectly only when facing superiors, and that time urgency and the other person's status and culture moved choices away from what nationality predicted; they attributed the remaining gap mainly to power distance.[^s4] A 2025 scoping review of 37 studies of Asian health trainees found that fear of losing face made negative feedback harder to receive, yet trainees still valued even harsh feedback when it came with advice for improvement - a caution against assuming indirect cultures want no criticism.[^s5]

Limits: much popular guidance, including the national rankings, rests on anecdote, and the studies above rely heavily on self-report and student or MBA samples.[^s1][^s3] Treat a country's position as a starting hypothesis about a person, never a verdict.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

- **Supported by** Sanchez-Burks et al. (2003), which showed East-West differences in indirectness at work.[^s3]
- **Challenged by** Brew and Cairns (2004), whose finding that status and urgency override cultural predictions cautions against nationality-based rules.[^s4]
- **Explained by** power distance, which Brew and Cairns identify as the main driver of the differences they found, and by face concerns, which the 2025 review links to how hard negative feedback is to receive.[^s4][^s5]
- **Applied in** multicultural teams, the setting Meyer's advice targets.[^s1]
- **Introduced in** Meyer's *The Culture Map* (2014), where it forms the Evaluating scale.[^s6]
- **Broader / related:** it is a dimension of feedback and of intercultural communication; it overlaps with Hall's high- and low-context cultures, and specific-behaviour methods such as Situation-Behavior-Impact and I-statements can be dialled up or down in directness. Meyer's advice to open with positives in indirect settings sits in tension with warnings against the feedback sandwich, so use positives that are sincere and specific rather than a formula.[^s1][^s2]
