# Power distance

Power distance is how far people accept that power, authority and status are distributed unequally.[^s1] In a workplace it is visible in who speaks first, who disagrees in front of whom, and whether a junior colleague offers an idea unprompted or waits to be asked.[^s1] Although it began as a score for national cultures, organisational researchers now treat it as a value that can describe individuals, teams, organisations and nations, each judged by how readily they see inequality as unavoidable or useful.[^s2]

## Origin

The term is best known from Geert Hofstede's model of national culture, built from IBM employee surveys, in which power distance is one of the six dimensions; the 2010 edition of *Cultures and Organizations* by Hofstede, Hofstede and Minkov is the usual reference for the full set.[^s1] A 2014 review in the *Journal of Management* argued that because power sits inside every hierarchy, power distance should also be studied as a property of individuals and groups, and as a boundary condition on many leadership and justice effects rather than only as a country label.[^s2] Researchers therefore distinguish a country's power distance score from an individual's *power distance orientation*, the personal value measured in studies of followers.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Open-textbook summaries of Hofstede's work describe the communication contrast plainly. Where power distance is high, subordinates tend to expect instructions, rarely volunteer opinions unless a manager invites them, and treat seniority, age and title as reasons for automatic respect.[^s1] Where it is low, bosses and staff are more likely to treat each other as equals and people at every level feel freer to say what they think.[^s1] The practical consequence is that silence means different things: in one team it signals agreement, in another it signals deference to the most senior person in the room.[^s1][^s3]

Consider a project review on a global video call. A director asks, "Any concerns with the plan?" and hears nothing; two weeks later a regional analyst's private message reveals a data problem she had spotted on day one. A more effective version, consistent with the participation research below, is for the director to ask each site lead by name for one risk, send the question in writing before the call, and say explicitly that challenging the plan is part of the analyst's job.[^s3] The structural invitation does the work that an open question cannot when people expect to defer.[^s3]

### Working with it without stereotyping

1. Treat any country score as a hypothesis about the room, then test it by watching who speaks and asking how the team prefers to raise problems.[^s1][^s5]
2. Check occupation, generation and education before nationality; in a large meta-analysis these grouped power distance values about as well as, or better than, country did.[^s5]
3. Build participation into the process (named turns, pre-reads, anonymous input) rather than relying on a formal "open door" alone.[^s3]
4. Expect people who are more comfortable with hierarchy to respond to leaders differently, and adjust how you explain decisions to them.[^s4]

## Evidence

The core contrast replicates at the group level. A study of 9,400 commercial airline pilots in 19 countries reproduced Hofstede's power distance index with a correlation of 0.87, and concluded that national culture influenced cockpit behaviour beyond pilots' shared professional culture, so one-size-fits-all crew training was inappropriate.[^s6] In 421 units of a multinational spread over 24 countries, formal employee-involvement programmes and a participative climate both reduced opinion withholding in low power distance countries; in high power distance countries, formal involvement was linked to voice only when employees also perceived a strong participative climate.[^s3]

The effects are real but moderated, which is why the status here is `mixed`. At the individual level, a study of 560 followers and 174 leaders in China and the United States found that followers' own power distance orientation changed how strongly transformational leadership translated into perceived procedural justice, with a weaker link for high-orientation followers; country differences did not significantly alter these relationships.[^s4] A 2016 meta-analysis found that only about 16 to 21 percent of variance in Hofstede-type values lay between countries, that occupation, education and generation often clustered power distance at least as well as nationality, and that power distance has been falling over time.[^s5] Country, in short, is a weak proxy for the individual in front of you.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a construct within `cm:intercultural-communication`, and one dimension (`partOf`) of `cm:hofstede-cultural-dimensions`, introduced through Geert Hofstede's IBM research.[^s1]
- **Causes:** high power distance combined with a weak participative climate produces collective `cm:employee-silence`, as Huang, Van de Vliert and Van der Vegt (2005) demonstrated across 24 countries.[^s3]
- **Supported by:** Merritt (2000), whose pilot survey replicated the power distance index.[^s6]
- **Challenged by:** Taras, Steel and Kirkman (2016), who show that country explains only a small share of value differences.[^s5]
- **Related:** power distance helps explain `cm:direct-vs-indirect-feedback` and why `cm:multicultural-team` members speak up unevenly, overlaps with the Leading scale of `cm:the-culture-map`, and interacts with `cm:psychological-safety`, `cm:managing-up`, `cm:unequal-participation` and `cm:face-and-facework` whenever a junior must contradict a senior.[^s2][^s3][^s4]
