# Developmental model of intercultural sensitivity

The developmental model of intercultural sensitivity (DMIS) describes how people's experience of cultural difference becomes more complex as they gain intercultural experience.[^s1] Its six stages run from three ethnocentric positions, where one's own culture is treated as the centre of reality, to three ethnorelative positions, where cultures are seen as alternative ways of organising reality.[^s2] The stages describe how someone perceives difference, not fixed personality types, which is why the model is used to decide what a learner needs next rather than to label them.[^s2]

## Origin

Milton Bennett introduced the model in a 1986 article in the *International Journal of Intercultural Relations*, framing it as a way to sequence intercultural training developmentally.[^s1] He built it by grounded-theory observation of students and corporate staff becoming more competent across cultures, drawing on constructivist theories of perception and communication.[^s2] In 2003 Bennett, Mitchell Hammer and R. Wiseman published the Intercultural Development Inventory (IDI), a Likert-type questionnaire that became the model's main quantitative measure.[^s2]

The six stages, in Bennett's later summary:[^s2]

1. **Denial** - difference is barely noticed or lumped into vague categories ("foreigners"), with little interest in contact.[^s2]
2. **Defense** - the world is split into "us and them", one's own culture is treated as superior; *reversal* is the mirror image, idealising the other culture and disparaging one's own.[^s2]
3. **Minimization** - surface differences are acknowledged but deeper ones are hidden behind "we're all the same underneath", which can also mask privilege.[^s2]
4. **Acceptance** - other cultures are seen as equally complex, without necessarily agreeing with every value; ethical judgement becomes the main challenge.[^s2]
5. **Adaptation** - empathy and perspective-taking let people shift behaviour to fit another cultural context.[^s2]
6. **Integration** - moving between cultural worldviews becomes part of one's identity.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Most workplace cross-cultural friction is not a lack of facts but a way of construing difference, and the DMIS gives managers and trainers a vocabulary for that.[^s1] Its practical claim is that training should match the stage: material that works for someone in acceptance can feel threatening to someone in defense, so content and challenge must be sequenced.[^s1] The Georgetown Consortium study of 1,297 US students found that exposure alone was not enough; gains on the DMIS-based IDI depended on interventions such as cultural mentoring, which supports the model's emphasis on guided development.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Listen for the stage in what people say. "Business is business everywhere" signals minimization; "their engineers are just less rigorous" signals defense.[^s2] Then pick the next step rather than the end goal.[^s1] For a team member in defense, stress shared goals and common ground before highlighting difference; for one in minimization, use concrete cultural contrasts (for example, by comparing how two offices give feedback) so differences become visible without being judged.[^s2] For people in acceptance, the work is practising behaviour shifts in real situations, such as rehearsing how to run a decision meeting with a partner team that expects consensus.[^s2] Pair experience with reflection: the Georgetown data showed students who met a cultural mentor often gained most, while those who spent their time with compatriots gained little.[^s4]

## Evidence

The model itself is a constructivist theory, and Bennett argues it should be judged by coherence and usefulness rather than statistics.[^s2] Tests through the IDI are mixed. Perry and Southwell's 2011 review notes that factor analyses (Paige and colleagues) found better support for a simple two-factor ethnocentric-versus-ethnorelative structure than for the separate stages, that Greenholtz questioned whether the instrument transfers across cultures, and that research does not support strictly linear progression.[^s3] Bennett himself lists critiques that the linear sequence may carry Western bias and that the IDI oversimplifies the construct.[^s2] Treat the stages as a diagnostic lens, not a score, and expect people to sit at different positions in different contexts or to slide back under stress.[^s3]
