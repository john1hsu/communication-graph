steps:
  - Collect real incidents from people who have worked across the two cultures, choosing moments that surprised, annoyed or confused someone.
  - Write each incident as a short scene that describes who did what and how people reacted, without giving the reason.
  - Draft three or four plausible explanations for each scene, including the ones your own culture would reach for first.
  - Ask colleagues from the other culture which explanation best fits, and keep only incidents where they broadly agree.
  - Write feedback for every option that explains why it is or is not likely, naming the underlying norm or value.
  - Have learners work through the incidents alone or in pairs, then debrief what surprised them and test the lessons in real exchanges.
example:
  situation: A project manager onboarding to a supplier team in another country after a tense first week.
  before: "Their engineers never push back in our calls, then miss dates. I'll tell them plainly that silence means agreement and they need to speak up."
  after: "I worked through the team's incident pack. One scene matched ours: silence in a group call often signals a problem the speaker can't raise in front of seniors. I'll ask for risks in writing before each call and check in one-to-one."
  why: The manager swapped a first-instinct explanation (they are passive or careless) for one locals would give, and changed the channel to fit it instead of lecturing the team.
selfCheck:
  - When a colleague's behaviour surprised me, did I list more than one explanation before settling on mine?
  - Would someone from their culture pick the same explanation I picked?
  - Is the lesson I took from an incident a tested hypothesis about this team or a stereotype about a whole country?

# Culture assimilator

A culture assimilator (also called an intercultural sensitizer) is a self-instructional training method made of short episodes, each describing a real intercultural encounter that went wrong or puzzled someone.[^s1] The learner reads the episode, chooses among several possible explanations for what happened, and gets feedback on every choice: a wrong answer explains why it misreads the situation and sends the learner back, while the right answer explains a principle of the other culture.[^s1] The aim is to help you explain a colleague's behaviour the way people from their own culture would, which researchers call making isomorphic attributions.[^s3]

## Origin

The method came out of work by Fred Fiedler, Harry Triandis and colleagues at the Universities of Illinois and Washington in the 1960s, and was first tested with US military, Peace Corps and adviser groups.[^s1] A 1971 technical report describes the classic format: about 75 to 100 episodes gathered from interviews, read over four to six hours, with each correct explanation checked by members of the host culture and only items they agreed on retained.[^s1] Later work broadened the idea. Culture-general assimilators teach themes common to many intercultural encounters, and Dharm Bhawuk built a theory-based version organised around individualism and collectivism.[^s2][^s4]

## Why it matters

Most workplace friction across cultures starts with a confident wrong explanation: a silence read as agreement, a blunt reply read as rudeness, a deferral read as laziness. The assimilator trains the habit of generating and checking alternative explanations before reacting.[^s1][^s4] Earley and Peterson, writing about how to build cultural intelligence (CQ) in managers, describe assimilators as a good fit for the knowledge side of CQ and as an attempt to strengthen its metacognitive side, the habit of questioning your own interpretations.[^s4]

## How to apply it

You can use a published assimilator for a region or build a small one for your own team.[^s1][^s5]

1. **Gather incidents.** Interview people who have worked across the two cultures about moments that surprised or upset them; the 1971 programme drew its episodes from extensive interviews.[^s1]
2. **Describe, don't explain.** Write the scene so the reason is missing, as critical-incident trainers recommend.[^s5]
3. **Offer real alternatives.** Include the explanations your own culture would reach for first, alongside the one locals would give.[^s1]
4. **Validate with insiders.** Keep only items on which members of the other culture broadly agree.[^s1]
5. **Explain every option.** Feedback should name the value or norm behind the behaviour, not just mark an answer right.[^s1]
6. **Debrief and practise.** Pair the reading with discussion or a practice interaction; one study found a short supportive practice session added a small benefit.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating answers as rules.** Earley and Peterson warn that learning may not transfer to situations with different surface details, and that value-based versions can lead managers to overweight one cultural dimension.[^s4]
- **Skipping the learner's own interpretation.** Because options are pre-written, the format asks for less critical analysis than having learners write and analyse their own incidents, which some educators prefer.[^s5]
- **Expecting instant comfort.** In one experiment, training raised trainees' anxiety about upcoming encounters before it fell over time.[^s3]
- **Using one pack for everyone.** Classic assimilators are mostly culture-specific and costly to build, and they assume every trainee needs the same material.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The status here is `mixed`. Early laboratory and field studies, including Peace Corps and military advisers in Honduras, Thailand, Iran and Greece, found that trained people usually learned more about the host culture and reported better adjustment and interpersonal relations, while effects on productivity were weaker and inconsistent.[^s1] In a randomised study of 102 students, Bhawuk's theory-based assimilator produced significant gains in intercultural sensitivity, category width and attribution-making compared with culture-specific and culture-general versions or a control.[^s2] A smaller 1977 experiment found knowledge gains and more enjoyable interactions, but attitude results pointed in both directions.[^s3] Most of these samples are small, old and often students, and none measured CQ with today's scales, so the link to CQ rests on theory more than on direct tests.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

The assimilator is one form of `cm:cross-cultural-training`.[^s1] It was introduced by `cm:fred-fiedler` and `cm:harry-triandis`, and `cm:bhawuk-1998` demonstrates its theory-based version.[^s1][^s2] It develops `cm:cultural-intelligence`, mainly its cognitive and metacognitive sides, and builds the intercultural sensitivity at the heart of `cm:intercultural-competence`.[^s2][^s4] Its first field tests involved leaders of mixed-nationality groups, the situation of a `cm:multicultural-team`.[^s1] By training people to doubt their first explanation, it works against `cm:ethnocentrism` and pairs naturally with `cm:describe-analyze-evaluate` and `cm:perspective-taking`, which ask learners to produce interpretations themselves.[^s4][^s5] For the size of cross-cultural training effects overall, see `cm:li-2021-cross-cultural-training-meta-analysis`.
