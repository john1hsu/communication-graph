steps:
  - When a colleague's behaviour surprises or irritates you, pause before replying and write down only what you saw or heard, in words a camera could confirm.
  - List at least two or three different explanations for that behaviour, including at least one that would make it reasonable in the other person's context.
  - Name your own evaluation and its source - what you feel about the behaviour and which norm you are measuring it against.
  - Check the most important explanation with the person or a cultural informant using a neutral question built on your description.
  - Only then decide how to respond, and if you give feedback, lead with the description rather than the evaluation.
example:
  situation: A team lead in a global project notices that a newly transferred engineer never raises objections in design reviews and later sends concerns privately by email.
  before: "You're not engaging in reviews. If you disagree, say it in the room - sending emails afterwards looks like you're going around the team."
  after: "In the last three reviews you didn't comment during the meeting, then emailed me concerns afterwards - and they were good ones. I can think of a few reasons that might happen. How do design disagreements usually get raised on teams you've worked on?"
  why: The first version presents an evaluation ("not engaging", "going around the team") as fact; the second states the observable pattern, admits several possible explanations and asks before judging, so the engineer can explain a norm the lead may not share.
selfCheck:
  - Could someone who disagrees with me still accept my description of what happened as accurate?
  - Have I written down more than one explanation, including one that would make this behaviour sensible where they come from?
  - Which of my own norms am I using to judge this, and where did I learn it?
  - Am I about to give feedback that starts with my verdict instead of what I observed?

# Describe-analyze-evaluate

Describe-analyze-evaluate (D.A.E.), better known by its older name describe-interpret-evaluate (D.I.E.), is a short discipline for slowing down judgement when someone's behaviour surprises you. You first describe what happened in observable terms, then analyse why it might be happening by generating more than one explanation, and only then evaluate - name how you feel about it.[^s1] The point is not to suppress judgement but to notice that description, interpretation and evaluation are different acts, and that the last two are shaped by your own personal and cultural background.[^s2]

## Origin

The exercise grew out of General Semantics teaching in American speech courses from the 1940s, which trained students to separate facts, inferences and judgements.[^s1] Janet and Milton Bennett, then doctoral students at the University of Minnesota, adapted it in 1975 for intercultural communication workshops, and a version appeared in a facilitators' guide by Bennett, Bennett and Stillings in 1977.[^s1] It became one of the most widely used intercultural classroom activities, typically run by showing an unfamiliar object or photo and asking people to "tell me something about this".[^s2][^s3] In 2008 Kyoung-Ah Nam and John Condon proposed renaming it D.A.E., because "interpret" and "evaluate" are near-synonyms in everyday speech - even facilitators mix them up - and because trainers kept apologising for the D.I.E. acronym.[^s1] Many teaching centres still use the original name.[^s5]

## Why it matters

When an interaction goes differently than expected, people tend to explain it instantly through their own experience and assumptions, often while feeling personally slighted.[^s5] In the original workshop, participants' first answers about an unfamiliar object mix all three kinds of statement, and the hardest part reported afterwards is keeping a description free of judgement.[^s2] That jump from seeing to judging is how ethnocentrism shows up in everyday work: a style that differs from your own is labelled "rude", "evasive" or "unengaged" before anyone has checked what it means.[^s2] Pulling the steps apart creates room to ask instead of assume.

## How to apply it

1. **Describe.** Record only what a camera would catch - words, actions, timing - and aim for a description that people with different views could agree on.[^s1][^s5]
2. **Analyse.** Ask why it might be happening and generate alternatives; in the Bennett version groups must offer at least two interpretations of each scene.[^s2] Nam and Condon stress that several explanations are acceptable at this stage.[^s1]
3. **Evaluate.** Note how you feel about each explanation, and treat that as your reaction rather than a verdict others must share.[^s1] Bennett's handout pairs each interpretation with both a positive and a negative evaluation to show how much the judgement depends on the reading.[^s2]
4. **Check.** Where possible, test your leading explanation against the person's own account - in the classroom version, the photographer's real caption is revealed only at the end.[^s2]

Teaching guides present it as a way to work through a specific miscommunication, including noticing whether you are personalising the incident.[^s5] At work that means running it before a sharp reply, a performance conversation or an escalation; the workshop's core lesson, that describing before judging is essential, also means the description from step 1 makes a non-accusatory opening line for feedback.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

- **Smuggling judgement into the description.** "She was rude in the meeting" is already an evaluation; "she left the meeting without speaking" is a description.[^s2]
- **Stopping at one interpretation.** A single explanation is usually your default one; the value comes from forcing alternatives.[^s1][^s2]
- **Treating evaluation as forbidden.** The model expects you to have feelings and judgements; it only asks you to own them and place them last.[^s1]
- **Confusing the two middle steps.** If "interpret" keeps sliding into "I didn't like it", use the D.A.E. wording and the question "why might this be happening?"[^s1]
- **Running it as a one-off icebreaker.** Williams argues the classic exercise can feel abstract and not action-oriented enough on its own, so it needs to be tied to real situations.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

D.I.E. is endorsed by long practitioner use rather than by controlled trials. Nam and Condon's history presents no outcome data and notes that the General Semantics tradition behind it was itself heavily criticised.[^s1] Williams likewise reports no effectiveness data, and describes the blurred line between interpreting and evaluating as a standing weakness.[^s3] More broadly, a 2020 review of cross-cultural training evaluation found a large gap between how such training should be evaluated and the research that actually exists, and stressed that trainee differences shape what works.[^s4] Treat D.A.E. as a sensible habit for reducing snap judgements, not as a proven cure for bias.

## Connections

- **Broader:** a core teaching tool of intercultural communication, born in the University of Minnesota's intercultural workshops.[^s1]
- **Addresses ethnocentrism:** it is designed to surface participants' own value judgements and show that interpretations and evaluations depend on cultural background.[^s2]
- **Develops intercultural competence:** it is one of the most widely used ways of teaching cultural self-awareness.[^s3]
- **Introduced by Janet and Milton Bennett,** who adapted it for intercultural workshops in 1975.[^s1]
- **Influenced by General Semantics,** the tradition that taught separating facts, inferences and judgements.[^s1]
- **Related:** the ladder of inference makes a similar fact-versus-inference distinction in conflict; humble inquiry supplies the questions for the checking step; high- and low-context communication and direct versus indirect feedback are the style differences most often misjudged without it.
