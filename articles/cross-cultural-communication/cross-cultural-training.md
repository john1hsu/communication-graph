steps:
  - Name the concrete situations the person will face, such as running calls with a supplier team or giving feedback to direct reports abroad, and the outcome you want from training.
  - Assess what the trainee already knows and has experienced across cultures, and skip material they have already mastered.
  - Combine a short culture-specific briefing with culture-general concepts, such as how directness or hierarchy vary, so the lessons travel beyond one country.
  - Add experiential practice, such as role plays of the real situations or critical-incident exercises, with feedback from someone who knows the other culture.
  - Schedule follow-up sessions after arrival or after the first weeks of working together, when real puzzles have surfaced.
  - Include the people around the trainee, such as a relocating partner or the host-country colleagues, when the assignment depends on them.
  - Evaluate the training against working outcomes, not only end-of-course satisfaction, and adjust the next round.

# Cross-cultural training

Cross-cultural training (CCT) is any structured programme that prepares people to communicate, make decisions and build working relationships with people whose cultural norms differ from their own.[^s3] It usually mixes two things: information, delivered through briefings, readings, videos or culture assimilators, and experience, delivered through role plays, simulations, workshops, look-and-see visits or job rotations.[^s5] Content can be culture-specific (how meetings and feedback tend to work in one country) or culture-general (how directness, hierarchy or time orientation vary in any encounter), and it can cover language, leadership and cultural intelligence as well as facts.[^s5]

## Origin

Research on preparing people for work abroad grew in the 1960s and 1970s, faded, and then surged again as globalisation sent more employees on international assignments.[^s3] In 1990 J. Stewart Black and Mark Mendenhall reviewed the literature, concluded that CCT was generally effective, and proposed social learning theory, in which people learn by observing, practising and getting reinforced, as a framework for explaining when it works.[^s1] Two years later Deshpande and Viswesvaran ran one of the first meta-analyses, testing training effects on expatriate managers' self-development, perception, relationships, adjustment and performance, and estimating the programmes' dollar value.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Much cross-cultural contact at work goes badly, and the costs show up as misread silences, damaged client relationships and assignments that end early.[^s1] Good training gives people a better first explanation for a colleague's behaviour and practice at adapting their own, instead of leaving them to learn by trial and error on a live project.[^s3] It also matters for people who never relocate: host-country colleagues, partners and the members of mixed teams who share the same misunderstandings.[^s5]

## How to apply it

1. **Start from the job.** Reviewers recommend tailoring training to the goals of the specific assignment rather than running one generic course.[^s3]
2. **Fit the trainee.** Assess experience and learning style; multicultural or experienced people need different material from first-timers.[^s5]
3. **Mix knowledge and practice.** Didactic and experiential methods complement each other, so pair a briefing with rehearsal of real conversations.[^s5]
4. **Spread it over time.** Pre-departure sessions set realistic expectations, while in-country sessions can address real incidents; one review recommends repeated sessions that follow the adjustment cycle from awareness to coping to experiential learning.[^s5]
5. **Include the wider system.** Partners and host-country nationals shape whether an assignment works and can be trained too.[^s5]
6. **Measure outcomes.** Rigorous evaluation uses pre- and post-measures, comparison groups, longer follow-up and several outcomes, which most programmes skip.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Sophisticated stereotyping.** Pre-departure content that reduces a culture to a few dimension scores can leave people with confident but rigid expectations.[^s5]
- **Knowledge without practice.** Training effects work through observation and rehearsal, so a lecture alone gives trainees little to imitate or try.[^s1]
- **One shot, too early.** Some studies found pre-departure training alone did nothing for adjustment, while post-arrival training helped knowledge more than adjustment, so neither timing should stand alone.[^s5]
- **Assuming more is better.** Support should match needs as they change; heavier programmes are not automatically more effective.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The status here is `mixed`. A 2021 meta-analysis by Li pooled 37 samples covering 7,040 trainees from 1991 to 2021 and found a medium positive effect on adjustment and small positive effects on cultural intelligence and job performance; the training method moderated effects on cultural intelligence, and who rated performance moderated the performance results.[^s4] Earlier reviews reached broadly positive conclusions.[^s1][^s2][^s3] But a 2013 meta-analysis of 28 studies restricted to managers found training linked to higher job satisfaction and commitment and lower intention to quit, yet not to work or interaction adjustment, and only marginally to cultural adjustment; the authors note that earlier meta-analyses drew heavily on soldiers, doctors and students.[^s6] Reviews also flag small and convenience samples, few randomised or longitudinal designs, thin theory, and almost no evidence on whether lessons transfer to daily behaviour on the job.[^s3][^s5]
