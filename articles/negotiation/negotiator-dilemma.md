# Negotiator's dilemma

The negotiator's dilemma is the pull between two things every negotiator has to do at once: **create value** (find trades and options that make the deal bigger for both sides) and **claim value** (secure a good share of whatever is created). Creating value depends on sharing information about interests and priorities; claiming value rewards concealing it. A negotiator who opens up can be exploited by one who does not, so each side is tempted to guard information, and both often leave joint gains on the table.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

The term comes from David Lax and James Sebenius, who set it out in *The Manager as Negotiator* (1986); the chapter was later reprinted in Goldberg, Sander and Rogers' *Dispute Resolution* reader.[^s1][^s2] They framed it like a prisoner's dilemma: if both sides cooperate, both do well; if one cooperates while the other competes, the cooperator does terribly and the competitor does very well; if both compete, both end up mediocre. Because no one wants the worst outcome, competing looks like the safe move, and mutual competition becomes the common result.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Most workplace negotiations - a vendor renewal, a budget split between teams, a job offer - have more than one issue, so there is usually value to create by trading what is cheap for you against what matters to the other side.[^s2] The dilemma explains why that value is so often missed: the hard-bargaining moves of claiming (opening high, conceding slowly, hiding priorities, waiting the other side out) directly block the information flow that finding trades requires.[^s3] It is a strategic barrier, not a personality flaw: a sensible fear of being exploited produces a worse deal for everyone.[^s2]

## Evidence

Lab research suggests the fear is often larger than the risk. In two experiments, Leigh Thompson (1991) found that both asking about the other side's interests and revealing one's own improved judgment accuracy and produced more integrative agreements; it was enough for one negotiator to do it, and those who shared information did not earn less than their counterparts.[^s5] Thompson's sharing concerned priorities across issues, not walk-away points, so the finding does not mean you should reveal your bottom line.[^s5]

The dilemma is also used as a lens for testing remedies. Leonardelli and colleagues (2019) ran six experiments in which offering several equivalent packages at once (MESOs) instead of a single offer gave the offerer stronger anchors and better outcomes, raised joint value and built a more cooperative climate, which the authors read as reducing the dilemma for both sides.[^s4] Most of this evidence comes from simulated negotiations, so effects in high-stakes, repeated or cross-cultural deals may differ.[^s4][^s5]

## How to spot it

- Both sides trade single-number positions on one issue (usually price) while other issues sit untouched.[^s3]
- Each side asks few questions and answers questions with deflections, so neither learns why the other wants what it wants.[^s3][^s5]
- You notice yourself thinking that whoever reveals priorities first loses - the defensive logic at the heart of the dilemma.[^s2]
- The deal closes, and later someone discovers an easy trade that would have helped both sides.[^s1]

## How to fix it

- **Share priorities, protect your limits.** Say which issues matter most and why, and ask the same of them; keep your reservation value to yourself.[^s5]
- **Be conditionally open.** Lax and Sebenius drew on Axelrod's work: start cooperatively, respond firmly if exploited, forgive quickly, and make your approach clear.[^s2]
- **Use the relationship.** Cooperation is easier when future dealings and reputation are at stake, so point to them explicitly.[^s2]
- **Offer choices rather than one position.** Two or three equivalent packages let you anchor and learn the other side's priorities at the same time.[^s1][^s4]
- **Use structure.** A neutral facilitator, a single negotiating text revised from both sides' comments, or a post-settlement review can surface joint gains without either side exposing itself first.[^s1]
- **Stay honest.** Choosing not to disclose something is different from misrepresenting it; the fix never involves bluffing about your interests.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a strategic barrier within **negotiation** (`cm:negotiation`); understanding it **requires** both **integrative** (`cm:integrative-negotiation`) and **distributive negotiation** (`cm:distributive-negotiation`), the two activities it sets against each other.[^s2]
- **Introduced by** **David Lax** (`cm:david-lax`) and **James Sebenius** (`cm:james-sebenius`) in **The Manager as Negotiator** (`cm:the-manager-as-negotiator`).[^s1][^s2]
- **Explained by** the **prisoner's dilemma** (`cm:prisoners-dilemma`), whose payoff logic Lax and Sebenius used to model it.[^s2]
- **Undermines** **integrative negotiation**, because claiming tactics cut off the open exchange that finds mutual gains.[^s3]
- **Demonstrated by** **Leonardelli et al. (2019)** (`cm:leonardelli-et-al-2019`), which tested **multiple equivalent simultaneous offers** (`cm:multiple-equivalent-simultaneous-offers`) as a way to reduce it.[^s4]
- **Challenged by** **Thompson (1991)** (`cm:thompson-1991`), which found that sharing priorities did not cost the sharer, suggesting the dilemma is often weaker than negotiators assume.[^s5]
- **Related:** the **post-settlement settlement** (`cm:post-settlement-settlement`) and **interests vs. positions** (`cm:interests-vs-positions`) are ways around it; the **mythical fixed pie** (`cm:mythical-fixed-pie`) makes it worse by hiding that value can be created; **reciprocal self-disclosure** (`cm:reciprocal-self-disclosure`) and the **norm of reciprocity** (`cm:norm-of-reciprocity`) describe how openness can be matched step by step.[^s1][^s3]
