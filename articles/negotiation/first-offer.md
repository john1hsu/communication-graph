steps:
  - Before the conversation, research the market and estimate the zone of possible agreement - your walk-away point, the other side's likely walk-away point, and what comparable deals settled at.
  - Decide whether to move first - do so when you know the range at least as well as the other side; if they clearly know more, ask questions and let them open.
  - Set the offer from your ambitious target, not from your walk-away point, and make sure it is one you can justify with a real reason.
  - Limit the opening to the issues where your interests conflict; hold back terms you suspect both sides want anyway until you have learned their preferences.
  - State the number precisely and confidently, give the reason in one or two sentences, then stop talking and listen.
  - If they open first, do not respond to their number straight away - restate your own target, their likely limits and your alternatives to yourself, then counter from your plan rather than from their anchor.
example:
  situation: A marketing team lead is buying a six-month analytics retainer from a small agency she has researched; similar retainers she found ranged from $8K to $11K a month.
  before: "We don't have a fixed budget yet - what would you normally charge for something like this?"
  after: "Based on the three proposals we reviewed for similar scope, we'd like to propose $8,500 a month for six months, with the weekly dashboard and quarterly review included. That reflects the market and a six-month commitment from us. How does that sit with you?"

# First offer

A first offer is the opening number or package put on the table in a negotiation. Because people adjust too little away from a starting point, the first concrete figure tends to pull counteroffers and the final agreement towards it - the first-offer effect.[^s1][^s2] Using it well is a two-part decision: whether to move first at all, and, if so, how ambitious to be.[^s5]

## Origin

The idea rests on anchoring research by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in the 1970s and on Gregory Northcraft and Margaret Neale's finding that real estate agents' valuations shifted with randomly assigned list prices.[^s5] Adam Galinsky and Thomas Mussweiler brought it into negotiation in a 2001 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* paper: across three experiments, whichever side moved first - buyer or seller - did better, and first offers strongly predicted final prices, in both face-to-face and email negotiations.[^s1] That overturned the older practitioner rule of always letting the other side name the first number so you can learn from it.[^s5][^s4]

## Why it matters

Workplace negotiations are full of opening numbers: a vendor quote, a salary range, a budget request, a project deadline, a headcount ask. Whoever sets that first reference point shapes what everyone afterwards treats as reasonable.[^s2][^s5] The effect is strongest when the value of the deal is ambiguous, which describes most services, roles and internal resource requests.[^s5] Gunia and colleagues found that low-power negotiators also gained from moving first, so a first offer is one of the few levers available to someone without much formal leverage.[^s6]

## How to apply it

Start from information. PON's guidance is to move first when you understand the zone of possible agreement better than the other side, and to hold back when they clearly know more - a hiring manager usually knows the salary band better than a candidate - or when neither side has a sound basis for a number.[^s5] Maaravi and Levy showed the cost of getting this wrong: when an uninformed party opened, offers were wildly off the market in both directions, and both sides did better when the informed party moved first.[^s4]

When you do open, aim from your target rather than your walk-away point, give a short reason, and keep the opening to the issues where you and the other side want opposite things. Loschelder and colleagues found that offers revealing a preference both parties share hand the other side something to trade back to you, turning the advantage into a disadvantage.[^s3] If the other side opens, the defence from Galinsky and Mussweiler's experiments is to focus deliberately on information that contradicts their anchor - their likely limits, your own target, your alternatives - which eliminated the first mover's advantage.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- Opening from your walk-away point, which leaves no room to move and anchors the deal at your worst acceptable outcome.[^s1][^s2]
- Making an opening so extreme that it insults the other side: the 2025 meta-analysis links ambitious first offers to more impasses and lower satisfaction for the recipient.[^s2]
- Moving first without knowing the market, so the anchor lands far from where an informed counterpart would settle.[^s4]
- Revealing shared preferences in the opening package and then conceding them as if they were costly.[^s3]
- Answering the other side's opening by adjusting from it instead of from your own preparation.[^s1]
- Inventing a justification. A precise, reasoned offer is persuasive only if the reason is true; a made-up competing bid is deception, not anchoring.

## Evidence

A preregistered 2025 meta-analysis in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* pooled 374 effects from 90 studies (N = 16,334). It found a moderate general first-mover advantage (g = 0.42), a strong correlation between the size of the first offer and the agreement value (r = .62), and better outcomes from ambitious rather than moderate openings.[^s2] It also found real costs - ambitious first offers produced fewer deals and lower satisfaction for recipients - and two new preregistered experiments with 2,121 participants replicated both sides.[^s2] The effects shrank as negotiations involved more and different kinds of issues.[^s2] Gunia and colleagues found the effect held across cultures and power levels but operated through distributive issues rather than integrative or compatible ones.[^s6] Because the core effect has replicated at scale, the status here is `robust`, with the qualifications that information asymmetry, compatible issues and extreme openings can reverse it.[^s2][^s3][^s4]

## Connections

The first offer is a distributive-negotiation move whose effect is explained by anchoring and adjustment, through the selective accessibility of anchor-consistent information.[^s2] Galinsky and Mussweiler's 2001 experiments and Gunia and colleagues' cross-cultural studies demonstrate it, and the 2025 meta-analysis supports it.[^s1][^s6][^s2] Loschelder and colleagues' first-mover disadvantage and Maaravi and Levy's information-asymmetry studies challenge the blanket advice to always open.[^s3][^s4] It depends on preparation - knowing your BATNA, reservation value and the zone of possible agreement - and PON applies it to salary and job negotiations, where the candidate often knows less than the employer.[^s5] Multiple equivalent simultaneous offers are a multi-issue variant of the same move, presenting several opening packages at once.
