# Negotiation

Negotiation is what happens whenever you cannot get what you need without someone else's cooperation, and you talk with them to settle the terms.[^s1] Boothby, Cooney and Schweitzer's 2023 review describes it as a social interaction aimed at an agreement that beats the status quo for the people involved.[^s2] Defined that way, it is not a rare, formal event reserved for deal-makers: people negotiate with employers, coworkers, suppliers, friends and their own children, and the results shape prices, salaries and day-to-day arrangements.[^s2] At work, most negotiations are internal and unlabelled - a deadline with another team, headcount with a finance partner, scope with a client, a colleague's time for your project.

## Origin

Empirical study of negotiation began only in the 1960s.[^s2] Thompson, Wang and Gunia's 2010 *Annual Review of Psychology* chapter, drawing mainly on social psychology and organisational behaviour, organised that research by level - what goes on inside one negotiator, between two parties, within groups, across organisations and over virtual channels - and, at each level, by whether a process helps the parties create value (integrative negotiation) or claim it (distributive negotiation).[^s1] The 2023 review argues the field has since matured by finding the moderators of effects once treated as universal, looking closely at the social interaction itself, and studying real-world negotiations rather than only classroom simulations.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Outcomes depend on more than the number you walk away with. Curhan, Elfenbein and Xu asked students, community members and practitioners what they valued after a negotiation, and found four clusters of subjective value: feelings about the instrumental outcome, about oneself (such as saving face), about the process (such as feeling heard) and about the relationship.[^s4] In their validation studies, these subjective feelings predicted later negotiation decisions better than economic results did.[^s4] In a related study summarised by Harvard's Program on Negotiation, 104 MBA students who scored higher on subjective value were more willing to work with the same counterpart again, while the economic outcome alone made no visible difference to that choice.[^s5] For workplace negotiations with people you will see on Monday, that is the central point: how the conversation felt shapes the next one.

## What works here

- **Treat it as two jobs.** Every negotiation mixes creating value (finding trades that make both sides better off) with claiming value (dividing what is on the table); the research is organised around this tension because good negotiators manage both rather than defaulting to one.[^s1]
- **Prepare in detail, especially when you have less power.** The 2023 review notes that detailed planning is one of the few strategies with support for low-power negotiators, and that a positive negotiating mindset - believing negotiation is worthwhile and that you can do it - was the strongest individual predictor of performance it identified.[^s2]
- **Manage the relationship and the process, not only the terms.** Make sure the other person feels heard and can save face, because those feelings carry into future dealings.[^s4][^s5]
- **Choose the channel deliberately.** Negotiators tend to reach more efficient agreements when they can see and hear each other than over text-only channels such as email.[^s2]
- **Get trained and practise.** In ElShenawy's meta-analysis of 57 laboratory experiments from 36 studies, longer negotiation training was associated with much better individual performance (r = .76) than short training (r = .22).[^s3]

A concrete example: a project lead needs two engineers from another team for six weeks. Asking "Can I borrow two people?" frames it as a single take-or-leave request. Preparing first - what the other manager is measured on, what else could be traded (timing, credit in the launch review, help with their backlog later) - turns it into a conversation both sides can win, and leaves a relationship that makes the next request easier.[^s1][^s4]

## Pitfalls

- **Treating every negotiation as a fight over a fixed amount.** Seeing only value claiming misses trades that would leave both sides better off.[^s1]
- **Using anger as a tactic.** The 2023 review finds anger can extract concessions in some conditions - depending on power, authenticity and whether it targets the offer or the person - but it reliably damages trust and the relationship; expressed disappointment can bring similar gains at lower cost.[^s2] Displays of emotion you do not feel shade into deception, which this graph does not recommend.
- **Assuming one style fits every culture or person.** Effects once thought universal turn out to depend on culture, gender stereotypes and power: for example, women who negotiate assertively can face backlash, and gender gaps in outcomes shrink when bargaining-range information is available or with experience.[^s2]
- **Winning the number and losing the relationship.** Squeezing the last concession from a colleague can cost more in future cooperation than it gains now.[^s4][^s5]

## Evidence

The research base is large and mostly experimental, which brings limits. Much of it uses student role-plays with a single, scorable outcome, and both Annual Review chapters stress that results depend on context, culture, power and relationship.[^s1][^s2] ElShenawy's training effects come from laboratory experiments and say little about long-term transfer to the job.[^s3] The subjective-value findings rest on validated measures but are correlational for later behaviour.[^s4][^s5] Hence `mixed`: negotiation can be learned and improved, but which tactics help depends on who is at the table and what comes next.
