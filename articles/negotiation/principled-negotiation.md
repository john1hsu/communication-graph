# Principled negotiation

Principled negotiation, also called negotiation on the merits, is the method set out in *Getting to Yes* by Roger Fisher, William Ury and Bruce Patton. It aims for wise agreements reached efficiently and amicably, and rests on four elements: separate the people from the problem, focus on interests rather than positions, invent options for mutual gain, and insist on objective criteria.[^s1] Any proposed deal is judged against your BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the goal is not agreement at any price but one better than what you could do without it.[^s1] Instead of a contest of wills, each disputed point becomes a joint search for a fair standard.[^s2]

## Origin

Fisher, a Harvard Law School professor, founded the Harvard Negotiation Project in 1979 with help from Ury and Patton, to build negotiation theory simple enough for practitioners to use in real time.[^s3] *Getting to Yes* followed in 1981 and is now in its third edition; the project applied its ideas in work on the Iran hostage crisis, Central American peace efforts, South Africa's constitutional talks and the Ecuador-Peru border dispute.[^s3]
## Why it matters

The method was designed as an alternative to positional bargaining, where each side opens with a demand and argues until someone gives way; PON contrasts this with settling the dispute by appeal to principles rather than opinions.[^s2] At work, most negotiations are repeated and relational - a budget split with a peer team, a vendor renewal, a scope change with a client, a job offer - so a method that protects the relationship while still defending your interests is useful.[^s1] Because each element is a portable habit rather than a script, it also applies where you have no formal authority.[^s1]

## How to apply it

1. **Separate the people from the problem.** Deal with emotions, perceptions and the relationship directly - listen actively, acknowledge feelings, correct misunderstandings - and frame the issue as a shared problem rather than a fight between people.[^s1]
2. **Focus on interests, not positions.** Ask what each side's demand is protecting, and say why you want what you want.[^s1]
3. **Invent options for mutual gain.** Brainstorm several packages before evaluating any, suspend judgement while generating them, and look for trades across issues each side values differently.[^s1]
4. **Insist on objective criteria.** Agree on an independent standard - market value, published benchmarks, precedent, expert opinion, a standard contract, a cost estimate - and treat choosing it as a joint task. If two standards compete, prefer the more widely used one, split the difference between them, or ask a neutral expert.[^s2]
5. **Yield to principle, not pressure.** If the other side relies on threats or a flat refusal to reason, steer back to the standard, and walk away if no deal beats your BATNA.[^s1][^s2]

**Example.** A team lead is renewing a software contract and the vendor proposes a 12% increase. *Before:* "That's too much. We can do 3%." The vendor counters at 9%, and the call becomes a haggle over a number. *After:* "Help us understand what's behind the increase. On our side, we need cost predictability for the next budget cycle. Could we look at what comparable customers pay and at your published list price, then consider a two-year term that gives you revenue certainty?" *Why:* the second version names interests on both sides, proposes a shared benchmark, and opens a trade (term length for price) that a single figure could not.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

The framework itself has rarely been tested as a whole, but parts of it have. In two laboratory experiments, Leigh Thompson (1991) found that negotiators told to share their own interests or ask about the other side's judged their counterpart more accurately and reached more mutually beneficial agreements, even when only one side did so, and those who shared did not lose individual profit.[^s4] That supports the interest-exchange core of the method, though the settings were simulated.[^s4]

Two critiques limit its scope. In a 1984 review, legal scholar James J. White credited the book's challenge to hard bargaining and its practical techniques, but argued that it plays down a large part of negotiation and simplifies or explains away the hardest problems, notably how to divide value that cannot be expanded.[^s5] A 2022 systematic review by Raphael Schoen found that research does not support the method's universal applicability: the fit of its principles divides along Hofstede's individualism dimension, so it works best in Western, individualist cultures, and the evidence even runs against "invent options for mutual gain" in Western settings.[^s6] Because component evidence is positive but its general claims are disputed, the status here is mixed.

## Common mistakes

- Reading "separate the people from the problem" as ignoring feelings; the advice is to deal with them explicitly, not set them aside.[^s1]
- Proposing a standard that happens to favour you and calling it objective; the criterion should be one neither side controls.[^s2]
- Assuming every issue can be expanded, and being unprepared when part of the deal is genuinely fixed-sum.[^s5]
- Applying the method unchanged across cultures, where direct questioning about interests or detaching the person from the issue may read as intrusive or dismissive.[^s6]

## Connections

Principled negotiation was introduced in *Getting to Yes*[^s1] and by Roger Fisher through the Harvard Negotiation Project.[^s3] Its second element is the principle of separating interests from positions, which is a prerequisite here, and its yardstick is the BATNA.[^s1] It is the main alternative to positional bargaining, which it addresses by moving the argument onto shared standards.[^s2] PON applies it to salary negotiations, where published pay data serve as the objective criterion.[^s1] Thompson's 1991 experiments support its interest-exchange core,[^s4] while White's review[^s5] and Schoen's cross-cultural review[^s6] challenge its reach; the cultural limits link it to high- and low-context cultures. It overlaps heavily with integrative negotiation, sits alongside distributive negotiation for the fixed-sum parts of a deal, and draws on active listening, negotiation preparation and multiple equivalent simultaneous offers.
