# Interests vs. positions

A position is what a negotiator says they want - a figure, a date, a yes or no, such as "we need a 10% raise." An interest is the reason behind it: the need, worry, pressure or goal that the position is meant to serve.[^s1] The principle of focusing on interests, not positions, holds that bargaining over the stated demands tends to stall or split the difference, while working on the interests underneath often reveals options that serve both sides better.[^s1][^s2] It is one of the core elements of the principled-negotiation method developed at the Harvard Negotiation Project.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

The idea is older than the phrase. In a 1925 talk later published as "Constructive Conflict," management thinker Mary Parker Follett distinguished three ways of handling conflict: domination, compromise and integration. Her example was a disagreement in a library over whether to open a window. She did not want the window shut so much as to avoid a draught; the other reader just wanted fresh air. Opening a window in an empty adjoining room met both needs without either giving anything up. Follett urged parties to put their cards on the table and find the real demand behind the declared one.[^s5] Roger Fisher, William Ury and later Bruce Patton made "focus on interests, not positions" one of the four principles of *Getting to Yes* (revised edition 2011), the best-known statement of the method.[^s4][^s1]

## Why it matters

Most workplace negotiations - a budget split between two departments, a contract renewal, a job offer, a deadline negotiated with a client - arrive framed as positions. According to *Getting to Yes*, as summarised by Harvard's Program on Negotiation (PON), bargaining over positions causes three problems. It produces unwise agreements, because negotiators dig in to save face and miss better deals. It is inefficient, because sides open with extreme demands and concede in small steps. And it damages relationships, because the exchange becomes a contest of wills that can leave resentment behind.[^s2] PON's example of two department heads fighting over a budget shows what hides beneath: one fears losing influence, the other faces pressure from senior leaders, and both worry about how their teams will perform.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Start with your own side: for each demand you plan to make, write down why you want it, because you cannot explain or trade interests you have not named.[^s2] In the conversation, ask the other side what they are trying to achieve and what would go wrong for them if they accepted your proposal, and play back what you heard.[^s1][^s2] Be willing to state your own interests too - PON advises explaining why you want what you want, not only what you want.[^s2] Then generate several options that would satisfy both sets of interests before evaluating any of them, and look for trades across issues each side values differently.[^s1] Use objective standards, such as published market pay data, to settle the parts that remain zero-sum.[^s1]

A workplace example: a candidate insists on a higher base salary, and the hiring manager says the band is fixed. Asking why reveals that the candidate wants financial security and recognition of their experience, while the employer cares about internal pay equity and predictable costs. With those interests in view, the two can discuss a signing bonus, a performance bonus, extra leave or a salary review tied to milestones - options neither position allowed for.[^s1]

## Evidence

The strongest evidence concerns the information exchange the principle depends on. In two experiments, Leigh Thompson (1991) instructed negotiators either to share information about their own interests or to ask about the other party's. Both moves made negotiators' judgements of their counterpart more accurate and led to more mutually beneficial agreements. The gains appeared even when only one side was instructed to exchange information, and those who disclosed their interests did not lose individual profit; judgement accuracy was strongly linked to performance.[^s3] These were laboratory negotiations, though, so the size of the effect in real workplace deals is less certain.[^s3]

The principle also has well-known critics. Legal scholar James J. White's 1984 review of *Getting to Yes* praised its critique of hard bargaining and its practical techniques, but argued that it seems to deny a large part of negotiation and oversimplifies or explains away the hardest problems.[^s6] In practice, uncovering interests enlarges the options but does not remove the need to divide value, which is why the method pairs interests with objective criteria and a clear BATNA.[^s1] Because the core empirical support is limited and the critique of its scope is long-standing, the status here is mixed.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "focus on interests" as being agreeable: the principle still means pressing hard for your own interests, not only accommodating theirs.[^s1]
- Guessing the other side's interests instead of asking, which defeats the information exchange that makes the approach work.[^s3]
- Revealing nothing about your own interests for fear of exploitation; in Thompson's experiments, negotiators who shared information did not lose individual profit.[^s3]
- Assuming every issue can be dissolved by creativity, and being unprepared when part of the deal really is fixed-sum.[^s6]

## Connections

Focusing on interests is a component of principled negotiation, alongside separating people from the problem, inventing options for mutual gain and using objective criteria.[^s1] It was popularised in *Getting to Yes* by Fisher, Ury and Patton.[^s4] It is the main remedy PON proposes for positional bargaining.[^s2] It counters the mythical fixed pie, the assumption that the other side's interests are simply opposed to yours: in Thompson's experiments, exchanging interest information corrected that kind of misjudgement and raised joint gains.[^s3] White's review challenges how far the approach can stretch.[^s6] Salary and job-offer negotiations are a common setting for it.[^s1] It underpins integrative negotiation and logrolling, depends on asking open-ended questions and practising perspective-taking, and works alongside knowing your BATNA and the zone of possible agreement.
