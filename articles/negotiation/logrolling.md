steps:
  - Before the meeting, list every issue in play (price, timing, scope, staffing, payment terms, risk, duration) and rank them by how much each matters to you.
  - Estimate the other side's ranking, then test it with questions such as "Which of these matters most to you this quarter?"
  - Keep several issues open together rather than closing them one at a time, so trades remain possible.
  - Propose a trade explicitly - "If I move on X, which matters less to me, can you move on Y?" - and make the first offer a complete package.
  - Give larger concessions on your low-priority issues and hold firm on your high-priority ones.
  - Before signing, check whether any re-arrangement of the package would leave both sides better off.
example:
  situation: A project lead and a peer manager are negotiating how many of the peer's analysts will support a migration next quarter.
  before: "I need four analysts for the full quarter. Can you do three? Fine, let's say three, and then let's talk about start dates separately."
  after: "Full-time headcount matters less to me than having them in weeks 3 to 8, when the data cut-over happens. You said month-end close is your crunch. If I take three analysts only for weeks 3 to 8 and give them back for close, could you make one of them your senior data person?"
  why: Haggling over headcount and then dates one at a time treats each issue as a fixed split; packaging timing, seniority and headcount lets each manager concede what they care less about and keep what they care most about.

# Logrolling

Logrolling is the core value-creating move in integrative negotiation: each side gives up something it values less in exchange for something it values more.[^s1] It works only when the parties rank the issues differently, and it requires a negotiator to be clear about their own priorities and to understand the counterpart's.[^s1] In research terms, it means trading concessions across high- and low-priority issues instead of conceding evenly on each one.[^s4]

## Origin

In bargaining research, logrolling was framed early on as the alternative to compromise: Froman and Cohen's 1970 research report in *Behavioral Science* compared the efficiency of the two bargaining processes - meeting halfway versus trading across issues.[^s6] By 1990, laboratory studies such as Leigh Thompson's work with naive and experienced negotiators were measuring logrolling directly as a driver of joint outcomes.[^s3] Operations researchers later formalised it: Tajima and Fraser (2001) described a procedure that exchanges two issues at a time and moves the parties toward Pareto-optimal agreements under a linear-preference assumption.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Most workplace deals - a vendor renewal, a staffing loan between teams, a client scope change, a job offer - involve several issues, and the parties seldom care equally about each. PON's guidance treats those differences in preference as a source of value rather than an obstacle, and notes that logrolling can also break a partial impasse where most terms are agreed but a few items are stuck.[^s1] In Thompson's 1990 study, logrolling raised joint outcomes and improved as negotiators gained experience, while spotting fully compatible issues did not.[^s3] Moran and Ritov (2002) found that negotiators who opened with logrolling offers earned more for themselves and also raised the pair's combined profit.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Rank your issues before the conversation and form a hypothesis about the other side's ranking, then check it by asking which issues matter most to them.[^s1] Keep issues in play together: in Henderson and Trope's 2009 experiment, pairs who made more multi-issue offers reached higher joint outcomes, and the multi-issue offers statistically accounted for the gain.[^s4] Think about the deal at a general level before the detail - the same study found that prompting negotiators to describe the issues abstractly made them more willing to concede on low-priority issues in return for gains on high-priority ones.[^s4] Put your first offer on the table as a package: Moran and Ritov found the issue-by-issue mix of an opening offer anchored counter-offers within each issue, beyond its overall value.[^s2] Stay self-interested but curious; PON reports Roman Trötschel and colleagues' finding that perspective-taking helped self-interested negotiators find logrolls, whereas telling people to care about the other side's outcome produced less efficient concessions.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- Settling issues one at a time. Piecemeal, single-issue offers miss reciprocal concessions that packaged offers make visible.[^s4]
- Assuming the other side ranks issues the way you do, or not knowing your own ranking.[^s1]
- Conceding generously out of goodwill rather than trading; altruistic concessions were less efficient than perspective-taking in Trötschel's experiments.[^s1]
- Expecting a logrolling offer to signal cooperation. Moran and Ritov found counterparts did not judge such offers more attractive, and the offers did not weaken their fixed-pie assumption.[^s2]
- Letting an experienced counterpart claim the extra value. Thompson found experienced negotiators took a larger share of joint resources from naive opponents, so creating value and claiming it are separate jobs.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence that logrolling increases joint gain comes mostly from laboratory negotiation simulations with points-based payoffs.[^s3][^s4] The samples can be small: Henderson and Trope's agreement analysis rested on 27 of 40 student dyads.[^s4] Moran and Ritov's participants were inexperienced negotiators responding to a hypothetical scenario, and two of their three hypotheses about why logrolling offers help were not supported; only the within-issue anchoring explanation held.[^s2] Formal models such as Tajima and Fraser's assume linear preferences, which real issues may not have.[^s5] Treat logrolling as a reliable source of joint value when priorities differ, but not as a way to build trust or change how the other side frames the deal.[^s2]

## Connections

Logrolling is a narrower technique within integrative negotiation, the approach of enlarging value before dividing it.[^s1] It complements perspective-taking, which helps self-interested negotiators find trades.[^s1] It also works with the first offer: a package opening anchors the counterpart issue by issue.[^s2] Its benefits are demonstrated by Moran and Ritov (2002) and supported by Henderson and Trope (2009) and by Thompson's 1990 study of naive and experienced negotiators.[^s2][^s4][^s3] It depends on separating interests from positions, pairs naturally with multiple equivalent simultaneous offers and a planned concession strategy, and is the practical counter to the mythical fixed pie and to positional bargaining over a single number.[^s1][^s4]
