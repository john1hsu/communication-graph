steps:
  - List every issue that is genuinely negotiable (price, scope, timing, payment terms, support, duration) and give each a weight that reflects how much it matters to you.
  - For each issue, write the realistic range of options and score them so that options you prefer earn more points.
  - Build two or three complete packages that score about the same in total for you but trade the issues off differently - for example, higher price with faster delivery versus lower price with a longer commitment.
  - Check every package against your BATNA and make all of them more ambitious than your target, so there is still room to move.
  - Present the packages together, say you would be comfortable with any of them, and ask which one fits the other side best - or, if none does, which is closest and why.
  - Treat their choice as information about their priorities; if they combine the best parts of different packages, reply with a fresh set of equivalent packages rather than conceding piece by piece.

  after: "We've put together three options we'd be glad to do. A: 12 weeks, $240K, two analysts on site, monthly billing. B: 16 weeks, $255K, one analyst on site plus remote support, quarterly billing. C: 10 weeks, $215K, two analysts, with your team owning the dashboard build. Which of these fits your budget cycle and team best - or which is closest?"

# Multiple equivalent simultaneous offers

Multiple equivalent simultaneous offers (MESOs) are two or three complete proposals presented at the same time, each built from a different mix of terms but each worth roughly the same to the person making them. Instead of one package the other side can only accept or attack, you give them a choice and ask which option suits them best.[^s2][^s3] The technique turns a first offer into a question: their pick tells you what they value, while every option still protects your own position.[^s3]

## Origin

Victoria Husted Medvec of Northwestern's Kellogg School and Adam Galinsky introduced the approach in a 2005 article in the Program on Negotiation's *Negotiation* newsletter, noting that the acronym echoes the Greek root for "middle" - a way to balance a profitable deal with a working relationship.[^s2] With Geoffrey Leonardelli, Jun Gu and Geordie McRuer they later tested it in a set of six experiments, published in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* in 2019 after circulating as a working paper since 2005.[^s1][^s4]

## Why it matters

Most workplace negotiations - a client contract, a vendor renewal, a job offer, a resourcing deal with another team - involve several issues at once. Negotiators face a tension between claiming value for themselves and creating value together, and a single package forces the other side either to accept it or to haggle over each term.[^s1] MESOs signal flexibility, tend to raise the counterpart's satisfaction with the outcome, and reveal priorities that people often will not disclose when asked directly.[^s2][^s3] They also help you spot when someone is overstating how much an issue matters to them.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Preparation is the work. Medvec and Galinsky's method is to list the issues, weight them, define the realistic options within each, and score those options so you can compare unlike terms - salary against location against vacation for a job candidate, or price against duration and payment terms for a supplier.[^s2][^s3] Then assemble packages whose totals match for you but whose composition differs, so each one appeals to a different possible priority on the other side.[^s3]

When you present them, make the first set more ambitious than your target, avoid announcing that the offers are mathematically equivalent, and ask which works best or which comes closest; choosing one does not bind the other side to a final deal.[^s3] Jun Gu's advice from the research is to offer two or three packages: in their studies two or three worked about equally well, and more risks choice overload.[^s4] Throughout, the offers must be real - presenting options you would not honour, or claiming indifference you do not feel, crosses from strategy into deception.

## Common mistakes

- Offering more than three packages, which overloads the other side and slows the decision.[^s3][^s4]
- Building packages that are not truly equivalent for you, so whichever one they pick leaves you worse off.[^s2][^s3]
- Anchoring the packages too close to your target, which leaves nothing to trade because MESOs disclose a lot about your interests.[^s3]
- Letting the other side cherry-pick the best term from each package; answer with a new set of packages built from your scoring system instead.[^s3]
- Skipping the scoring step and improvising options at the table.[^s2]

## Evidence

Across six experiments with students, MBA participants and online adults, Leonardelli and colleagues (2019) found that recipients saw MESOs as a more sincere effort to reach agreement than a single offer, which made the offers stronger anchors and produced counteroffers and agreements more favourable to the offerer.[^s1] MESOs were also more likely to contain a starting point attractive to the recipient, which raised joint value indirectly, and offerers were rated as more cooperative.[^s1] The results are not uniform: the direct effect on joint value was inconsistent across studies, the anchoring advantage appeared mainly when offers were ambitious, several effects were marginal, and the most realistic MBA study was small.[^s1] Most evidence comes from one research team using simulations, and independent replications in real workplace deals are scarce, so the evidence status here is mixed.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

MESOs are a form of first offer used in integrative, multi-issue negotiation, and they depend on knowing your BATNA so every package stays above your walk-away point.[^s1][^s3] Medvec introduced the technique with Galinsky, and the 2019 experiments by Leonardelli and colleagues demonstrate its effects.[^s1][^s2] Those experiments explain the claiming advantage through anchoring - offers seen as sincere pull the counterpart's estimates towards them - and present MESOs as a way to ease the negotiator's dilemma between creating and claiming value.[^s1] PON's guidance applies the method to job and salary negotiations, where pay, location and time off can be traded against each other.[^s3]
