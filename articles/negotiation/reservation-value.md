# Reservation value

Your reservation value is the least attractive deal you would still accept rather than walk away. Program on Negotiation (PON) materials define it as the lowest-valued agreement you are willing to take and derive it directly from your BATNA, the best thing you will do if this negotiation fails: an offer below it should be rejected in favour of the alternative, and an offer above it is worth accepting.[^s1] For a buyer it is the highest price they would pay; for a seller, the lowest price they would take, and bargaining is the process of reconciling two limits that each side usually keeps hidden.[^s4]

## Origin

Textbooks use several names for the same threshold. Lewicki, Barry and Saunders's *Negotiation* calls it the resistance point - the point beyond which a party will not go - and pairs it with an opening point and a target point; the space between the two parties' resistance points is the bargaining range.[^s2] Howard Raiffa's 1982 *The Art and Science of Negotiation* brought decision analysis and step-by-step diagrams to bargaining for practitioners, and that analytic tradition is where today's "reservation price" vocabulary is taught.[^s7][^s1] PON's training materials now present reservation value as the second step after identifying a BATNA.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Without a reservation value decided in advance, negotiators make the walk-away call in the moment, under pressure, pride or fatigue. PON's report gives the example of a business owner whose expected value from litigating was about $7.4 million (a 70% chance of $10 million and a 30% chance of $3 million, less $0.5 million in costs); he rejected a settlement roughly $1.1 million above that figure and later conceded he should have taken it.[^s1] A calculated number protects you from both errors: accepting a deal worse than your alternative, and rejecting one that beats it.[^s1]

It also tells you whether a deal is possible at all. When the two sides' resistance points leave a positive range, settlement is likely and each side tries to capture as much of it as it can; when they do not overlap, negotiation may be impossible and both should turn to their alternatives.[^s2]

## How to use it at work

Set it before the first meeting, following PON's sequence: list your realistic alternatives, value each, pick the best as your BATNA, and translate it into a reservation value in the same terms as the deal on the table.[^s1] Compare like with like - PON warns that an alternative that looks 30% cheaper on price can turn out worse once coverage, timing and switching costs are counted.[^s1]

Example: a team lead is renewing a design agency's retainer. Their best alternative is a freelancer at $9,000 a month plus about $1,000 a month of extra management time, so the reservation value for the agency deal is roughly $10,000 a month for equivalent scope. The lead opens with a target of $8,000, concedes in steps, and declines anything above $10,000 - while staying open if the agency adds something the freelancer cannot, which changes the comparison rather than the principle. The steps are PON's: value the alternative in comparable terms, accept offers above the threshold and reject those below it.[^s1]

Estimate the other side's reservation value too, by asking what they would do without a deal; PON's farmland example shows a seller who valued his land on its local price and accepted far less than a buyer with few alternative sites would have paid.[^s1] Treat that estimate as a hypothesis and test it with open, neutral questions rather than leading ones.[^s4][^s2]

Keep your own reservation value private. Revealing it invites the other side to settle right at it, which is why textbooks describe resistance points as the most carefully concealed information in distributive bargaining.[^s2] Concealing it is different from lying about it: a 1998 survey of MBA students found most thought misstating one's bottom line acceptable, so you should expect it from others, but PON recommends setting a norm of truthfulness early and walking away from counterparts whose significant lies have broken trust.[^s5] Philosophers still argue over whether such lies can ever be justified - Strudler (2023) defends them only where both sides knowingly accept the risk in a fair context - so the safer workplace rule is to decline to disclose rather than invent a number.[^s6]

## Common mistakes

Aiming at your reservation value instead of your goal is the most costly. In three experiments, Galinsky, Mussweiler and Medvec (2002) found that negotiators who focused on their target price secured objectively better deals than those who focused on their reservation price - yet felt less satisfied with them; reminding target-focused negotiators of their reservation price after the deal restored their satisfaction.[^s3] The practical lesson is to bargain toward the target and use the reservation value afterwards to judge the outcome. Other mistakes PON lists are letting sunk investment in an alternative or anger move the number, and forgetting that reservation values shift as information arrives or coalitions form in multiparty talks.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Reservation value is an analytic construct rather than an empirical effect, so its value lies in clearer decisions rather than in a measured effect size.[^s1][^s2] The focus findings come from laboratory simulations with students, so their size in real workplace deals is uncertain.[^s3] Many negotiations also involve non-price issues, relationships and uncertain alternatives, which make a single number an approximation that should be revisited as facts change.[^s1]

## Connections

Reservation value requires and complements the BATNA: the BATNA is the alternative, and the reservation value converts it into an accept-or-reject threshold for this deal.[^s1] Two parties' reservation values bound the zone of possible agreement, so it complements ZOPA analysis within distributive negotiation and negotiation preparation.[^s2] It contrasts with the target or aspiration point, the realistic best outcome you aim for, and Galinsky, Mussweiler and Medvec's 2002 experiments demonstrate why focusing on one rather than the other changes both results and satisfaction.[^s3] It sets the boundary for first offers and concession strategy, and it is a core preparation step in salary negotiation.[^s1]
