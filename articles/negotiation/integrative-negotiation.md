steps:
  - Before the conversation, list every issue that could be on the table - not only price, but timing, scope, payment terms, staffing, risk sharing, duration - and add issues if there is only one.
  - Rank those issues by how much each matters to you, and note your BATNA so you know the point below which no package is worth taking.
  - Open by asking why, not what - ask what the other side needs to achieve, what constraints they face and which terms matter most to them - and listen for differences in priority.
  - Share some of your own priorities in return, a piece at a time, watching whether the other side reciprocates before you disclose more.
  - Propose trades across issues - give ground on what you value less in exchange for movement on what you value more - instead of meeting in the middle on each issue separately.
  - Bundle the trades into complete packages and test them with "what if" questions, using objective criteria such as market rates to settle the remaining distributive pieces.
  - Before closing, ask whether any other arrangement would leave both sides better off than the one on the table.
example:
  situation: An IT manager is negotiating a software renewal with a vendor account executive who has proposed an 8% price increase.
  before: "An 8% increase isn't going to work for us. Can you meet us halfway at 4%?"
  after: "Help me understand what's driving the 8% - is it a revenue target for this year, or the cost of supporting us? On our side, budget certainty matters more than anything, and we're flexible on contract length. If we signed for three years instead of one, and agreed to be a reference customer, could you hold this year's price flat with a capped increase after that?"
  why: Splitting the difference treats the renewal as a single fixed pie; asking about the vendor's interests and offering terms that are cheap for the buyer but valuable to the vendor (a longer commitment, a reference) lets both sides gain more than a 4% compromise would give them.

  - Am I sharing information step by step and checking that it is reciprocated?

# Integrative negotiation

Integrative negotiation (often called win-win or integrative bargaining) is an approach in which the parties look for both shared and differing interests so that they can enlarge the total value on the table before deciding how to divide it.[^s1] Its opposite, distributive negotiation, treats value as fixed: whatever one side gains, the other loses.[^s1][^s2] In practice, integrative negotiation means talking about interests rather than positions, putting several issues in play, and trading across them so each side gets more of what it values most.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

The distinction comes from *A Behavioral Theory of Labor Negotiations* (1965) by Richard Walton and Robert McKersie, built partly on transcripts of real union-management bargaining.[^s2] They described four sub-processes - distributive bargaining, integrative bargaining, attitudinal restructuring and intra-organisational negotiation - and defined integrative bargaining as finding new ideas that close the gap between the parties by reframing or broadening the issue.[^s2] Later work, including Fisher, Ury and Patton's *Getting to Yes*, turned these ideas into practical guidance such as focusing on interests and using objective criteria.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Most workplace negotiations - a vendor renewal, a client scope change, a job offer, a resourcing deal with another team - involve more than one issue, and the parties rarely value every issue equally. Thompson and Hastie's 1990 studies found that most negotiators begin by assuming the other side's interests are directly opposed to their own, and many never notice when interests are actually compatible, so they settle for worse deals than were available.[^s3] Negotiators who learned the other side's priorities early, often in the first few minutes, earned more.[^s3] Integrative negotiation is the deliberate habit of testing that fixed-pie assumption instead of acting on it.

## How to apply it

PON's guidance starts with separating a position (a demand, such as a price) from the interest behind it (the reason for the demand), then asking diagnostic questions about why an issue matters and what constraints the other side faces.[^s1] Expand the agenda: add variables such as timing or payment terms, then combine them into packages that give each side what it values most, using objective standards like market rates for the parts that remain contested.[^s1]

The core move is logrolling - conceding on issues you value less in exchange for gains on issues you value more, which requires knowing both your own priorities and theirs.[^s5] Research summarised by PON found that asking negotiators to focus on the other side's interests and intentions improved their perspective-taking and helped them find these trades, while simply telling them to care about the other side's outcome led to less efficient concessions.[^s5] Share information gradually: openness helps create value, but over-disclosure can weaken you against a counterpart who is playing purely distributively.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- Splitting the difference on each issue in turn, which leaves joint gains unclaimed when the parties' priorities differ.[^s3][^s5]
- Assuming the other side wants the opposite of everything you want - the fixed-sum error.[^s3]
- Equating "win-win" with being nice: concern for the other side's outcome without understanding their interests produces concessions, not better deals.[^s5]
- Disclosing everything up front to a counterpart who does not reciprocate.[^s1]
- Forgetting that value still has to be divided; Michael Wheeler of Harvard Business School notes a large share of a small pie can beat a modest share of a slightly bigger one.[^s1]

## Evidence

A meta-analysis of 28 studies by De Dreu, Weingart and Kwon (2000) found that negotiators with a prosocial motive were less contentious, did more problem solving and reached higher joint outcomes than egoistic negotiators - but only when resistance to yielding was high or unknown, consistent with dual concern theory.[^s4] In other words, joint gains come from combining concern for the other side with firmness about your own interests, not from concern alone.[^s4] Effects varied with study features such as participant recruitment and publication status, and most evidence comes from simulated negotiations, so the status here is mixed.[^s4] PON also cautions that the approach suits multi-issue deals and ongoing relationships better than one-off, single-issue haggling or counterparts who refuse to collaborate.[^s1]

## Connections

Integrative negotiation contrasts with distributive negotiation, in which value is treated as fixed.[^s1] Walton and McKersie introduced the distinction in *A Behavioral Theory of Labor Negotiations*.[^s2] It addresses the mythical fixed pie - the assumption, documented by Thompson and Hastie, that interests are wholly opposed.[^s3] De Dreu and colleagues' meta-analysis supports its central claim that problem solving combined with firmness raises joint outcomes.[^s4] It complements perspective-taking, which helps negotiators find logrolling trades.[^s5] PON applies it to salary negotiations among other settings.[^s1] Multiple equivalent simultaneous offers are one way to put integrative packages on the table, and the approach depends on knowing your BATNA and asking open-ended questions.
