# Negotiation preparation

Negotiation preparation is the work you do before you sit down - and between sessions - to understand what is at stake, what you and the other side need, what you will do if there is no deal, and how the conversation itself will run. Harvard's Program on Negotiation calls failing to prepare the most common negotiating mistake, one that leaves value on the table or exposes you to being taken advantage of.[^s1] Its real payoff is not a script but readiness: unexpected issues always come up, and preparation is what lets you think on your feet when they do.[^s1]

## Origin

Planning has long been treated as the first phase of negotiation, and most guidance takes the form of checklists: the Program on Negotiation lists 32 questions, and one review of major negotiation textbooks counted 58 distinct preparation activities.[^s1][^s2] Academic negotiation textbooks condense this into a planning sequence - define the issues and the overall bargaining mix, find the interests behind each issue, identify alternatives and a walk-away point, set targets and openings, understand your constituents and the other party, plan how to present your case, and settle the agenda, attendees, time and place.[^s5] Yet researchers have repeatedly noted that the planning phase itself has been studied far less than what happens at the table.[^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

In an early experiment on bargaining plans, Roloff and Jordan (1991) found that negotiators given high profit goals put more effort into planning and were better at spotting logrolling trades - giving ground on low-priority issues in return for gains on high-priority ones.[^s3] Experience helped only in context: general bargaining experience increased planned logrolling only where integrative potential existed, and role-specific experience increased planning for the other side's moves only in a familiar role.[^s3] Preparation also changes who you consult inside your own organisation: PON recommends agreeing the BATNA and the estimate of the counterpart's interests with key leaders before the talks, and later judging the result against those pre-agreed numbers rather than against gut feel.[^s6]

## What works here

Use the textbook sequence as your skeleton, and write the answers down.[^s5] Start with your own side: list the issues, rank your interests, and work out your BATNA, reservation point and aspiration point, including how you could strengthen the BATNA before you meet.[^s1] Then do the same for the other party - their likely interests, alternatives, walk-away point, authority and internal politics - and ask whether a zone of possible agreement exists or could be created by adding issues.[^s1][^s5] Look for trades built on differences in priorities, forecasts, risk tolerance or deadlines, and gather objective benchmarks or precedents you can point to.[^s1] Finish with process: who attends, who speaks, what the agenda bundles together, the setting and channel, and a rehearsal of your opening and their likely replies.[^s1][^s5]

A workplace example: a team lead asking for two extra contractors for a quarter-end push might, before the meeting, list three issues (headcount, duration, budget line), note that the director's real interest is the delivery date, identify a fallback (deferring one low-priority feature), and prepare a benchmark from last year's overtime costs. Walking in with that, the lead can propose a package rather than defend a single number.[^s5][^s1]

Keep preparing during the negotiation. In a year-long field study of a team negotiating a contract worth more than EUR 75 million, preparation happened before sessions, in debriefs after them and between them - right up to the end, including real-time coordination over an internal chat channel during sessions.[^s2]

## Pitfalls

- **Preparing only to claim.** The observed team's early preparation centred on its own limits and hard bargaining, with little attention to the other party's interests; its expected interest-based preparation never materialised.[^s2]
- **Never revisiting assumptions.** The same team rarely tested its arguments or revisited its assumptions, and used post-session meetings mostly to assign tasks rather than evaluate strategy.[^s2] Schedule a short debrief after each session that asks what you learned and what you now believe differently.
- **Goals that blind you.** Specific, challenging goals usually help, but in Polzer and Neale's (1995) experiments negotiators holding them sometimes failed to incorporate new information that emerged at the table, and setting a separate goal for each issue pushed people toward competitive, issue-by-issue haggling.[^s4] Set an overall goal for the package and treat your plan as a hypothesis.
- **Rigid backup plans.** When negotiators in Roloff and Jordan's study built backup plans, they mostly reused their original tactics but were more likely to add coercive ones - a reason to check fallback plans for escalation you would not actually endorse.[^s3]

## Evidence

The evidence is **mixed** rather than robust. Preparation is near-universally recommended, but much of the guidance is checklist-based, and the empirical base relies heavily on simulations, surveys and self-reports, with very few observed field studies.[^s2] Experimental work shows that planning effort and goal setting can improve integrative outcomes,[^s3] while other experiments show the same goals can make negotiators less responsive to new information.[^s4] The field evidence suggests real teams under time pressure prepare less cooperatively and less reflectively than the advice assumes.[^s2]

## Connections

Preparation is a phase of negotiation, which textbooks treat as the first step of the overall process.[^s5] It complements BATNA work: the checklist builds on knowing and strengthening your best alternative and your reservation and aspiration points.[^s1] Its value is demonstrated experimentally by Roloff and Jordan's 1991 study of bargaining plans[^s3] and observed in the field by Lindholst, Bülow and Fells' 2018 case study of a complex contract negotiation.[^s2] Related techniques that live inside a good plan include the zone of possible agreement, interests vs. positions, the first offer, concession strategy, multiple equivalent simultaneous offers and role reversal, and salary negotiation is a common context where it pays off.[^s1][^s5]
