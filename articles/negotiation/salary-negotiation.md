# Salary negotiation

Salary negotiation is the situation in which a candidate and an employer settle the pay and other terms of a job offer, or in which an employee asks for a raise. It is one of the few negotiations most professionals face repeatedly, usually with little practice, high personal stakes and a counterpart (a recruiter or hiring manager) who does it far more often than they do. Most people skip it: in a 2023 Pew Research Center survey of more than 5,000 U.S. workers, only about three in ten said they had asked for higher pay than they were first offered in their current job (32% of men, 28% of women).[^s5] Of those who did ask, about two-thirds reported getting at least part of what they asked for.[^s5]

## Origin

Pay bargaining is as old as employment, but salary negotiation became a research subject in its own right through work on why outcomes differ between people who appear equally qualified. Hannah Riley Bowles, Linda Babcock and Kathleen McGinn's 2005 study of MBA graduates is a landmark: it found women accepted base salaries about 5% lower than men after controls, but that the gap was small and not significant in industries where salary standards were clear, and about 10% in industries where they were ambiguous.[^s2] Later work shifted attention from "who asks" to how askers are treated and why candidates hold back.[^s3][^s1]

## Why it matters

A starting salary becomes the base for later raises and for the next employer's offer, so a small difference at the start compounds. The main barrier is fear rather than skill. Hart, Bear and Ren (2024) ran seven studies with 3,338 participants - surveys of candidates, hiring committees, managers and hiring professionals, plus incentivised negotiation experiments - and found that candidates consistently overestimated the chance that negotiating would make the employer withdraw the offer, sometimes enough to avoid negotiating at all.[^s1] Two beliefs drove the overestimate: seeing the negotiation as zero-sum, and feeling powerless; prompts that reduced either narrowed the gap but did not close it.[^s1] In a field experiment with over 3,000 U.S. tech job seekers, Cullen, Pakzad-Hurson and Perez-Truglia found that a short encouragement message raised the share who countered (61% versus 54% in the control group) and raised pay, while a deep discount on paid coaching did not change how many people negotiated - again pointing to beliefs, not skill gaps.[^s6]

## What works here

**Reduce ambiguity before you talk.** The gender gap in the MBA data shrank where salary standards were well known,[^s2] and a meta-analysis of 123 effect sizes (N = 10,888) found the male advantage in negotiated outcomes was smaller when negotiators had information about the bargaining range or prior experience.[^s4] For anyone, the practical lesson is the same: find credible ranges for the role, level and location, and know your own walk-away point and alternatives before the first call.[^s2][^s4]

**Treat it as joint problem-solving.** Because zero-sum framing inflates the perceived risk,[^s1] open with appreciation and interest in the role, then make a specific request with a reason, and include terms that may cost the employer little but matter to you (start date, title, remote days, development budget).

**Ask once, clearly, and then listen.** For example, a candidate offered $82K might write: "Thank you - I'm excited about the role. Based on what I'm seeing for this level in our region and the scope we discussed, I was hoping for $90K. Is there flexibility there?" rather than "Is this negotiable at all?", which invites a yes-or-no answer and anchors nothing. The figure should come from the range research above, and the reason should be one you could repeat to the hiring manager's boss.[^s2][^s4]

**Anticipate how the ask will be heard.** In four experiments, evaluators penalised women more than men for asking for higher pay, mainly because women who asked were seen as less nice and more demanding, even though they were rated equally competent.[^s3] Mazei and colleagues also found the gender difference shrank when people negotiated on behalf of someone else.[^s4] Framing that shows you care about the relationship and the team is therefore useful for everyone, and especially where backlash is a known risk - but the authors stress the larger fix lies with evaluators and organisations, not with telling individuals to ask differently.[^s3]

## Pitfalls

- **Not asking at all.** It is the most common outcome, and candidates misjudge its safety.[^s5][^s1]
- **Treating the risk as zero.** Candidates overestimate the chance of losing the offer, but the risk is not nil, and the payoff depends on the job market; the researchers expected larger gains in a stronger market than the one they studied.[^s1][^s6]
- **Negotiating without information.** Ambiguity widens unexplained gaps and leaves you anchored on the employer's number.[^s2][^s4]
- **Assuming "just ask" fixes inequity.** Asking carries social costs that are not evenly distributed, so managers who set pay should publish ranges and evaluate requests consistently.[^s3][^s2]

## Connections

Salary negotiation is a recurring context of **negotiation** (`cm:negotiation`) and builds on knowing your **BATNA** (`cm:batna`). Its main evidence base is **Hart, Bear and Ren (2024)** on inflated fear of losing the offer,[^s1] the **Bowles, Babcock and McGinn (2005)** MBA study on structural ambiguity,[^s2] **Bowles, Babcock and Lai (2007)** on the social cost of asking,[^s3] the **Mazei et al.
