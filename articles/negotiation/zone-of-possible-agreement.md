# Zone of possible agreement

The zone of possible agreement (ZOPA) is the set of outcomes that both parties prefer to an impasse. Each side's reservation point - the worst deal it would still accept rather than walk away, derived from its BATNA - marks one edge of the zone; where the two ranges overlap, a deal is possible, and where they do not, it is not.[^s1][^s5] Thinking in ZOPA terms turns a vague "can we make this work?" into two concrete questions: does a zone exist, and where in it will we land?[^s1]

## Origin

The idea of a bargaining or contract zone bounded by two reservation prices is usually traced to decision scientist Howard Raiffa, whose 1982 book *The Art and Science of Negotiation* set out step-by-step, diagram-based analysis of bargaining for practitioners such as lawyers, executives and diplomats.[^s2][^s5] Later economic work refers directly to "Raiffa's Zone of Possible Agreements" and refines his model of how parties make concessions within it.[^s3] The Program on Negotiation at Harvard Law School now teaches ZOPA analysis as a preparation step that builds on the BATNA idea from Fisher, Ury and Patton's *Getting to Yes*.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Workplace negotiations - a job offer, a vendor renewal, a budget or deadline request to another team - often stall or end badly because people never check whether a deal is possible. PON's salary example: a candidate who would accept 70,000-80,000 and an employer willing to pay 65,000-75,000 share a ZOPA of 70,000-75,000; if the employer's ceiling were 65,000, there would be no ZOPA and both sides should pursue their alternatives rather than keep haggling.[^s1] Knowing the zone also protects against the "agreement trap" of accepting a deal that is worse than walking away, which PON links to sunk costs and a wish to protect the relationship.[^s1]

## How to use it

- Fix your own reservation point from your BATNA before the first conversation.[^s1]
- Estimate the other side's alternatives and reservation point through research and direct questions, and treat the estimate as a hypothesis to test, not a fact.[^s1][^s4]
- Put non-price issues - start date, scope, payment terms, support - on the table; trading across issues can create a zone where price alone had none, which is the opposite of treating the deal as a fixed pie.[^s1][^s6]
- If the evidence says no zone exists, say so plainly and turn to your alternatives instead of conceding past your walk-away point.[^s1]

For example, a team lead asked to absorb a project in six weeks might first answer, "That's impossible." Mapping the zone instead, she asks what matters most to the requester, learns that a partial release by week six would do, and proposes the core feature in six weeks and the rest in ten - a deal both sides prefer to a refusal. The original framing hid a zone that existed once a second issue, scope, was added.[^s1][^s6]

## Evidence

ZOPA is an analytic model rather than a single empirical effect, but research shows people misjudge it in predictable ways. In three studies, Larrick and Wu (2007) found negotiators systematically underestimate the size of the bargaining zone - a "small-pie bias" - and therefore overestimate how much of the surplus they captured; estimates of the counterpart's reservation price that happen to fall inside the zone tend to go unchallenged and become self-fulfilling.[^s4] Schaerer, Loschelder and Swaab (2016) showed in five experiments with online participants, students and MBA students that holding several weaker alternatives, rather than one, led negotiators to perceive the zone as lower, make less ambitious first offers and reach less profitable deals, even when the best alternative was identical.[^s5] Earlier, Thompson and Hastie (1990) found most negotiators start by assuming the other side's interests are directly opposed to theirs, and those who learned the other side's real interests early achieved better outcomes.[^s6] These are mostly laboratory and simulation studies, so the size of the effects in real workplace deals is uncertain.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

ZOPA sits within distributive negotiation and negotiation preparation, and it requires the BATNA concept, which supplies each side's reservation point; the two are complementary halves of the same analysis.[^s1] It is attributed to Howard Raiffa.[^s3] It contrasts with the mythical fixed pie, the assumption that every negotiation is purely win-lose, because looking for trades across issues can widen or create the zone.[^s1] Larrick and Wu's small-pie studies and Schaerer, Loschelder and Swaab's bargaining-zone-distortion experiments demonstrate how perceptions of the zone drive first offers and outcomes, which links it to first-offer strategy and to multiple equivalent simultaneous offers.[^s4][^s5]
