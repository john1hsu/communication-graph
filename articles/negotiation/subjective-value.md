# Subjective value in negotiation

Subjective value is everything a negotiator takes away from a deal apart from its economic terms: how they feel about what they got, about how they behaved, about how the talks were run, and about the person across the table.[^s1] Two people can sign identical terms and walk away with very different subjective value, depending on how the offers were exchanged and how they were treated along the way.[^s1][^s4] For anyone who negotiates with colleagues, clients or suppliers they will meet again, it is the part of the outcome that shapes the next conversation.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

The concept was mapped by Jared Curhan, Hillary Anger Elfenbein and Heng Xu in a four-study paper published in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* in 2006.[^s1] They first asked students, community members and negotiation practitioners what they valued after negotiating, which produced 20 categories; negotiation scholars then sorted these into four groups.[^s1] The resulting **Subjective Value Inventory (SVI)** measures feelings about:

1. **The instrumental outcome** - whether you got a good share of what was on the table.
2. **The self** - whether you saved face and acted in line with your principles.
3. **The process** - whether you felt heard and treated fairly.
4. **The relationship** - whether the talks built trust and a working relationship.[^s1][^s4]

Later studies confirmed the four-factor structure and showed that SVI scores predicted later negotiation decisions better than the economic results did.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The strongest workplace evidence comes from job offers. Curhan, Elfenbein and Kilduff (2009) surveyed people who had negotiated job offers and followed them for a year: those who came away with higher subjective value reported more satisfaction with their pay and their job and were less inclined to leave, while the salary they had actually negotiated showed no visible effect on those attitudes.[^s2] A hiring manager who wins every point but leaves a new hire feeling unheard may have bought a worse year, not a cheaper one.[^s2]

Feelings also feed back into money. In a two-round study, negotiators who felt better about round one went on to get better individual and joint economic outcomes in round two with the same counterpart, even after controlling for how they had scored in round one; round-one feelings, not round-one points, predicted who wanted to negotiate together again.[^s3] Harvard's Program on Negotiation reports a similar pattern among 104 MIT MBA students, whose SVI scores - but not their graded outcomes - predicted whether they wanted to work with their counterpart again.[^s4]

## How to use it at work

Treat the four SVI areas as a checklist for any negotiation inside a continuing relationship, such as a budget split between teams, a contract renewal or a promotion discussion.[^s4]

- **Outcome:** before closing, make sure the other side can name something concrete they gained, not just what they conceded.[^s1][^s4]
- **Self:** leave room for them to save face - avoid framing the result as their loss in front of others, and do not push them to act against stated principles.[^s1]
- **Process:** summarise their main concerns back to them before you respond, so they can confirm they were heard.[^s4]
- **Relationship:** close by agreeing how you will work together on the next step, not only on the terms.[^s4]

Example: a procurement lead renewing a software contract secures a 12% discount by hammering on price in a terse email thread; the vendor's account manager complies but deprioritises the account. In the next cycle, the lead opens with a call, asks about the vendor's renewal targets, explains the internal budget limit, and agrees on a smaller 8% discount plus a joint review in six months. The money is slightly worse, but the vendor feels heard and the relationship is intact for the next negotiation - the trade-off the research suggests is often worth making when parties will meet again.[^s3][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The SVI is a validated measure with good convergent, discriminant and predictive validity, and its link to later behaviour has been shown in role-plays, multi-round studies and a year-long field survey.[^s1][^s2][^s3] Much of the evidence comes from one research group and from correlational designs, so subjective value may partly reflect other factors such as a counterpart's skill or personality.[^s2][^s3]

Feeling good is not always good for the next deal. When people negotiate in sequence with *different* counterparts, high subjective value in one negotiation has been linked to worse outcomes in the next: Becker, Cropanzano and Goldman found this in a lab experiment and a field study, and Becker and Curhan traced it to pride, which lowers vigilance, among both students and shipping-company staff negotiating fuel prices.[^s6][^s5] The effect faded after a day's break, and the researchers suggest pausing or asking two questions after each negotiation - what went well, and what would you do differently - to temper overconfidence.[^s5] Hence `mixed`: subjective value reliably matters, but its effects depend on whether the next negotiation is with the same person.

## Connections

Subjective value is a dimension of **negotiation** (`cm:negotiation`) introduced by **Jared Curhan** (`cm:jared-curhan`) and colleagues and **demonstrated** in Curhan, Elfenbein and Xu 2006 (`cm:curhan-elfenbein-xu-2006`), Curhan, Elfenbein and Kilduff 2009 (`cm:curhan-elfenbein-kilduff-2009`) and Curhan, Elfenbein and Eisenkraft 2010 (`cm:curhan-elfenbein-eisenkraft-2010`).[^s1][^s2][^s3] Its "self" factor, saving face, **complements face and facework** (`cm:face-and-facework`), and its relationship factor links it to **rapport** (`cm:rapport`).[^s1][^s4] It matters most in **integrative negotiation** (`cm:integrative-negotiation`) and **salary negotiation** (`cm:salary-negotiation`), where the parties keep working together, and it complicates purely economic yardsticks such as **reservation value** (`cm:reservation-value`) in **distributive negotiation** (`cm:distributive-negotiation`).[^s2][^s3] It is also related to the **post-settlement settlement** (`cm:post-settlement-settlement`), another tool for negotiations where both sides keep dealing with each other.
