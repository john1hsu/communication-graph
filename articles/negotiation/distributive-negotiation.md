steps:
  - Confirm the issue really is fixed-sum - one number with nothing else worth trading - before you treat the talk as a pure split.
  - Write down your BATNA and reservation value, then improve the BATNA by opening talks with other parties where you can.
  - Research the counterpart's likely alternatives and walk-away point, and estimate the zone of possible agreement from both bottom lines.
  - Set a target that is ambitious but defensible, and prepare the market data or reasoning that makes it look reasonable.
  - If you know the market value well, make the first offer near your target and explain the basis for it.
  - Concede only in exchange for movement from the other side, name what each concession costs you, and tie it to something specific you get back.
  - After you make or receive an offer, stop talking and let the other side respond instead of sweetening your own number.

# Distributive negotiation

Distributive negotiation (or distributive bargaining) is the part of a negotiation in which the parties divide a resource they have already identified - most often by haggling over a price, rate or budget figure.[^s1] Because the amount is treated as fixed, it is sometimes described as splitting the pie: offers and counteroffers move back and forth until the two sides settle somewhere between their opening positions.[^s2] Distributive tactics do not have to be adversarial; PON stresses that effective value claiming rests mainly on preparation, not on threats, bluffs or tricks.[^s1]

## Origin

The term comes from Richard Walton and Robert McKersie's *A Behavioral Theory of Labor Negotiations* (1965), which split bargaining into four sub-processes, of which distributive bargaining is the one that settles conflicting interests.[^s3] In their model each side has a target point (its best realistic outcome) and a resistance point (the worst it will accept); when the resistance points overlap there is a positive contract zone and a deal is possible, and when they do not, an impasse such as a strike becomes likely.[^s3] Kochan and Lipsky note that game theorists call this situation zero-sum and behavioural scientists call it win-lose.[^s3] Howard Raiffa later labelled the rhythm of offers and concessions the negotiation dance.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Almost every workplace deal contains some fixed-sum element - a salary figure, a contractor's day rate, a vendor's unit price, a share of a shared budget - so even collaborative negotiators eventually have to divide value.[^s2] PON's closing point is that claiming value and creating value go together: knowing how to claim your share fairly is what makes it safe to share information and look for trades elsewhere in the deal.[^s2] Doing it badly has a clear cost: in Korobkin and Doherty's settlement simulations, the single strongest predictor of who captured more of the surplus was how accurately each negotiator estimated the other's reservation price.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Preparation does most of the work. Know your own BATNA and walk-away point, and strengthen the BATNA by pursuing more than one deal at a time.[^s1][^s2] Then estimate the other side's alternatives and bottom line from research and from questions at the table, which tells you roughly where the zone of possible agreement lies and lets you aim higher within it.[^s1][^s2]

Set an ambitious but realistic target and prepare the arguments that make it look reasonable.[^s1] When you know the item's value well, an assertive opening offer helps, because the first number named tends to anchor the rest of the discussion.[^s1] In Korobkin and Doherty's study, the size of the first offer was among the next strongest predictors of outcome after the reservation-price estimate.[^s5]

Manage concessions deliberately. Malhotra and Bazerman's advice, summarised by PON, is to avoid moving unilaterally, to be comfortable with silence after an offer, to label each concession so its cost is noticed, and to make concessions contingent on a specific action by the counterpart - which sometimes adds new issues and turns part of the talk integrative.[^s2]

Stay truthful about your limits. Surveys summarised by PON suggest most managers have lied in a negotiation, but lying puts reputation and future deals at risk and can be illegal.[^s6] The ethical alternative is to change the facts before you sit down - for example by lining up genuine alternative offers or asking your principal to cap your authority - so the strong statement you want to make is also a true one.[^s6] Declining to reveal your bottom line is fair play; inventing a competing offer is not.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a multi-issue deal as a single number, which leaves trades on the table; check whether the pie is really fixed before you start dividing it.[^s2]
- Conceding twice in a row, or filling a silence with a better offer, before the other side has moved.[^s2]
- Arriving without an estimate of the counterpart's walk-away point, the factor that best predicted results in the settlement experiments.[^s5]
- Confusing toughness with deception: bluffing about alternatives or limits trades a small gain for a large reputational risk.[^s6]

## Evidence

Hüffmeier and colleagues' 2014 meta-analysis of 34 studies found that hardline strategies (high demands, few and small concessions) were linked to better economic outcomes in distributive negotiations, while softline strategies were linked to better socio-emotional outcomes such as liking and satisfaction.[^s4] Hardline negotiators gained most when they knew the bargaining zone and had been told to maximise their own result; softline negotiators did best economically when they reciprocated the counterpart's concessions accurately.[^s4] Contrary to the authors' prediction, softness did not pay off when impasses were costly.[^s4] Korobkin and Doherty's 2009 path analysis explained more than half the variation in simulated settlement outcomes with seven variables and argued the results generalise to two-party distributive bargaining.[^s5] Both bodies of evidence come largely from laboratory simulations, and the trade-off between money and relationship means the evidence status here is `mixed`: firmness claims more value, but at a relational price that matters when you will deal with the same counterpart again.[^s4]

## Connections

Distributive negotiation was introduced in Walton and McKersie's *A Behavioral Theory of Labor Negotiations*, as the sub-process that resolves conflicting interests.[^s3] Hüffmeier et al.'s meta-analysis supports its core tactics of high aspirations and controlled concessions, and Korobkin and Doherty's settlement study demonstrates how estimating the other side's bottom line and making a strong first offer shape who wins.[^s4][^s5] The power of opening offers is explained by anchoring.[^s1] It complements integrative negotiation: PON treats value claiming and value creation as two halves of the same skill, and contingent concessions can open a fixed-sum talk to trades.[^s2] It depends on knowing your BATNA, and it is the setting for reservation value, the zone of possible agreement, first offers and concession strategy.[^s2] Treating every deal as distributive is the mythical fixed pie, and arguing only from positions is positional bargaining - the reasons to check the issue really is single before claiming.
