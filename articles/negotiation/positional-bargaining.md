# Positional bargaining

Positional bargaining is negotiating by positions: each side states what it wants, argues for it and gives ground step by step until the two meet, usually near the middle.[^s1] The classic case is haggling over a price, where a buyer offering 10 and a seller asking 30 settle at 20.[^s2] At work it shows up whenever two people trade numbers (days of a shared resource, a budget line, a deadline, a rate) without ever saying what the number is for.[^s1][^s2] The problem is not that positions exist but that the conversation never gets beneath them.[^s1]

## Origin

The term was popularised by Roger Fisher, William Ury and Bruce Patton in *Getting to Yes*, whose first chapter warns readers not to bargain over positions and defines the pattern as taking a position, arguing for it and conceding toward a compromise.[^s1] The book presented principled, interest-based negotiation as the alternative, and later teaching materials from the Harvard Program on Negotiation and the University of Colorado's Beyond Intractability project kept the contrast between positional and interest-based bargaining.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

The *Getting to Yes* critique, as summarised by the Program on Negotiation, names three costs.[^s1] First, agreements are less wise: once people have defended a position in public, retreating feels like losing face, so they cling to it and overlook gains they could have had.[^s1] Second, it is inefficient: extreme openings and small counter-offers stretch talks out, and every concession becomes a separate decision.[^s1][^s2] Third, it strains relationships: the exchange turns into a contest of wills, and a side that feels it gave up too much carries resentment into the next deal.[^s1] Settlements often amount to a mechanical split of the difference rather than an answer to what either side needed.[^s2] Inside an organisation, where the same colleagues negotiate again next quarter, that relational cost compounds.[^s1]

## How to spot it

- Both sides open with a number or a demand and no reason attached.[^s1]
- Moves are framed as concessions ("I can go to three and a half") rather than as options.[^s2]
- The talk narrows to a single issue even though timing, scope or other terms could be traded.[^s1][^s2]
- The likely outcome is the midpoint between the opening positions, whether or not the midpoint works for anyone.[^s2]
- People defend earlier statements ("I already said four") more than they test whether they still fit.[^s1]

## How to fix it

- **Ask what the position protects.** Ask why the other side wants what it is asking for, and state your own reasons; *Getting to Yes* illustrates this with two sisters who split an orange when one wanted only the juice and the other only the peel.[^s1]
- **Widen the issues.** Bring timing, scope, sequencing or other terms onto the table so trade-offs become possible, instead of fighting over one number.[^s1]
- **Hold firm on interests, not on the number.** De Dreu, Weingart and Kwon's meta-analysis found that negotiators who cared about the other side's outcome reached higher joint gains, but only when they also resisted yielding; being cooperative is not the same as giving in.[^s4]
- **Signal the kind of deal you want.** Say early that you are looking for an outcome both sides can live with.[^s1]
- **Recognise a genuine split.** Where interests really are opposed and widening the discussion would only polarise it, a focused positional exchange aimed at a fair compromise can be the practical choice.[^s2]

## Evidence

Most anti-positional advice comes from practitioner teaching, so the claims need care.[^s1] A 2000 meta-analysis of 28 studies supports part of it: negotiators with a prosocial motive were less contentious, solved more problems and reached higher joint outcomes than egoistic negotiators, provided they also resisted yielding.[^s4] But in purely distributive deals, hard bargaining pays. Hüffmeier and colleagues' 2014 meta-analysis of 34 studies found that hardline strategies won better individual economic results, while softline strategies produced better socioemotional outcomes.[^s3] Law professor James J. White's 1984 review of *Getting to Yes* praised its critique of traditional negotiating behaviour but called the book naive about the hardest, value-dividing part of negotiation.[^s5] The fair reading: positional bargaining often costs joint value and goodwill, yet the claim that it always loses outruns the evidence where there is little to create and much to claim.[^s3][^s5]

## Connections

Positional bargaining sits under **negotiation** and was **introduced in** *Getting to Yes* (`cm:getting-to-yes`), which named and criticised it.[^s1] It **contrasts with integrative negotiation** (`cm:integrative-negotiation`), the interest-based approach that looks for value both sides can share.[^s2] Because it turns talks into a contest of wills and leaves resentment, it **undermines rapport** (`cm:rapport`).[^s1] The case against it is **supported by De Dreu, Weingart and Kwon (2000)** (`cm:de-dreu-weingart-kwon-2000`), whose meta-analysis linked cooperative but firm motives to better joint outcomes.[^s4] It is **challenged by Hüffmeier et al. (2014)** (`cm:huffmeier-et-al-2014`), which found hard tactics win economically in distributive deals,[^s3] and by White's review **The Pros and Cons of Getting to YES** (`cm:the-pros-and-cons-of-getting-to-yes`).[^s5] The remedies already in the graph are **interests vs. positions** and **principled negotiation**, while **distributive negotiation** and **concession strategy** describe how to claim value well when a split is genuinely fixed.[^s1][^s3]
