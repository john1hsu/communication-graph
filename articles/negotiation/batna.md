steps:
  - Before the first meeting, list every realistic thing you could do if no deal is reached - other vendors, other candidates, building it in-house, waiting, or doing nothing.
  - Translate each alternative into the same terms as the deal on the table (total cost, scope, risk, timing, effort to switch) so you are comparing like with like.
  - Pick the single best alternative - that is your BATNA - and from it set your reservation value, the worst deal you would still accept over walking away.
  - Spend some preparation time improving the BATNA itself (get a real competing quote, line up a second option) rather than only describing it.
  - Estimate the other side's BATNA too, and ask about their alternatives, so you know how much they need this deal.
  - Anchor your opening ask on your goal, not on your BATNA; keep a weak BATNA to yourself and never invent an alternative you do not have.
  - Re-check the BATNA whenever new information arrives during the talks, and walk away only when an offer is genuinely worse than it.
example:
  situation: An operations manager is renegotiating an annual software contract after the vendor proposes a 12% price rise.
  before: "We really can't move away from you, so please do what you can on the price. What's the best you can offer?"
  after: "We've priced a comparable tool that would cost us about 8% less in year one, including migration. We'd rather stay - the team knows your product - so we're looking for a renewal at last year's rate with the new reporting module included. What would make that work on your side?"

# BATNA

Your BATNA - best alternative to a negotiated agreement - is what you will actually do if the current negotiation ends without a deal. Working it out in advance tells you whether an offer is worth accepting, how much leverage you have, and when to walk away.[^s1][^s2] Developing a BATNA is therefore less a concept to know than a preparation routine: find your real alternatives, compare them honestly with the deal on the table, and improve them where you can.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Roger Fisher and William Ury, later joined by Bruce Patton, introduced the term in their book *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*, now in its third edition.[^s1][^s2] They presented the alternative as the standard against which any proposed agreement should be measured.[^s3] The Program on Negotiation at Harvard Law School still teaches it as a core preparation step.[^s2]

## Why it matters

At work most negotiations are internal or commercial and quiet: a contract renewal, a deadline with another team, a job offer, a scope change with a client. Without a clear alternative, people tend to accept a worse deal than they could have walked away from, or reject one that beat every option they actually had.[^s3] Knowing the other side's alternatives matters just as much: if theirs is weak, you have more room; if it is strong, you learn early whether a deal is even feasible.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Start by listing realistic alternatives, not hypothetical ones, then translate each into the same terms as the offer - total cost, coverage, timing, switching effort - before deciding which is best.[^s2] Subramanian's example is a homeowner who nearly switched insurers for a policy 30% cheaper, until a like-for-like reading of limits and exclusions showed the incumbent's renewal was the better deal.[^s2] From the best alternative, set a reservation value - the worst agreement you would still take over walking away - so you neither accept a deal worse than your alternative nor reject one that beats it.[^s3]

Then work on the alternative itself - get the competing quote, line up the second candidate - rather than just talking about it, and reassess it as the talks reveal new information.[^s2][^s3] On disclosure, PON's guidance is to keep a weak BATNA private, mention a strong one later rather than as an opening threat, and never bluff about alternatives you do not have, because the reputational cost outlasts any one deal.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- Judging an alternative by its headline number instead of its full terms.[^s2]
- Treating BATNA analysis as a one-off task done before the first meeting.[^s2]
- Letting the other side talk you out of your alternatives without checking their claims - they have an incentive to make your options look worse.[^s3]
- Brandishing the BATNA early, which can harden the other side and sound like a threat.[^s3]
- Letting a weak alternative set your opening ask (see Evidence).[^s5][^s6]

## Evidence

Laboratory studies broadly support the core idea. In a 1996 experiment, Brett, Pinkley and Jackofsky found that negotiators who had an alternative earned higher individual payoffs and claimed a larger share of the joint outcome than those without one; combining an alternative with a specific goal or high self-efficacy raised individual share further but also produced more impasses.[^s4]

The effect is not simply "any alternative helps". Across several experiments, Schaerer, Swaab and Galinsky (2015) found that sellers with a weak alternative felt more powerful than sellers with none, yet made lower first offers and reached less profitable deals - the weak alternative acted as a low anchor.[^s5][^s6] Focusing on an ideal target price instead of the weak alternative reduced, but did not remove, the penalty.[^s6] So the evidence status here is mixed: a strong BATNA helps, but a weak one should inform your walk-away point, not your opening ask. Most supporting studies are simulations with students or online participants, so effect sizes in real workplace negotiations are uncertain.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

BATNA is a building block of principled negotiation and of negotiation preparation generally, introduced in *Getting to Yes* by Roger Fisher and William Ury.[^s1][^s2] It is the basis for your reservation value and, together with the other side's alternatives, for judging whether any zone of possible agreement exists.[^s3] Brett, Pinkley and Jackofsky's experiment demonstrates the advantage of having an alternative, while Schaerer, Swaab and Galinsky's anchoring results challenge the blanket advice to always bring one and show that it works best alongside an ambitious, goal-based first offer.[^s4][^s5]
