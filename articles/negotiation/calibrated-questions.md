steps:
  - Before the meeting, list the two or three points where you expect resistance and draft one "how" or "what" question for each, written so it carries no blame or hidden answer.
  - Open each sticking point with a question about their constraints or goals ("What's driving the June date for you?") rather than with your counter-position.
  - When asked for something you cannot give, reply with a question that asks them to solve it with you ("How would you suggest we fit that into a fixed budget?") instead of a flat no.
  - Swap "why" and yes/no questions for "what" versions - "What makes you ask?" rather than "Why do you ask?", "What would need to be true to go ahead?" rather than "Can we go ahead?".
  - Ask implementation questions before closing ("How will this get signed off on your side?", "What could get in the way after we agree?") so you learn who else must say yes.
  - Stop after each question, let the silence work, and summarise their answer back before asking the next one.
example:
  situation: A project manager at an IT services firm hears from a client's operations director that the firm must add a new reporting module to the current fixed-price phase at no extra cost.
  before: "No, that's out of scope - can't you just push it to phase two?"
  after: "I can see the module matters for your quarter-end. How would you like us to fit it into this phase, given the budget and the go-live date are both fixed? And who else on your side would need to agree to a change in scope?"
  why: The flat refusal and yes/no question invite a power struggle and a one-word answer; the calibrated questions put the real constraint on the table, ask the director to help solve it, and surface the decision-makers who are not in the room.
selfCheck:
  - Could my question be answered with a single word, or does it ask them to explain or propose something?
  - Does my wording hide an accusation or a "right answer" that would make them defensive?
  - Am I asking because I want to learn and solve the problem together, or to corner them?
  - Have I asked how the agreement will actually be implemented and who else must approve it?

# Calibrated questions

Calibrated questions are "how" and "what" questions a negotiator words in advance to make the other side think aloud about a problem and take part in solving it.[^s1] Instead of stating a demand or refusing, you ask something like "How am I supposed to do that?" or "What would have to be true before we move forward?", which cannot be answered with one word and shifts the work of finding a solution onto the counterpart.[^s2][^s3] The practitioners who coined the term avoid "who", "when", "where" and "which" openers because those invite short factual answers.[^s1]

## Origin

The technique was named and popularised by former FBI hostage negotiator Chris Voss, and the Black Swan Group's own account draws it from chapter 7 of his book *Never Split the Difference*.[^s1] It builds on the much older advice in negotiation scholarship to prefer open questions, avoid leading or loaded wording, and follow up with probes when answers are thin.[^s4] What Voss added is a narrower purpose: questions tuned to give the counterpart a sense of control and ownership of the problem while you still steer what gets discussed.[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

Negotiators who ask about the other side's interests reach better deals. In Leigh Thompson's two 1991 experiments, seeking information made negotiators more accurate about the other party and produced more integrative agreements, even when only one side asked, and accuracy was closely tied to joint outcomes.[^s5] Calibrated questions are a practical way to do that seeking under pressure. They also replace three common habits that close down talks: yes/no questions that get a flat "no", yes-ladders that make people feel pushed, and "why" questions that sound like a demand for justification.[^s2] A question such as "How does this fit?" can reveal that the person across the table must check with a manager, and "How do we know your team is on board?" tests whether the deal will survive once it leaves the room.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Prepare the questions for your likely sticking points before you meet, because good wording is hard to improvise once tension rises.[^s4] Use them at every stage: early to set a collaborative tone, in the middle to explore options, and late to troubleshoot blockers.[^s2] Black Swan trainers describe two useful families: implementation questions that make the other side think ahead ("What happens if this slips?") and questions that return them to an earlier commitment ("When you agreed to the March plan, what should I have expected?").[^s2] When someone asks you a pointed question, "What makes you ask?" usually reveals more of their motive than "Why do you ask?".[^s3] Keep each question single; bundling an open question with a closed one tends to get only the closed part answered.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

The commonest error is a disguised demand - "How could you possibly think that's fair?" is a "how" question but an accusation in effect, and PON advice warns that loaded wording breeds defensiveness.[^s4] Another is overuse: a string of questions with no listening or summary between them feels like an interrogation. Wording also signals what you assume. Minson and colleagues found in four studies (2018) that questions presuming a problem drew more honest disclosure than neutral or positively worded ones, because askers seemed more assertive and informed; but presumptive questions about rare behaviours made askers look worse.[^s6] Finally, the "illusion of control" framing in practitioner material can tip into manipulation.[^s3] The ethical line is to use these questions to learn and to solve a real shared problem, never to hide material facts or to trap someone into a concession.

## Evidence and limits

There is no controlled study of calibrated questions as a named technique; the evidence is Voss's field experience and trainer accounts, so it is rated practitioner.[^s1][^s2] The surrounding research is supportive but indirect: asking about interests improves joint gains,[^s5] and question wording measurably changes what people disclose.[^s6] Because the scripts come from crisis negotiation rather than routine business deals, treat them as starting points; a stream of "how" questions to a senior client, or in a culture that expects direct answers, may read as evasive. Mixing in a closed question when you need clarity or commitment is still sound advice.[^s4]

## Connections

Calibrated questions are a specialised form of open-ended questions and assume you can already ask them well, and they stand in contrast to closed yes/no questions.[^s2][^s4] They were introduced by Chris Voss in *Never Split the Difference*,[^s1] are used throughout negotiation to test buy-in and implementation,[^s3] and help prevent the defensiveness that "why" questions can provoke.[^s2] Thompson's 1991 experiments supply the evidence that information-seeking questions lead to better integrative outcomes.[^s5] In practice they pair with labeling emotions and mirroring from the same toolkit, with separating interests from positions, and with follow-up questions that dig into the answers.[^s1][^s4]
