# Mythical fixed-pie bias

The mythical fixed-pie bias is the assumption that a negotiation is purely win-lose: whatever the other party gains comes straight out of your share. Research on negotiator judgment found that most people walk into a negotiation expecting the other side's interests to be completely opposed to their own, even when the issues on the table allow trades that help both sides.[^s1] The belief is "mythical" because most workplace negotiations involve several issues that the two sides value differently, so the pie can usually be enlarged before it is divided.[^s6]

## Origin

Harvard Business School professor Max Bazerman, working with Margaret Neale, popularised the label "mythical fixed pie" in negotiation teaching, describing it as a win-lose mindset that makes parties fight over existing value instead of creating more.[^s6] The empirical groundwork came from Leigh Thompson and Reid Hastie (1990), whose two studies measured what negotiators believed about each other. They named the belief that interests are directly opposed the *fixed-sum error*, and separated it from a second, unrelated mistake, the *incompatibility error*: failing to notice issues on which both sides actually want the same thing.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The bias leaves value on the table. Thompson and Hastie found that negotiators who discovered the other side's real interests early earned more, and that a substantial share settled for worse terms on issues where both parties wanted the same outcome.[^s1] Thompson and Hrebec (1996) called this the lose-lose effect: an agreement that is worse for both people than another option they could easily have reached. Their meta-analytic review of 32 experiments showed such agreements are common, and that they hurt both individuals and organisations.[^s2] In practice the bias turns multi-issue deals (a vendor contract, a project hand-off, a job offer) into haggling over one number, and can push people to walk away when a better deal existed.[^s6]

## Evidence

The core finding is well replicated across decades of laboratory negotiation research. Thompson and Hastie (1990) documented the fixed-sum belief and showed it is revised mostly in the first few minutes of interaction.[^s1] The 1996 meta-analysis of 32 experiments confirmed that failing to spot shared interests is widespread and that practice reduces it only partly.[^s2] The bias is flexible rather than fixed: De Dreu, Koole and Steinel (2000) showed in two experiments that holding negotiators accountable for how they negotiated raised their motivation to be accurate, weakened fixed-pie perceptions and produced more integrative agreements.[^s3] Context also moderates it. Kern, Brett, Weingart and Eck (2020), across three studies plus an internal meta-analysis that included unpublished studies, found that negotiators in multiparty settings arrived with weaker fixed-pie perceptions than negotiators in pairs.[^s5] Most of this evidence comes from negotiation simulations, so effect sizes in real deals will vary.[^s2][^s5]

## How to spot it

- You describe the deal as a single number (price, headcount, budget) even though delivery dates, scope, risk or support terms are also in play.[^s6]
- You assume that whatever the other side asks for must be bad for you, without checking.[^s1]
- You agree to a compromise on an issue where you never asked what they actually preferred; afterwards it turns out you both wanted the same thing.[^s1][^s2]
- Every concession feels like a loss, and the talk is only about splitting the difference.[^s6]

A typical example: two managers negotiate how a shared analyst's time is split next quarter. Each assumes the other wants as many hours as possible, so they argue over a 50/50 versus 60/40 split. Neither asks *when* the work is needed; one team's peak is in the first month and the other's is in the third, so a schedule-based split would give both teams more of what they need.[^s1][^s6]

## How to fix it

- **List every issue first.** Before talking numbers, write down all the issues and both sides' likely tangible and intangible interests; look for differences in priorities, timing or risk that make trades possible.[^s6]
- **Ask, and also share.** Thompson (1991) found that both asking about the other side's priorities and volunteering information about your own made negotiators' judgments more accurate and produced better joint outcomes, even when only one side did it, and those who shared did not lose out individually.[^s4]
- **Test your assumptions early.** Because most learning about the other side happens in the first few minutes, use the opening to check what they care about rather than to state a position.[^s1]
- **Raise your own accuracy motivation.** Imagine you will have to explain to a peer or your manager how you reached the deal, not only what you got; accountability for the process weakened fixed-pie thinking in experiments.[^s3]
- **Know your walk-away point.** Set a reservation value and an ambitious aspiration so that exploring trades does not feel like giving ground.[^s6]

These steps stay ethical: the aim is to discover real shared and complementary interests, not to mislead the other side about yours.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a **cognitive bias** (`cm:cognitive-bias`) that operates in negotiation, which it **requires** as background (`cm:negotiation`).[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** **Thompson and Hastie (1990)** (`cm:thompson-hastie-1990`), the study that measured the fixed-sum error.[^s1] Also **demonstrated by** **Kern, Brett, Weingart and Eck (2020)** (`cm:kern-brett-weingart-eck-2020`), which showed the perception is weaker in multiparty settings.[^s5]
- **Supported by** the **Thompson and Hrebec (1996)** meta-analysis (`cm:thompson-hrebec-1996`), and it **causes** the **lose-lose agreement** (`cm:lose-lose-agreement`) that review describes.[^s2]
- **Explained by** **motivated information processing** (`cm:motivated-information-processing`): negotiators fall back on the fixed-pie default unless they are motivated to process information accurately.[^s3]
- **Introduced by** **Max Bazerman** (`cm:max-bazerman`), who named it in negotiation teaching.[^s6]
- **Undermines** **integrative negotiation** (`cm:integrative-negotiation`) by keeping parties focused on dividing value rather than creating it.[^s6]
- **Related:** **interests vs. positions** (`cm:interests-vs-positions`) and **multiple equivalent simultaneous offers** (`cm:multiple-equivalent-simultaneous-offers`) are remedies for it; **distributive negotiation** (`cm:distributive-negotiation`) is the rare case where the pie really is fixed; the **ZOPA** (`cm:zone-of-possible-agreement`), **salary negotiation** (`cm:salary-negotiation`), **egocentric bias** (`cm:egocentric-bias`), **anchoring** (`cm:anchoring-and-adjustment`) and **Thompson (1991)** (`cm:thompson-1991`) on information exchange are closely linked.[^s4][^s6]
