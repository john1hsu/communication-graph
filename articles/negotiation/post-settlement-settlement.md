steps:
  - Close and record the current deal first, so both sides know exactly what they keep if the follow-up talk goes nowhere.
  - Propose the follow-up in plain terms - the signed terms stand, and anything new replaces them only if both of you prefer it.
  - Name the terms that were settled quickly or by default (timing, payment schedule, scope, service levels, risk-sharing) and invite the other side to add their own.
  - Share which of those terms matter most to you and ask what matters most to them, now that neither side can lose the deal by being open.
  - Draft one or two revised packages that leave both parties at least as well off as the signed deal, and check each against both sides' stated priorities.
  - Adopt a revision only with clear agreement from both sides; if either hesitates, keep the original deal and thank them for looking.
example:
  situation: A team lead has just agreed a 12-month software contract with a vendor's account manager at an agreed price with standard quarterly invoicing and a March start.
  before: "Great, we're signed. Actually, one more thing - can you knock another 5% off if we pay early?"
  after: "We're happy with what we signed, and it stands either way. Before we file it, would you be open to a quick look at whether any terms could work better for both of us? For example, we'd value an earlier start more than the payment schedule - is there anything on your side that you'd trade for that?"
  why: The "before" reads as a last-minute squeeze for a one-sided concession; the "after" guarantees the vendor keeps the signed deal, shares a priority first and asks for theirs, which makes a trade that improves both sides possible.

# Post-settlement settlement

A post-settlement settlement (PSS) is a second round of negotiation that starts only after the parties have already reached an agreement. The signed deal becomes a safe floor: either side can walk back to it at any moment, and a new package replaces it only if both prefer the new terms.[^s1][^s2] The aim is to recover value that the first round left on the table because each side was guarding its priorities while the deal was still at risk.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The idea comes from the Harvard decision scientist Howard Raiffa, who proposed it in *The Art and Science of Negotiation* (1985) as a remedy for agreements reached with incomplete information.[^s1] He also set it out in a short paper, "Post-settlement settlements", in the first volume of *Negotiation Journal* in 1985.[^s4] Later negotiation scholarship broadened the idea to include bringing in a mediator after two parties settle, to see whether the outcome can be improved.[^s4][^s5]

## Why it matters

During a live negotiation, people often hold back what they care about most, fearing the other side will use it against them; the result is a deal that works but is not as good as it could be for either party.[^s1][^s3] Once a deal is signed, that fear shrinks, because neither side can lose the agreement by being candid. Program on Negotiation writers argue that this guarantee is exactly what makes negotiators more willing to reveal their real priorities.[^s1] In workplace terms, it is a low-risk way to turn a supplier contract, a service agreement or an internal resourcing deal into one that fits both teams better.

## How to apply it

Start by confirming the current deal so it is clearly the default.[^s2] Then frame the follow-up as optional and informal: you are not reopening the agreement, only checking whether there is a better version you would both sign.[^s2] Look at terms that were settled quickly or never discussed - in a house sale, for example, the closing date, seller financing or furnishings - because these are where trades that help both sides usually hide.[^s1] Share your own priorities first and ask for theirs, then build revisions that leave both parties at least as well off. If either side is unsure, the original deal simply stands.[^s1][^s2]

A neutral third party can help. A mediator or facilitator can collect each side's priorities privately and propose an improvement, which can make the step feel less like a renewed bargaining session.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Using it as a last-minute squeeze.** The most common risk is that the other side reads the proposal as an attempt to extract extra concessions. Say explicitly that nothing changes without full agreement from both of you.[^s2] A demand for a one-sided discount after signing is not a PSS.
- **Trying it after a bruising hard bargain.** After a tough, win-lose negotiation, switching to a cooperative mood can be hard, and goodwill from the first round is what makes the follow-up work.[^s1][^s2]
- **Waiting too long.** Even cooperative negotiators run out of energy once a deal is done, so propose the follow-up soon after signing, before attention moves on.[^s1]
- **Reopening a deal that is already efficient.** If the first round explored the issues thoroughly, there may be little left to gain, and follow-up talks tend to succeed more often when the original agreement was already integrative.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is thinner than the advice. A 2016 laboratory study of post-settlement negotiations reported that earlier experiments found these follow-up rounds rarely produced a new agreement.[^s3] In the authors' experiment, success depended on how actively the negotiators engaged in the follow-up phase, how integrative the first agreement had been, the negotiators' gender and their perceptions of each other's language proficiency. When follow-up talks did occur, negotiators tended to enlarge joint gains rather than shift value from one side to the other.[^s3] Legal scholarship also notes that the concept of "joint gains" is harder to define in practice than the theory assumes, and offers guidance for third parties trying to help.[^s5] The PON articles are practitioner guidance drawing on Raiffa rather than field tests.[^s1][^s2] Taken together: the logic is sound and the downside is small, but expect many attempts to end with the original deal unchanged.

## Connections

The technique was introduced by Howard Raiffa in *The Art and Science of Negotiation*.[^s1] It is a form of integrative negotiation, extending the search for trades between differently valued issues beyond the moment of signing, and it depends on knowing interests rather than positions.[^s1][^s3] It is related to the BATNA idea: the signed deal works as a guaranteed fallback for the second round, the way a BATNA does for the first.[^s2] Like multiple equivalent simultaneous offers, it is a way to discover the other side's priorities without giving ground. It works against the mythical fixed-pie bias, since its success depends on finding gains for both sides rather than redistributing them.[^s3] Its effectiveness is examined in the 2016 study by Gettinger, Filzmoser and Koeszegi,[^s3] and third-party intervenors such as mediators can run it on the parties' behalf.[^s4][^s5]
