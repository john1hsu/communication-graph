steps:
  - Before the meeting, write down your opening position, your target and your walk-away point, and the total amount you are prepared to concede between them.
  - List what you want back for each possible concession - a longer term, faster payment, a reference, reduced scope - so you never give without a matching ask ready.
  - Plan the moves as a sequence of real steps that get smaller as you approach your limit, rather than one large jump, and hold some of the concession budget back.
  - When you concede, name it - say what it costs you and what it gives them - and state exactly what you need in return, or make it explicitly conditional ("we can do X if you can do Y").
  - Answer the other side's concessions in kind and on time; acknowledge them and move a proportionate amount, rather than pocketing them in silence.
  - Keep every statement about your costs and limits true; stop conceding when the next step would cross your walk-away point.
example:
  situation: A software vendor's account manager is renewing a contract with a client who has asked for a 15% discount.
  before: "OK, we can do 15% off. Hopefully that works for your budget - shall I send the paperwork?"
  after: "I can't get to 15%, but I can offer 6% off the renewal, which comes out of our services margin. To make that work, we'd need a two-year term instead of one. If the two-year term is a problem, tell me what matters most and we can look at the payment schedule instead."
  why: The first reply gives away the whole concession budget at once, labels nothing and asks for nothing, so the client learns the price was soft and has no reason to give back. The second makes a smaller, named move tied to a specific return, leaves room for later steps, and invites the client to reveal what they value.
selfCheck:
  - Do I know my total concession budget and walk-away point before I make the first move?
  - For the concession I am about to make, what exactly am I asking for in return?
  - Have I said clearly what this move costs me, without exaggerating it?
  - Are my steps getting smaller as I get closer to my limit, or am I making one big jump under pressure?
  - Have I acknowledged and reciprocated the concessions the other side has already made?

# Concession strategy

A concession strategy is a plan for how you will move from your opening position towards agreement: how much you are willing to give up in total, in what size steps, how quickly, and what you will ask for in return each time.[^s1] Concessions are the main way most negotiations - a contract renewal, a project deadline, a budget request, a job offer - actually close, so how you make them shapes both the deal you get and how the other side feels about it.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Concession making has been a core topic in negotiation research for decades; meta-analytic work on "hardline" (high demands, few and small concessions) versus "softline" (lower demands, larger concessions) bargaining draws on older theories such as level-of-aspiration theory and the graduated-reciprocation model of tension reduction.[^s2] Practical guidance has been distilled by Harvard Business School professor Deepak Malhotra into four rules: label your concessions, demand and define reciprocity, make concessions contingent, and give them in instalments.[^s1] More recent experiments look at the shape of concession sequences and how recipients read them.[^s3][^s5]

## Why it matters

Concessions are signals as well as value. The other side uses your pattern of moves to guess where your limit is and how much more there is to get.[^s3] Concessions also carry social meaning: in Kwon and Weingart's 2004 experiments, the timing of a counterpart's concessions and the reason given for them shaped how negotiators valued the item, how satisfied they were with the outcome and partner, and why they thought the counterpart had moved.[^s4] A concession that arrives too easily can lower the perceived value of what you are giving and leave the other side less satisfied, not more.[^s4]

## How to apply it

**Plan the budget.** Before you start, fix your opening, target and walk-away point, which bounds how much you can give up in total.[^s1][^s3] Decide what you want back for each move.

**Label each move.** Do not assume the other side will notice or credit what you gave up; say what it costs you and what it does for them. Malhotra notes that people tend to reciprocate according to the benefit they perceive, so an unnamed concession often earns nothing.[^s1]

**Ask for something specific in return.** Either ask directly ("in exchange, could we have an extra month on each milestone?") or make the concession explicitly conditional - useful when trust is low or the deal is one-off. Malhotra cautions that making every concession conditional can look self-serving in a long relationship.[^s1]

**Move in steps, not jumps.** Splitting a concession into smaller instalments gives you repeated chances to show flexibility and lets you keep some of the budget. Malhotra links this to Tversky and Kahneman's work suggesting people prefer gains spread out.[^s1] Steps that get smaller as you approach your limit tell the other side you are nearing it: in seven studies with 2,311 participants, Tey and colleagues (2021) found that recipients of decreasing concessions made less ambitious counteroffers and, in one study, reached worse deals.[^s3]

**Reciprocate theirs.** Acknowledge and answer the other side's concessions. In Hüffmeier and colleagues' meta-analysis, cooperative ("softline") negotiators got their best economic results when they reciprocated accurately rather than giving ground regardless.[^s2]

**Stay honest.** A concession pattern is persuasive because it is read as information about your real limits. Inventing costs, faking a final offer or shrinking steps you could easily exceed is deception; the strategy here is to make true moves legible, not to mislead. Malhotra's advice to label concessions is about making real costs visible, and the reciprocity it relies on depends on the other side trusting what you say.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- Making one large concession early to "get it done", which gives away the budget and can lower the other side's valuation of the deal.[^s1][^s4]
- Conceding without naming it or asking for anything back.[^s1]
- Making every concession conditional in a long-term relationship, which can read as transactional.[^s1]
- Holding out with tiny, grudging moves: Mertes, Kunz and Hüffmeier (2023) found that low-value, distributive concession making increased impasses, through anger and negative judgments about the other party's character.[^s5]
- Ignoring the other side's concessions, which breaks the reciprocity that keeps both sides moving.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

The evidence is real but context-dependent. Hüffmeier and colleagues' 2014 meta-analysis of 34 studies (135 economic and 30 socioemotional effect sizes) found that tough bargaining tends to win more money while softer bargaining builds better relationships - a trade-off that planned, reciprocal concessions try to manage.[^s2] Tey et al.'s decreasing-concession effect held across several studies but shrank when concessions fell too slowly or too fast, when there were very few or very many rounds, and when recipients focused on their own target price.[^s3] Kwon and Weingart's role-play study only partly replicated their scenario study.[^s4] Most findings come from laboratory or role-play negotiations over a single price, so treat the specific patterns as starting points rather than rules.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

Concession strategy sits within distributive negotiation and negotiation preparation, and depends on knowing the zone of possible agreement and your reservation value, which set the space you can concede within.[^s3] It is explained by the norm of reciprocity: concessions work when they are noticed and returned.[^s1] Tey and colleagues' 2021 studies demonstrate how the shape of a concession sequence shifts the other side's expectations;[^s3] Hüffmeier et al.'s 2014 meta-analysis supports the value of accurate reciprocation;[^s2] Kwon and Weingart's 2004 experiments support careful timing and justification;[^s4] and Mertes et al.'s 2023 study supports avoiding stonewalling, which raises impasse risk.[^s5] It follows naturally from the first offer, and in multi-issue deals it pairs with integrative negotiation and multiple equivalent simultaneous offers, where trading packages can replace one-issue-at-a-time giving.[^s1]
