# Checking understanding

Checking understanding is the skill of verifying, instead of assuming, that a message landed as intended. It runs in both directions: checking that the other person understood you (asking for a read-back, a restatement or their next step) and checking that you understood them (restating a request before you act on it). Specific techniques such as teach-back, paraphrasing and the check-back sit under it; the skill is knowing *when* a check is needed and choosing one that produces evidence rather than politeness.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The skill draws on two traditions. Psycholinguists showed that people systematically overrate how well they communicate: in a study by Boaz Keysar and Anne Henly at the University of Chicago, published in *Psychological Science* in 2002, speakers who believed an ambiguous sentence had been understood were wrong close to half the time, and Keysar's advice was to treat your own message as far less transparent than it feels.[^s3] In safety-critical teamwork, aviation and then healthcare formalised the check as closed-loop communication; the U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's TeamSTEPPS curriculum teaches it as a three-part check-back in which the sender states the information, the receiver repeats it, and the sender confirms it.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Misunderstanding is usually invisible to both sides. In a 2022 *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* study, listeners in pairs sharing a language picked the intended meaning of ambiguous phrases 44% of the time but believed they were right 85% of the time, and speakers also overestimated their success; the overconfidence persisted even when speaker and listener shared no language at all.[^s2] Because neither party feels the gap, it surfaces later as rework, missed deadlines or a wrong decision. Checking moves that discovery to the moment when fixing it is cheap.[^s2][^s3]

## What good looks like

- **Targeted checks.** A skilled communicator checks the one to three details that would cause real harm if wrong (a figure, a date, an owner, a sequence) rather than quizzing people on everything.[^s1]
- **Open invitations.** "What questions do you have?" assumes questions are normal; "Do you have any questions?" invites a reflexive no. Medical students taught through a longitudinal communication curriculum used the open phrase in 51.9% of observed encounters, against 6.7% of a didactic-curriculum comparison group (Coleman and colleagues, 2022).[^s4]
- **Evidence, not receipt.** The check asks for a restatement or a next step, and the sender closes the loop by confirming or correcting it.[^s1]
- **Checking inbound too.** Before acting on a request, restate what you heard ("So you need the Q3 numbers by region, not by product, by Thursday?").[^s1]
- **Channel awareness.** AHRQ recommends check-backs for phone instructions and for emails and texts, where messages can be missed or garbled by typos and autocorrect.[^s1]

**Workplace example.** A manager ends a call by asking a contractor, "Clear?" and hears "Yep." Two weeks later the wrong file format arrives. A better close: "Before we hang up, can you play back the format and the deadline so I know I explained it properly?" The contractor says "PDF by the 20th," the manager corrects it to "editable Word file by the 20th," and the loop is closed while it still costs nothing.[^s1][^s3]

## How to practise it

1. Before any handover, instruction or decision, name the critical details that must survive the conversation.[^s1]
2. Swap "Any questions?" for "What questions do you have?", then wait through the pause.[^s4]
3. Frame the check as testing your explanation ("let me make sure I explained that well") so it does not feel like a quiz.[^s3]
4. For instructions, use a full check-back: say it, hear it repeated, then say "correct" or fix it.[^s1]
5. In email or chat, ask for a reply that restates the key point or names the next step, not just a thumbs-up.[^s1]
6. After a week of practice, review where a misunderstanding still slipped through and which detail you failed to check.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence that people overestimate understanding is strong and comes from controlled experiments.[^s2][^s3] Evidence that checking fixes the problem is more indirect and comes mainly from healthcare. In a study of pediatric trauma resuscitations, El-Shafy and colleagues concluded that closed-loop communication may make task completion faster and more efficient, and recommended building it into team-leader training.[^s6] Yet a 2013 *BMJ Open* study of in-situ trauma team training by Härgestam and colleagues found closed-loop communication was used infrequently even by teams that had already had teamwork training, so knowing the technique does not guarantee using it under pressure.[^s5] Transfer to office settings is plausible but largely untested, which is why the status here is mixed.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Checking understanding is a branch of questioning: it uses open questions to get evidence rather than assent.[^s4] It addresses the illusion of transparency, the gap between how clear a message feels to its sender and how clear it is, which Keysar's work documents.[^s3] Its case is supported by the illusion-of-understanding experiments of Lau and colleagues[^s2] and by the closed-loop findings of El-Shafy and colleagues.[^s6] It is applied in phone instructions and in email, which AHRQ singles out as channels where messages are easily missed or distorted,[^s1] and in patient education, where the open "What questions do you have?" invitation was tested.[^s4] Teach-back, paraphrasing, summarizing and meeting recaps are its everyday techniques; closed-loop communication is its formal team version.[^s1][^s5]
