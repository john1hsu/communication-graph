steps:
  - Start from the other person's own claim, in their words, and confirm you have it right before asking anything else.
  - Ask informational questions they can actually answer - what happened, what they have seen, what the numbers say - to bring concrete evidence into view.
  - Listen for surprises in the answers (an unexpected word, a fact that does not fit) and follow those rather than your planned script.
  - Summarise what has emerged so far, aloud or in writing, and ask them to correct it.
  - Ask a synthesising question - how does this fit with what you said at the start? - and let them draw the conclusion, even if it differs from yours.
  - If they reach a dead end, name the open question together and agree what evidence would settle it, rather than supplying the answer.
example:
  situation: A product manager insists a feature launch slipped because the engineering team is "too slow", and asks their manager to escalate.
  before: "I don't think that's fair. Engineering hit every sprint goal last quarter. The real issue was that requirements kept changing - you need to lock scope earlier."
  after: "Let's look at it together. What did the original plan assume about scope? How many requirement changes landed after sprint one? Looking at that list, what do you think drove most of the delay - and what would you change next time?"
  why: The first version tells the manager's diagnosis and invites defensiveness; the second walks through evidence the product manager can check, so the conclusion about late scope changes is theirs and more likely to change how they plan the next launch.

# Socratic questioning

Socratic questioning is a structured way of asking open, probing questions so that another person examines the assumptions and evidence behind their own view, rather than receiving your conclusion.[^s6] The questioner works from what the other person believes and lets them judge where the reasoning leads, which is why it is also taught as "guided discovery".[^s1][^s5] At work it is useful whenever someone holds a confident but untested belief - about a cause, a customer or a colleague - and telling them they are wrong would only harden it.

## Origin

The method takes its name from Socrates as portrayed in Plato's early dialogues. In the *elenchus*, Socrates starts from his partner's own claim to know something, secures agreement to a series of premises, and shows that those premises do not fit the original claim; the partner, not the audience, judges the outcome.[^s1] Many of these dialogues end in *aporia*, an honest impasse, and Socrates presents his own wisdom as awareness of what he does not know.[^s1] Teaching in the Western tradition has drawn on this dialectical questioning ever since, though modern teaching guides stress shared inquiry rather than the cross-examination of the original.[^s6]

In the twentieth century the method was adapted for cognitive therapy. Christine Padesky's 1993 keynote drew a widely cited distinction between using questions to *change minds* - steering someone to the questioner's preset conclusion - and using them to *guide discovery*, where curiosity drives the questions and the conclusions belong to the person answering.[^s5] Most workplace versions descend from that second, collaborative form.

## Why it matters

Conclusions people reach for themselves tend to fit their situation better and last longer than conclusions handed to them, which is the core of Padesky's case for guided discovery.[^s5] Colorado State University's teaching guidance frames the aim as productive discomfort: probing the values and assumptions under a view, which matters most in complex or uncertain questions where there is no fact to look up.[^s6] For managers, consultants and coaches, that makes it a way to challenge a flawed plan or diagnosis without the defensiveness that a flat contradiction provokes.

## How to apply it

Padesky describes four stages that translate well to a workplace conversation.[^s5]

1. **Ask informational questions.** Start with questions the person can answer from their own experience or data, which makes a vague concern concrete.[^s5]
2. **Listen for the unexpected.** Pay attention to surprising words, facts and reactions, and be willing to be surprised yourself.[^s5]
3. **Summarise.** Pause to recap what has emerged so both of you see the whole picture; Padesky notes this is the step questioners most often skip.[^s5]
4. **Ask synthesising questions.** Ask how the new information bears on the original belief, so the person revises it or forms a new one.[^s5]

In Socrates' version the questions begin from the partner's own statement and every premise needs their agreement, so start by restating their claim and check it.[^s1] The method suits complexity and uncertainty better than settling facts, so if the question has a known answer, share it.[^s6] In email or chat, a long string of probing questions reads as an interrogation; save the full sequence for live conversation and use one or two well-chosen questions in writing.

## Common mistakes

- **Steering to a predetermined answer.** Building a case toward the conclusion you already hold is leading questioning, and Padesky warns it produces quick agreement but weak change.[^s5]
- **Asking unanswerable questions.** Questions the person cannot answer undermine collaboration.[^s5]
- **Humiliating cross-examination.** Guides reject the intimidating law-school-professor caricature and the permanent devil's advocate; the questioner is not an opponent.[^s6]
- **Stopping before synthesis.** Skipping the final "so what does this mean?" leaves the person with information but no new conclusion.[^s5]

## Evidence

Direct evidence comes almost entirely from cognitive therapy. A 2015 narrative review concluded that, although the Socratic method is treated as a core part of cognitive behavioural therapy, no direct evidence yet showed it was beneficial there.[^s2] That same year, Braun and colleagues rated therapist Socratic questioning in early sessions with 55 depressed adults: sessions with more Socratic questioning than a client's own average were followed by greater symptom improvement, even after controlling for the therapeutic alliance.[^s3] A 2021 follow-up by the same lab, also with 55 clients, again found that Socratic questioning predicted session-to-session improvement, more strongly for clients with greater pessimistic bias.[^s4] Both studies are observational, small and run by one research group, so causation is not established.[^s3] No controlled studies were found testing the technique in workplace conversations, so its use at work rests on practitioner experience and this therapy evidence; hence the `mixed` status.

## Connections

- **Broader:** a disciplined form of questioning that relies on open-ended questions and careful listening as prerequisites.[^s5][^s6]
- **Introduced by** Socrates, through Plato's dialogues.[^s1]
- **Addresses** telling instead of asking: teaching guidance frames it as dialogue in place of lecturing.[^s6]
- **Contrasts with** leading questions, which steer toward the questioner's conclusion.[^s5]
- **Complements** summarizing, which is one of Padesky's four stages.[^s5]
- **Supported by** Braun et al. (2015) and Vittorio et al. (2021), and **challenged by** Clark and Egan's (2015) review of the evidence gap.[^s2][^s3][^s4]
- **Related:** humble inquiry shares its curiosity-first stance; coaching and follow-up questions use similar moves; paraphrasing supports the restating step.[^s5][^s6]
