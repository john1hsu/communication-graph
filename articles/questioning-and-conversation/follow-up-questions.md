steps:
  - Listen to the whole answer and note one specific word, claim or detail in it that you want to understand better.
  - Ask about that detail in their terms ("You said the handover was messy - what happened there?") instead of moving to your next prepared question.
  - Keep the probe neutral - "how did you decide?", "what did that look like?" - rather than suggesting the answer you expect.
  - Go one or two layers deeper on what matters, then summarise what you heard before switching topic.
  - Offer something of your own between questions so the exchange feels like a conversation, not an interrogation.
  - In formal settings such as structured interviews or reviews, plan the likely probes in advance and use the same ones for everyone.
example:
  situation: A manager's one-to-one with a team member who mentions the client launch "went okay, apart from the data issue".
  before: "Good to hear. Next thing - how are you getting on with the Q3 planning doc?"
  after: "What was the data issue, and how did the client react when it came up?"
  why: The first reply jumps to the manager's own agenda and loses the one signal of trouble; the follow-up picks up the speaker's own words, shows they were heard, and surfaces a risk while it is still small.

  - Was my probe neutral, or did it hint at the answer I wanted to hear?
  - Have I asked several questions in a row without sharing anything myself?

# Follow-up questions

A follow-up question builds on what the other person has just said instead of changing the subject: after "the launch went okay, apart from the data issue", it asks about the data issue. In the conversation studies that made the idea prominent, Huang and colleagues defined follow-ups as questions on a topic the partner had just raised, and contrasted them with "switch" questions that introduce an unrelated topic, "mirror" questions that bounce back a question just asked, and routine introductory questions.[^s1] In research interviewing the same move is called a probe - an expansive "tell me more about that" or a targeted "how did you arrive at that answer?".[^s5]

## Origin

Interviewers in survey research, counselling and personnel selection have long relied on probes to get beyond a first answer; survey methodologists, for example, distinguish probes planned in advance from reactive ones improvised in response to what a respondent says.[^s5] Selection practice built them into formal tools: the U.S. Office of Personnel Management describes the most structured interviews as asking every applicant the same predefined lead and follow-up questions, scored against benchmarks.[^s6] The interpersonal case for follow-ups came later, from a 2017 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* paper by Karen Huang, Michael Yeomans, Alison Wood Brooks, Julia Minson and Francesca Gino at Harvard.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Follow-ups do two jobs at once. They get information - the first answer is usually a headline, and the useful detail sits one question further down.[^s5] They also signal attention: you cannot ask a good follow-up without having listened, which is why the Harvard team argue they convey responsiveness - understanding, validation and care - more than other questions.[^s1] In online chats between strangers, people told to ask more questions asked proportionally more follow-ups, were rated as more responsive, and were liked more; follow-ups were the most common question type and the one most tied to liking.[^s1] Askers themselves did not predict that asking would make them more likeable, which helps explain why people under-use it.[^s1][^s4] Brooks has suggested the habit may help in management and job interviews, framing these as plausible applications rather than tested ones.[^s4]

## How to apply it

**Hook onto their words.** Pick one concrete detail from the answer and ask about it, reusing their phrasing; switch questions, by contrast, were linked to lower perceived responsiveness.[^s1] Expansive probes ("tell me more", "what did that look like?") and targeted ones ("how sure are you?", "how did you decide?") both work; choose by whether you need breadth or precision.[^s5]

**Keep probes neutral.** Survey guidance advises balanced wording ("was that easy or hard?"), avoiding speculative probes that steer people toward issues they had not raised, and asking only as many probes as you need.[^s5]

**Balance asking with sharing.** Brooks warns that a barrage of questions with no disclosure of your own can come across as guarded or invasive.[^s4] The original authors likewise suspect the benefit is curvilinear: past some point, more questions stop helping.[^s1]

**By setting.** In a formal interview or performance conversation, plan follow-ups ahead and use the same ones for every candidate, which supports fairness and rater agreement.[^s6] Across cultures, probes asking for direct opinions do not work equally well everywhere, so watch how people respond and adjust.[^s5] In writing - email, chat - one well-aimed follow-up is easier to answer than a list.

## Common mistakes

- **The pivot.** Acknowledging an answer ("great") and moving straight to your own agenda, which reads as not having listened.[^s1]
- **Leading probes.** "So the vendor caused the delay?" puts the answer in their mouth; neutral wording yields a truer account.[^s5]
- **Interrogation.** Stacking probe on probe without reflecting or sharing anything back.[^s4]
- **Improvised probes where fairness matters.** Ad hoc follow-ups in hiring let some candidates get easier questions than others.[^s6]

## Evidence

The experimental core is reasonably strong: two chat experiments with several hundred participants manipulated how many questions people asked, and responsiveness partly explained the liking effect.[^s1] An independent reanalysis challenged the field part of the work: Kluger and Malloy (2019) argued the speed-dating data needed a dyadic social-relations analysis, found that follow-up and switch questions were hard to separate statistically, and noted that for men asking more questions tended to predict fewer second-date offers - concluding that a second-date offer is not the same as liking.[^s3] The original team replied that the critique tested the wrong variables and misrepresented the results, and that all three studies still showed benefits of follow-ups.[^s2] The rating here is mixed: the effect is well supported in short getting-acquainted chats, while its size in real workplace conversations, and how it varies with status and culture, remains largely untested.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

Follow-up questions are a form of **questioning** (`cm:questioning`) and depend on **active listening** (`cm:active-listening`), which they **complement**: listening supplies the material, the follow-up shows it was heard.[^s4] Their effect on liking is **demonstrated by Huang et al. 2017** (`cm:huang-et-al-2017`) and **explained by perceived partner responsiveness** (`cm:perceived-partner-responsiveness`).[^s1] The finding is **supported by** the authors' 2019 reply (`cm:yeomans-et-al-2019`)[^s2] and **challenged by Kluger and Malloy 2019** (`cm:kluger-malloy-2019`).[^s3] They are **applied in job interviews** (`cm:job-interview`), where structured interviews standardise them.[^s6] They relate to **open-ended questions**, which open a topic that follow-ups then deepen; to **small talk** and **reciprocal self-disclosure**, which keep questioning balanced with sharing; and to **humble inquiry**, **paraphrasing** and **perspective-getting**, other ways of checking you have understood someone rather than assuming.[^s1][^s4][^s5]
