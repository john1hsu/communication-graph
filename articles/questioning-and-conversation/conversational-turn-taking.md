# Conversational turn-taking

Conversational turn-taking is the system people use, mostly without noticing, to decide who talks next and when. In ordinary talk the floor changes hands again and again, one person usually speaks at a time, overlaps are short, and many transitions happen with almost no gap and no overlap at all.[^s1] Replies typically start about 200 milliseconds after the previous turn ends, roughly the length of a blink.[^s3] Knowing how the system works explains why interruptions sting, why a three-second silence feels long, why video calls feel clumsy and why the same few people dominate a meeting.

## Origin

The idea was set out by sociologists Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson in a 1974 paper in *Language*, a founding text of conversation analysis.[^s1] They listed fourteen plain facts any model of conversation must explain, such as recurring speaker change and turn order and length not being fixed in advance, and proposed a model with two parts: a turn-constructional component (turns are built from units that can end at a "transition-relevance place") and a turn-allocation component.[^s1] At each such place an ordered set of rules applies: if the current speaker has picked the next speaker, that person should speak; if not, anyone may self-select and the first to start gets the turn; if nobody does, the current speaker may continue.[^s1]

Later work tested the model across cultures and in the lab. A 2009 study by Tanya Stivers and colleagues compared question-answer timing in ten languages, from major world languages to small indigenous communities, and found that every one avoided overlap and minimised silence, with average gaps differing by no more than about a quarter of a second from the cross-language mean.[^s2] Stephen Levinson's 2016 review argued that the system is ancient: infants take turns with caregivers at about six months, and turn alternation appears across the major primate lineages.[^s3]

## Why it matters

The timing is tight because the brain is working against the clock. Preparing even a single word takes about 600 milliseconds or more, so to reply within 200 milliseconds listeners must plan their answer while still taking in the other person's turn.[^s3] That compressed processing is why a late, hesitant or mistimed reply is noticed and read as meaningful. Stivers and colleagues suggest that the vivid stereotypes of "slow" or "fast" talking cultures likely come from sensitivity to quite small tempo differences.[^s2]

Timing also carries social meaning. In studies of 322 conversations between students who were mostly strangers, and 65 between friends, faster replies predicted feeling more connected and enjoying the talk more, and how quickly your partner answered mattered more than how quickly you did.[^s4] When outside listeners heard the same recordings with the gaps edited shorter, they rated the speakers as enjoying the conversation more and being more connected.[^s4]

At work the stakes show up in groups. A 2010 *Science* study of 699 people working in small groups found that how evenly members shared conversational turns was one of the factors linked to a group's performance across many tasks, while members' average or top individual intelligence mattered little.[^s6] This is one correlational study, so treat it as a reason to watch participation, not proof that enforcing equal airtime raises output.[^s6]

## How to use it at work

- **Allocate turns on purpose.** In a meeting, the turn-allocation rules mean that an open "Any thoughts?" goes to whoever self-selects fastest. Naming the next speaker ("Ana, how does this look from support?") uses the current-speaker-selects-next rule to bring in quieter people.[^s1][^s6]
- **Mark the end of your turn.** Finish with a clear question or a complete sentence so others can tell the floor is open; trailing off or stacking clauses delays the transition point.[^s1]
- **Expect friction on video.** Turn hand-offs take longer on Zoom than face to face, and even the tens of milliseconds of lag on a call may disrupt the brain's automatic turn timing.[^s5] Use hand-raise features or a speaking order, and leave a beat before you start so you do not talk over a delayed speaker.[^s5]
- **Read gaps carefully.** Because silence is minimised everywhere, a long pause after a request often signals reluctance or a problem; a fast reply often signals engagement.[^s2][^s4] Small tempo differences between colleagues, including across cultures, are not evidence of rudeness or disinterest.[^s2]

## Evidence

The core pattern is robust. Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson's model was built from close analysis of recorded talk,[^s1] and Stivers and colleagues confirmed the overlap-avoidance and gap-minimisation pattern across ten languages, with the same factors explaining variation in response speed in each.[^s2] Levinson's review draws together developmental and comparative evidence that the system predates and underlies language.[^s3] The social reading of response speed comes from one set of studies with mostly American students in polite, ten-minute get-acquainted talks; the authors note that arguments or negotiations may behave differently.[^s4] The video-call findings concern small transmission delays and come from a single lab; the authors do not test fixes.[^s5]

## Connections

Turn-taking is a core topic within **interpersonal communication** (`cm:interpersonal-communication`) and is **studied in conversation analysis** (`cm:conversation-analysis`), the field Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson helped found; it was **introduced by Harvey Sacks** (`cm:harvey-sacks`) and his co-authors.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by Stivers et al. 2009** (`cm:stivers-et-al-2009`), the ten-language comparison,[^s2] **supported by Levinson 2016** (`cm:levinson-2016`), the review on its origins and processing demands,[^s3] and **demonstrated by Templeton et al. 2022** (`cm:templeton-et-al-2022`), which tied response speed to felt connection.[^s4] It is **demonstrated by Boland et al.** (`cm:boland-et-al-zoom-turn-taking`) and **applied in video-call communication** (`cm:video-call-communication`), where lag disrupts turn timing.[^s5] **Interrupting** (`cm:interrupting`) is a breakdown of the system and **cooperative overlap** (`cm:cooperative-overlap`) a benign variant; **wait time** (`cm:wait-time`) and **silence and pauses** (`cm:silence-and-pauses`) deliberately stretch the minimal gap.[^s1][^s3] The group findings of **Woolley et al. 2010** (`cm:woolley-et-al-2010`) link it to **unequal participation** (`cm:unequal-participation`), **facilitation** (`cm:facilitation`) and **workplace meetings** (`cm:workplace-meetings`).[^s6] It also relates to **face-to-face conversation**, **Grice's cooperative principle** and **common ground**, the other implicit rules that keep talk coordinated.[^s1]
