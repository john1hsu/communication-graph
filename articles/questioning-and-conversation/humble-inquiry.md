steps:
  - Before the conversation, write down what you are assuming about the situation and the person, and mark which parts you have not actually checked.
  - Open with a question you do not know the answer to ("Walk me through how you approached this") instead of your verdict or fix.
  - Stop after the question and let the other person talk; resist filling the first silence with your own view.
  - Ask one genuine follow-up that builds on what they just said, rather than steering back to your agenda.
  - Acknowledge what you heard in your own words before you add any opinion, request or decision.
  - Only then tell - share your view or ask for what you need - and invite them to correct or add to it.
example:
  situation: A team lead notices a direct report delivered a client analysis in an unexpected format, a day before the client review.
  before: "This isn't the template we use. Please redo it in the standard deck format tonight so we're ready for tomorrow."
  after: "You built this differently from our usual deck - I'd like to understand why. Can you walk me through what you were going for and what you know about this client that I might not?"

# Humble inquiry

Humble inquiry is the habit of drawing someone out with questions you really do not know the answer to, driven by curiosity about the person and aimed at building a relationship with them.[^s1] It is defined against telling: opening with what you think the other person needs to know, which Schein argues tends to shut people down.[^s1] The "humble" part is an attitude, not a tone of voice. You accept that you do not have all the answers and that the other person may know something you need.[^s3]

## Origin

The term comes from Edgar H. Schein, an organisational psychologist and emeritus professor at the MIT Sloan School of Management, in his 2013 Berrett-Koehler book *Humble Inquiry: The Gentle Art of Asking Instead of Telling*.[^s1] The book contrasts humble inquiry with other kinds of inquiry and examines the cultural, organisational and psychological barriers that keep people from practising it.[^s1] A revised and expanded second edition, written with Peter A. Schein, appeared in 2021 as part of their Humble Leadership series, which focuses on building trust across hierarchy rather than relying on authority.[^s2]

## Why it matters

At work, the person with more status usually does the telling: the manager, the senior consultant, the expert on the call. Schein's argument is that this habit costs ideas and causes avoidable mistakes, because people who are told stop offering what they know.[^s1] His publisher's manager guide sums up the practice in three moves: tell less, ask more, and listen better while acknowledging what you hear.[^s3] Research on related behaviours points the same way. In a field study of 3,149 restaurant employees and 223 managers, managerial openness predicted improvement-oriented speaking up more consistently than transformational leadership did, and the link ran through employees' sense of psychological safety.[^s5] In laboratory conversations, people who were told to ask more questions were liked more by their partners.[^s4]

## How to apply it

1. **Notice your assumptions first.** Humble inquiry starts from accepting that you may be missing information, so name what you are assuming before you walk in.[^s3]
2. **Open with a real question.** Ask something whose answer could surprise you. When a report does a task in an unexpected way, the manager guide suggests asking them to walk you through their work rather than correcting it.[^s3]
3. **Let them talk.** In Huang and colleagues' studies, extra questions led partners to say more about themselves, and that extra disclosure accounted for much of the increase in liking.[^s4]
4. **Follow their thread.** Build your next question on their answer, which shows interest in them rather than in confirming your view.[^s1][^s3]
5. **Acknowledge, then tell.** Reflect back what you heard before you add your opinion or decision; asking does not mean never telling.[^s3]

The practice changes with the relationship. With direct reports it is mainly a leadership habit for one-on-ones and reviews of work.[^s3] With a senior stakeholder or client, a humble question ("What would make this decision easy for you?") shows interest without seeming to lack a view. In email or chat, one well-chosen open question works better than a list, because text hides the curiosity that tone carries in person. These channel and seniority adaptations extrapolate from Schein's manager-focused guidance rather than from tested advice.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Leading questions.** "Don't you think we should use the template?" is telling in disguise; the other person hears the answer you want.[^s1]
- **Interrogating.** A rapid string of questions feels like an audit; Schein's emphasis is on interest in the person, not on gathering data from them.[^s1]
- **Asking, then ignoring.** Asking for input and then deciding exactly as you would have anyway teaches people that the door is not really open, which is the gap Detert and Burris's title points to.[^s5]
- **Using it only downward or only upward.** The aim is to build trust across hierarchy in both directions, not to play a status game.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

Humble inquiry itself is a practitioner framework: Schein built it from decades of consulting and teaching, and no controlled trial of the technique as a package was found, so its status here is `practitioner`.[^s1] Supporting evidence comes from studies of nearby behaviours. Huang et al. (2017) reported two experiments in which assigned high question-askers were liked more, with small to moderate effects (d = 0.27 to 0.38), plus a correlational speed-dating study; people did not predict this effect, and third-party observers did not show it.[^s4] Detert and Burris (2007) is a single-company field study of managerial openness, not of questioning as such.[^s5] Owens, Johnson and Mitchell (2013), across eight samples, linked leader-expressed humility to employee engagement through team learning orientation, and to retention through job satisfaction; humility there is broader than question-asking.[^s6] Treat these as consistent with Schein's claims, not proof of them.

## Connections

- **Broader:** a form of questioning, alongside the open-ended questions it usually relies on.[^s1]
- **Addresses telling instead of asking**, the default of opening with answers that Schein says shuts people down.[^s1]
- **Introduced by** Edgar Schein in *Humble Inquiry* (2013), expanded with Peter Schein in 2021.[^s1][^s2]
- **Applied in one-on-one meetings** and other manager conversations, the setting the publisher's guide focuses on.[^s3]
- **Complements psychological safety:** leader openness predicted employees speaking up through perceived safety.[^s5]
- **Supported by** Huang et al. 2017 on question-asking and liking,[^s4] Detert and Burris 2007 on openness and voice,[^s5] and Owens et al. 2013 on expressed humility.[^s6]
- **Related:** active listening and follow-up questions carry out the "listen better" part, advice-giving is the reflex it displaces, and managing up applies the same curiosity toward a boss.[^s3]
