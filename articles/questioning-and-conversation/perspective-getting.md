steps:
  - Before a decision that depends on someone else's view, write down your best guess of what they think and how confident you are.
  - Turn each guess into a short, neutral question you can put to them directly, covering every point that matters rather than one or two.
  - Ask, then listen to the whole answer without arguing, and note anything that contradicts your guess.
  - Restate what you heard in your own words and ask whether you have it right.
  - Make an honest answer safe - say why you are asking, show you can hear an unwelcome reply, and never penalise one.
  - Where you cannot ask, get the information another way - a survey, their written priorities, or someone who has been in their position - and treat any remaining guess as a hypothesis.
example:
  situation: A team lead is planning next quarter's on-call rota and assumes the senior engineers want fewer weekend shifts in exchange for more weekday nights.
  before: "Team lead (to their manager) - I've redone the rota so seniors get fewer weekends. I know they'd rather take extra weeknights; they've got families."
  after: "Team lead (to each engineer, in one-on-ones) - I'm redrawing the on-call rota. Which matters more to you, fewer weekends or fewer weeknights, and is there anything about the current set-up I'm not seeing? So, if I've got it right, weekends are fine but back-to-back nights are the real problem?"
  why: The lead swaps a confident guess for a quick question about each point that matters and a check of the answer, and finds that the actual complaint (consecutive nights) is not the one they had planned to fix.
selfCheck:
  - Which of my beliefs about this person's view did I hear from them, and which did I only imagine?
  - Have I asked about every point my decision depends on, or only the one I was least sure of?
  - Did I restate their answer and let them correct it?
  - Would they feel safe telling me something I don't want to hear?
  - Am I skipping the question because I feel sure - and is that certainty based on anything they told me?

# Perspective-getting

Perspective-getting means learning another person's view from the person themselves: you ask what they think, want or feel, and you listen to the answer.[^s1] It is defined against perspective-taking, which works top-down by imagining yourself in their position; perspective-getting works bottom-up by collecting new information you did not have.[^s1] At work it is the difference between a manager deciding what the team "obviously" wants and a manager who asks each person and checks the answer.[^s3]

## Origin

The University of Chicago psychologist Nicholas Epley set out the contrast in *Mindwise* (2014), arguing that imagining someone's position often just recycles your own assumptions and stereotypes, whereas asking and listening is how understanding is usually gained.[^s3] With Tal Eyal and Mary Steffel he then tested it in a 2018 package of 25 experiments in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, whose title gave the idea its slogan: accurate understanding requires getting perspective, not taking it.[^s1] Tilburg University researchers took the work further in 2021 by studying why people so rarely ask even when they can.[^s2]

## Why it matters

In 24 experiments, instructions to take another's perspective made people work harder and less self-centred, but did not make them more accurate and sometimes made them less so.[^s1] The final experiment compared the two directly. Predictors in 104 couples guessed their partner's opinions on 20 statements; those who spent about five minutes asking their partner about all 20 were far more accurate than controls, while those who imagined a day in their partner's life did worse.[^s1] Gains appeared only on the items actually discussed, so asking about one issue does not tell you about the next.[^s1] Confidence did not move with accuracy: in every condition people believed they had about 14 of 20 right and averaged about six.[^s1] That overconfidence is why perspective-getting is a deliberate technique and not something people do by default.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Start by noticing that you are guessing; overconfidence is what leads people to skip the question.[^s3] Ask about each point your decision depends on, because in the Tilburg studies those who asked about more items were more accurate, yet only a minority asked about everything.[^s2] Listen for hints when people do not say outright what they want, and confirm you heard correctly rather than assuming you understood.[^s3] Then make candour possible: Epley's workplace examples - an organisation that kept junior officers only after asking why they were leaving, a university that learned about faculty well-being only by surveying them - turn on people feeling safe to give an answer the asker would not like.[^s3] Asking also has a social upside: in conversation studies, people who asked more questions were liked more by their partners, though neither askers nor observers predicted it.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Asking once and generalising.** Accuracy improved only on the topics discussed; a single question is not a reading of the whole person.[^s1]
- **Not asking because it feels awkward or obvious.** Participants skipped questions because asking felt too simple, unnecessary or less interesting than working it out themselves.[^s2]
- **Treating a close relationship as a reason not to ask.** Couples who had been together for around ten years on average still misjudged each other's opinions until they asked.[^s1]
- **Asking a question that cannot be answered honestly.** People may not tell the truth, or may not know their own minds, so a reply given under pressure or with fear of retaliation is weak evidence.[^s1][^s3]
- **Dropping perspective-taking entirely.** Imagining another's view still helps you notice that they see things differently and may build connection, which is a reason to ask, not a substitute for asking.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence

The status is `mixed`. The core finding is strong but narrow: one large controlled experiment with romantic partners, inside a paper whose other 24 experiments tested perspective-taking, not asking.[^s1] Damen and colleagues found that asking predicted accuracy in two of three studies, but their first study, with a small sample, showed no significant gain, and a short intervention urging people to ask did not increase how much they asked.[^s2] Workplace evidence is so far mostly illustrative cases rather than controlled trials.[^s3] The weight of evidence supports asking as more accurate than imagining, with the caveat that its benefit depends on asking widely and on honest answers.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a form of questioning aimed at understanding another person's mind.[^s1]
- **Addresses** egocentric bias: asking supplies information that replaces your own view as the default.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Eyal, Steffel and Epley (2018), whose final experiment showed asking improved accuracy where imagining did not.[^s1]
- **Introduced by** Nicholas Epley, who set out the distinction in *Mindwise*.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with** perspective-taking, the top-down strategy of imagining the other person's position.[^s1]
- **Supported by** Damen, Pollmann and Grassow (2021), who linked more asking to more accurate predictions and documented why people ask too little.[^s2]
- **Applied in** one-on-one meetings and staff surveys, where managers learn why people stay, leave or struggle.[^s3]
- **Related:** open-ended questions and humble inquiry supply the question forms; checking understanding closes the loop; psychological safety makes honest answers possible; Huang and colleagues' 2017 question-asking studies show asking also builds liking.[^s3][^s4]
