# Questioning

Questioning is the skill of deciding what to ask, how to word it, in what order and with what follow-up, so that a question gets an accurate, useful answer and leaves the relationship stronger rather than weaker. Communication-skills researchers treat it as one of the most used conversational skills and one of the most deceptively complex: the grammar of a question (open, closed, leading) does not fix its function, and a single question often does several jobs at once - getting information, showing interest, steering the conversation, or asserting status.[^s3] Individual techniques such as open-ended questions, follow-up questions, wait time and humble inquiry are the tools; questioning is the underlying skill of choosing among them.[^s3][^s2]

## Origin

Questioning has long been studied in professional settings where answers matter - classroom teaching and medical consultation in particular - and the 2025 edition of Owen Hargie's *Handbook of Communication Skills* gives it a dedicated chapter by Karyn Stapleton, which analyses questions both as information-handling tools and as tools for managing roles, relationships, power and the flow of talk.[^s3] Its workplace profile rose after Alison Wood Brooks and Leslie John of Harvard Business School argued in *Harvard Business Review* (2018) that executives spend much of the day asking questions yet, unlike litigators, journalists and doctors, are rarely trained to do it.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Brooks and John argue that good questioning helps organisations learn, exchange ideas, innovate and build trust, and that it lowers risk by surfacing hazards nobody had flagged.[^s2] The relational payoff has experimental support: in a 2017 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* paper (a pilot and four studies), Huang and colleagues found that people who were assigned to ask more questions in text chats were liked more by their partners, apparently because questions invited the partner to talk about themselves, and that participants did not predict this benefit.[^s1] Wording also changes what you learn: across four studies, Minson and colleagues (2018) found that questions presuming a problem ("What problems does it have?") drew out more honest disclosure of negative information, including in face-to-face job-recruitment meetings, than general questions or questions presuming everything was fine.[^s5]

## What good looks like

Brooks and John's research-based summary names five habits: favour follow-up questions, know when to keep questions open-ended, get the sequence right, choose the right tone, and pay attention to group dynamics.[^s2] Skilled questioners also match the wording to the goal. To explore, they use open questions; to uncover a problem the other person may be reluctant to mention, they phrase the question as if a problem is normal - for example asking a vendor "What issues have other clients run into after go-live?" rather than "Everything went smoothly, right?"[^s5] They remember that a question can carry power: in Stapleton's account, questions also allocate turns and assert or concede control, so the same question lands differently from a director than from a peer.[^s3] Minson's work adds a caution on tone: very pointed questions about uncommon misbehaviour made the asker look worse to others, so assertive phrasing should be kept for issues that are genuinely likely.[^s5]

## How to practise it

Start by preparing your questions, not just your talking points, and tag each one as explore (open), confirm (closed) or surface a risk (problem-presuming).[^s2][^s5] In the conversation, aim for more questions than you would naturally ask, since participants in the 2017 studies underestimated the benefit, and make most of them build on the last answer.[^s1][^s2] Afterwards, review a recording or notes and count your questions, how many were follow-ups, and how many were really statements in question form.[^s2][^s3] In a status meeting, for example, replacing "Are we on track?" with "What's most likely to slip, and what would you need to prevent it?" invites the honest answer the closed version discourages.[^s5]

## Evidence

The evidence is supportive but not settled, so the status here is *mixed*. Huang et al.'s laboratory studies support a causal effect of question-asking on liking, but the authors note that their speed-dating evidence is correlational, that chats were text-based, that question type was not systematically varied, and that third-party observers did not prefer the question-askers.[^s1] Kluger and Malloy (2019) re-analysed the speed-dating data with the social relations model and found that question-asking reflects the asker, the partner and the specific pairing; they found no clear distinction between follow-up and switch questions, and for men, asking more questions was linked to fewer second-date offers - so they cautioned that asking questions is not the same as listening.[^s4] Minson et al.'s findings on phrasing come from several studies in one paper and still need independent replication across cultures and settings.[^s5]

## Connections

Questioning is a skill within **interpersonal communication** (`cm:interpersonal-communication`), whose research tradition analyses the forms and functions of questions.[^s3] Its value for building relationships is **demonstrated by Huang et al. 2017** (`cm:huang-et-al-2017`)[^s1] and **challenged by Kluger and Malloy 2019** (`cm:kluger-malloy-2019`), who dispute how the speed-dating data were analysed.[^s4] The claim that phrasing shapes what people disclose is **supported by Minson et al. 2018** (`cm:minson-et-al-2018`), whose second study took place in job recruitment meetings, so questioning is **applied in job interviews** (`cm:job-interview`).[^s5] It is also **applied in negotiation** (`cm:negotiation`), one of the tense, information-poor settings Brooks and John single out.[^s2] Its main techniques are **open-ended questions**, **closed questions**, **follow-up questions**, **wait time**, **humble inquiry**, **perspective-getting** and the coaching questions of the **GROW model**, and it works hand in hand with **active listening**, since the answer only helps if it is heard.[^s2][^s3][^s4]
