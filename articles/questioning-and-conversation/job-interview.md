# Job interview

A job interview is the conversation in which an employer questions an applicant to decide whether to hire them. It is one of the most widely used selection tools, and it is a communication problem on both sides of the table: the interviewer has to ask questions that surface job-relevant evidence, and the candidate has to give answers that make that evidence easy to see.[^s1] Research separates interviews by how much **structure** they have. A low-structure interview lets the interviewer ask whatever comes to mind and form an overall impression; a high-structure interview asks every candidate the same job-based questions and scores answers against defined benchmarks.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The interview has long been the default way to hire, but most of the modern guidance comes from industrial-organizational psychology. Over roughly two decades of research, Levashina, Hartwell, Morgeson and Campion (2014) organised what was known about structured interviews into eight themes: how to define structure, reducing bias, applicant impression management, measuring personality, question types, rating scales, probing and applicant reactions.[^s2] Public employers adopted the same model; the U.S. Office of Personnel Management (OPM) tells federal agencies to build questions from a job analysis and from critical incidents of notably good or poor performance.[^s1]

## Why it matters

How the conversation is run changes how well it predicts the job. In a 2022 re-analysis of selection research, Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens found that structured interviews had the highest average operational validity of the predictors they examined (about r = .42), although results varied widely from study to study.[^s5] OPM likewise reports that more structure brings higher validity, better agreement between raters and less adverse impact, yet both interviewers and applicants tend to prefer looser, chattier formats.[^s1] For candidates, the stakes are personal: interviewers' ratings respond to how people present themselves, not only to what they have done.[^s3]

## What works here

**For the interviewer.** Derive the critical competencies from a job analysis, then write the same lead questions and follow-up probes for every applicant.[^s1] Use two kinds of questions: *behavioural* questions ask about a real past situation ("Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news to a client"), and *situational* questions pose a job-like scenario ("A key stakeholder misses a deadline that blocks your launch. What do you do?"). OPM notes that both work, with behavioural questions showing higher validity for complex professional and managerial roles.[^s1] Score each answer against written benchmarks, have panel members rate independently, and only then discuss large gaps.[^s1] Probing is part of the design, not improvisation: decide in advance which follow-ups you will use so each candidate gets the same chance to elaborate.[^s2]

**For the candidate.** Because structured interviews are built on job competencies, read the posting for the competencies it implies and prepare one concrete story for each: the situation, what *you* did, and the result.[^s1] Answer the question that was asked, then stop and let the interviewer probe. Interview anxiety tends to show up less in obvious nervous gestures than in how assertive and warm a candidate seems and in their speech rate, and those impressions carry through to interview ratings; a calm, steady pace and a clear statement of your own contribution help counter that.[^s4]

**Example.** *Before:* asked "How do you handle conflicting priorities?", a candidate says "I'm very organised and I always stay calm." *After:* "Last quarter two directors both wanted my analyst time in the same week. I listed both requests with their deadlines, asked my manager to rank them, and told the second director the new date that day. Both projects shipped, the second three days later than first asked." The second answer gives the interviewer scorable evidence instead of a self-description.[^s1]

## Pitfalls

- **Relying on gut feel.** Unstructured conversations feel more natural but rate candidates less consistently and predict performance less well.[^s1][^s5]
- **Mistaking polish for competence.** Peck and Levashina's 2017 meta-analysis found that self-promotion and ingratiation both raise interview ratings, with self-focused tactics mattering more in interviews than in later performance ratings.[^s3] Interviewers should anchor on evidence; candidates should promote real accomplishments, never invented ones, since fabrication is deception and collapses under probing.[^s2][^s3]
- **Asking different candidates different questions.** It undermines fair comparison and lets bias creep in.[^s1][^s2]
- **Ignoring anxiety.** Nervous candidates can come across as less assertive and warm than they are, which depresses ratings.[^s4]

## Evidence

The core finding that structure improves prediction is well supported across meta-analyses, but its size varies across settings and depends on correction methods.[^s5] Impression management complicates the picture: Barrick, Shaffer and DeGrassi's 2009 meta-analysis, as summarised by Peck and Levashina, found that self-presentation tactics related more strongly to interview ratings (r = .47) than to later job-performance ratings (r = .15).[^s3] Evidence on anxiety comes mainly from mock interviews, so its effect on real hiring is less certain.[^s4] Overall the status is **mixed**: robust for structure, more conditional for candidate-side tactics.

## Connections

A job interview is a recurring context within **personnel selection** (`cm:personnel-selection`) and is studied mainly in **industrial-organizational psychology** (`cm:industrial-organizational-psychology`), the field behind the structured-interview research reviewed by Levashina and colleagues.[^s2] Its predictive value is demonstrated by **Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens (2022)** (`cm:sackett-zhang-berry-lievens-2022`),[^s5] and the pull of self-presentation on ratings by **Barrick, Shaffer and DeGrassi (2009)** (`cm:barrick-shaffer-degrassi-2009`).[^s3] Candidates' self-presentation is a form of **impression management** (`cm:impression-management`),[^s3] and nerves overlap with **speaking anxiety** (`cm:speaking-anxiety`).[^s4] Interviewers draw on **open-ended** and **follow-up questions** (`cm:open-ended-questions`, `cm:follow-up-questions`) to probe answers.[^s2] An offer often leads straight into **salary negotiation** (`cm:salary-negotiation`).
