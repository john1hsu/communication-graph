  - "Goal: ask what the person wants from this conversation and what success would look like, and agree a goal they own (\"What would good look like by the end of the quarter?\")."

  - "Options: ask for several possible routes before judging any (\"What else could you try?\"), and offer your own idea only after theirs, labelled as one option."
  - "Will / Way forward: ask which option they will start with, what the first step is, by when, and how you will both know it is working."
  - Summarise the commitment in their words and agree when you will review it, then open the next conversation by checking progress against the goal.

example:
  situation: A team lead's direct report says in a one-on-one that she is drowning in ad-hoc requests from another department and keeps missing her own project milestones.
  before: "OK, just tell them no. Send me the requests and I'll talk to their manager - and block out Friday mornings for the project."
  after: "What would a good week look like for you a month from now? ... Roughly how many requests came in last week, and which ones did you take on? ... What could you do about the ones that aren't urgent? Anything else? I'd add one option - routing them through a shared intake form. ... Which will you try first, and shall we look at how it went next Tuesday?"
  why: The first reply solves the problem for her, so she learns nothing and the lead now owns it; the GROW version gets her to set the target, size the problem, pick an option and commit to a review date, while still letting the lead contribute an idea as one option among several.

# GROW model

The GROW model is a four-stage sequence for a coaching conversation: agree the **Goal**, examine the current **Reality**, generate **Options**, and settle the **Will** or **Way forward** - the specific first step and when it will be reviewed.[^s5] Its defining feature is that the person leading the conversation works mainly through questions, so the other person sets the target, diagnoses the situation and chooses the action rather than being told what to do.[^s5] For managers it offers a simple shape for development and goal-setting conversations that would otherwise drift into advice-giving.[^s5]

## Origin

Coach Graham Alexander developed GROW - in his version, Goal, Reality, Options, Wrap-up - from his own practice; he had not set out to build a model until he and Ben Renshaw analysed recordings of his coaching sessions from the 1980s and 1990s and found a consistent structure, later described in their 2005 book *Supercoaching*.[^s1] Sir John Whitmore popularised the model in *Coaching for Performance* and insisted it be used to build the coachee's awareness and responsibility at every stage, arguing it had little value without them.[^s1] Many later versions replace the last letter with "Way forward", and organisations such as UCL teach it to managers for goal-setting and review conversations.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Managers default to solving problems for their people, which is quick but leaves the manager owning the problem and the employee no more capable. GROW turns a one-on-one or development review into a conversation in which the person states what success looks like, surfaces what helps and hinders them, and commits to small steps that are revisited at each meeting.[^s5] Coaching in general has measurable payoffs: a 2014 meta-analysis of organisational coaching studies found positive effects on every outcome examined, from performance and well-being (Hedges' g = 0.43 for coping) to goal-directed self-regulation (g = 0.74).[^s3] GROW is one of the commonest ways that coaching is structured, and a model gives inexperienced coaches scaffolding; without one, coaches tend to dwell on problems instead of solutions.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Start with the goal, not the problem, and get the person to describe success in their own words and with a time frame.[^s5] In Reality, ask what is happening now and what helps or gets in the way, but keep it short. In a 2015 randomised replication with 204 university students, answering solution-focused questions (what would be better, what is already working) raised self-efficacy, goal approach and planned action steps more than problem-focused questions about causes and history, repeating an earlier finding by coaching psychologist Anthony Grant.[^s4] In Options, ask for more than one route ("Anything else?") before evaluating any.[^s5] Close with Will: which option first, what step, by when, and how progress will be judged - then use the next meeting to review it.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

The commonest error is treating the letters as a script. Critics argue that presenting GROW as a strictly linear sequence makes coaches attend to the model rather than the person, and experienced coaches interviewed by Glenn Wallis described rigid frameworks as an obstacle to asking good questions.[^s1] Cox and Wallis add that many versions drop Whitmore's emphasis on awareness and responsibility, leaving an empty checklist.[^s1] Other pitfalls are the manager setting the goal, spending the whole session on Reality, and ending without a dated commitment or review.[^s5][^s4] Anthony Grant argues that coach training has paid little attention to how session structures are taught and proposed RE-GROW, a variant that ties session structure explicitly to self-regulation theory.[^s6]

## Evidence

GROW itself is better described as practitioner consensus than as a tested intervention. It grew out of one coach's practice rather than a study, and its spread rests on use and training rather than trials.[^s1] A 2025 scoping review of 20 studies of GROW in schools (2015-2022), most of them classroom action research from Indonesia, found it used mainly to develop teachers' teaching skills and principals' supervisory skills; only about 10% of the studies were experimental, and the review did not appraise study quality.[^s2] The broader coaching meta-analysis cannot isolate GROW's contribution, because it pooled coaching of many kinds.[^s3] What the evidence does support is the questioning stance at its core: solution-focused questions outperform problem-focused ones in controlled comparisons.[^s4]

## Connections

GROW was developed by Graham Alexander and popularised by John Whitmore through *Coaching for Performance*.[^s1] It is applied in performance and development reviews and complements open-ended questions, since every stage depends on facilitative questions rather than instructions.[^s5] It sits under questioning and coaching; it is studied in coaching psychology, where Grant's RE-GROW variant links session structure to self-regulation theory.[^s6] It relates to humble inquiry and wait time as habits that keep the coach asking rather than telling, to one-on-one meetings as its usual setting, and to feedforward, Situation-Behavior-Impact and Hattie and Timperley's model, which all supply a forward-looking "where next" step.[^s5]
