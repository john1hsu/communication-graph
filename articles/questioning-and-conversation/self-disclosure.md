# Self-disclosure

Self-disclosure is telling someone something about yourself that they do not already know - a hobby, an opinion, a worry, a past failure.[^s4] It usually unfolds in layers: people share facts first, then opinions, then feelings, and the range and depth of what they share grow as trust builds.[^s4] At work it is the raw material of rapport, but it is also a judgment call: the same story can make a manager seem authentic in one setting and careless in another.[^s5][^s6]

## Origin

The modern study of disclosure in relationships is anchored in social penetration theory, which Irwin Altman and Dalmas Taylor proposed in the early 1970s to explain why some relationships move quickly toward closeness while others develop slowly.[^s3] Communication textbooks describe its central picture: relationships deepen as disclosure widens across topics (breadth) and reaches more personal layers (depth).[^s4] The Johari window, developed by Joseph Luft and Harry Ingham, gives a companion map in which disclosure moves information from the hidden "façade" area into the shared "arena".[^s4] In 1994 Nancy Collins and Lynn Carol Miller pulled the empirical work together in a *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis of disclosure and liking.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Collins and Miller found three linked effects: people who disclose more intimately tend to be liked more, people disclose more to those they like, and people come to like those who have disclosed to them - with the strength of each effect depending on study design, the kind of disclosure and the discloser's gender.[^s1] The urge to share is strong. Across five studies, Diana Tamir and Jason Mitchell (2012) estimated that about 30-40% of speech is about one's own experience, found that disclosing activated the brain's mesolimbic reward regions, and showed that participants would give up money for the chance to talk about themselves.[^s2] That pull is why unchecked disclosure can crowd out listening, and why well-chosen disclosure is such an efficient way to signal openness.[^s2][^s1]

For leaders, a 2022 package of twelve studies (eleven experiments plus a field study of 35,073 people) by Li Jiang, Leslie John and colleagues found that managers who voluntarily revealed a work-related weakness were judged more authentic, because the disclosure made them seem less strategic about self-presentation.[^s5] In an in-person trust game, employees sent more money to managers who had disclosed a weakness, a benefit the managers themselves failed to predict; in a pilot, only about a third of managers chose to mention a weakness when introducing themselves.[^s5]

## Using it well at work

Lisa Rosh and Lynn Offermann's guidance for leaders turns the research into a sequence of judgments:[^s6]

- **Know yourself first.** Disclosures from people with an inaccurate self-image ring hollow; 360-degree feedback and reflection reduce that risk.[^s6]
- **Tie it to the task.** Share when it helps the work, builds trust or eases collaboration, not to promote yourself; shared interests are a lower-risk way to build early rapport.[^s6]
- **Keep it true.** Exaggerated or invented stories are easily discovered and damage credibility.[^s6]
- **Read the context.** Norms differ by culture and organisation; individualistic cultures tend to disclose more than collectivist ones, so watch how much colleagues share.[^s6]
- **Hold the most personal material back.** Intimate disclosures strengthen relationships that already exist but do not create them.[^s6][^s4]

Disclosure also works best in both directions: when one person shares far more than the other, the imbalance can leave them exposed and the relationship lopsided.[^s4]

## Pitfalls

Rosh and Offermann describe characteristic failures: the "open book" who overshares (including other people's confidential information) and ends up sought out but not trusted; the "inscrutable" leader who shares so little that people find them remote; the "bumbler" who misreads cues across cultures; and the "social engineer" who pushes disclosure exercises on others without modelling it.[^s6] Disclosure is also only valued when it is voluntary: in Jiang and colleagues' studies, the benefit disappeared when the disclosure was required.[^s5]

## Evidence

The disclosure-liking link rests on a meta-analysis, but its size varies with design, content and gender, so it is a tendency rather than a rule.[^s1] The reward findings come from laboratory and neuroimaging studies with modest samples.[^s2] Workplace evidence is moderated by status: Jiang and colleagues found the authenticity benefit for high-status disclosers, while low-status disclosers gained no significant advantage, and their field data were correlational.[^s5] Practitioner guidance on timing and culture is largely drawn from case observation rather than controlled tests.[^s6] Hence *mixed*: real effects, strongly dependent on who discloses what, to whom and when.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

Self-disclosure is a core topic of **interpersonal communication**.[^s4] It is **supported by** Collins and Miller's 1994 meta-analysis (`cm:collins-miller-1994`) of disclosure and liking,[^s1] **demonstrated by** Tamir and Mitchell's 2012 reward studies (`cm:tamir-mitchell-2012`),[^s2] and **explained by** Altman and Taylor's **social penetration theory** (`cm:social-penetration-theory`).[^s3] Its workplace value is **supported by** Jiang et al. 2022 (`cm:jiang-et-al-2022`), which also shows why it **contrasts with impression management**: disclosure helps precisely because it reads as less strategic self-presentation.[^s5] Its turn-by-turn practice is **self-disclosure reciprocity** (`cm:reciprocal-self-disclosure`), and it relates to **rapport**, **small talk**, the **trusted advisor** model, **psychological safety**, the **Johari window** and **conversational narcissism**, its self-focused failure mode.[^s4][^s2][^s6]
