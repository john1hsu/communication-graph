steps:
  - Before replying to someone you disagree with, restate their main point in a sentence of your own and check it is one they would accept.
  - Open by acknowledging that view explicitly ("So your concern is...", "I understand that you see...").
  - Name at least one real point of agreement or shared goal before you give your own position.
  - Hedge your claims where you are genuinely less than certain ("I think", "it's possible that", "in my experience").
  - Replace flat negations and "no, because" rebuttals with positive, forward-looking phrasing about what you would do instead.
  - Reread the message and cut any line that only proves the other person wrong without moving the work forward.
example:
  situation: In a project channel, an operations lead argues that a new release should be delayed two weeks for more testing; the product manager thinks the release should ship on schedule.
  before: "No, delaying makes no sense. The reason is that the testing we've done is enough, and two weeks costs us the launch window. We can't keep moving dates."
  after: "So your worry is that we haven't tested the failover path enough to be safe in production - I understand that, and I agree we can't ship something that pages on-call every night. I think it's possible we can cover that risk without losing the launch window: what if we run the failover test this week and hold the go/no-go on Friday based on what it shows?"

# Conversational receptiveness

Conversational receptiveness is the use of language to show that you are willing to engage thoughtfully with a view that opposes your own.[^s1] It is not agreement and not surrender: you can keep your position entirely while signalling that you have heard, considered and weighed the other side.[^s4][^s5] The practical version is a short recipe, usually taught as HEAR - Hedge your claims, Emphasise agreement, Acknowledge the other perspective, and Reframe to the positive.[^s4]

## Origin

The construct comes from a research programme led by Julia Minson at the Harvard Kennedy School with Michael Yeomans, Hanne Collins, Frances Chen and Francesca Gino.[^s1] Minson and Chen frame the broader trait, receptiveness to opposing views, as a willingness to seek out and weigh supporting and opposing views in a relatively impartial way, and argue it is best studied as an interpersonal quality that shifts during a conversation and depends heavily on how the other party behaves.[^s3] The 2020 paper turned that idea into something observable: the team trained a machine-learning model on real disagreements to find which words partners actually perceived as receptive, then distilled those features into the teachable recipe.[^s1] Minson popularised the HEAR acronym in talks and later in her 2026 book *How to Disagree Better*.[^s5][^s4]

## Why it matters

Workplaces run on disagreement - about priorities, budgets, designs and risk - and people judge colleagues by how they disagree. In the 2020 studies, government executives whose language in contentious policy discussions was rated more receptive (by partners and by the algorithm, though not by themselves) were seen as better teammates, advisors and representatives of their organisation.[^s1] In an archive of online editing disputes, contributors whose first message was more receptive drew fewer personal attacks from those who disagreed, suggesting receptiveness early on helps stop conflict escalating.[^s1] Notably, people's own sense of how receptive they sound did not match how they came across, which is why the behaviour is worth deliberate practice.[^s1]

## How to apply it

The language analysis found that partners reacted badly to negations and to argumentative reasoning phrases such as "because" and "the reason is", and reacted well to acknowledgement, hedges, agreement and positive emotion.[^s5] HEAR turns those findings into four moves.[^s4]

- **Hedge.** Qualify claims where you are not certain ("I think it's possible that..."), which signals that the issue is complex and leaves room for the other view.[^s4]
- **Emphasise agreement.** Point to shared goals or points you accept, which lowers defensiveness; agreeing on part of the issue does not mean conceding the whole.[^s4][^s5]
- **Acknowledge.** Restate what the other person said so they feel heard before you respond.[^s4]
- **Reframe to the positive.** Swap "no" and "can't" for constructive phrasing about what you propose instead.[^s4]

In the original intervention, simply giving writers a brief description of these strategies made their messages read as more receptive, and readers rated them as more desirable collaborators and their messages as more persuasive.[^s1] The 2024 follow-up also found the behaviour is contagious: when one side was trained, the untrained person they were disagreeing with became more receptive in reply.[^s2] Minson describes this as a feedback loop that improves engagement, though it does not guarantee agreement.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is treating HEAR as a script. The 2024 authors warn that receptive language could backfire in spoken conversation if it seems insincere, so an acknowledgement you do not mean is worse than none.[^s2] Over-hedging is another: the move is to signal genuine uncertainty, not to dissolve your position into vagueness.[^s4] People also confuse receptiveness with caving; the researchers stress that finding common ground is not giving in to the other view.[^s5] Finally, receptiveness is hardest exactly when it is most needed - when people are angry or tired - so it helps to draft difficult replies before sending rather than improvising them.[^s5]

## Evidence

The evidence is rated mixed: the findings are consistent and include pre-registered experiments, but they come mainly from one research group and the effects are modest. The 2020 paper reports five studies, including field data from executives and online editors and a randomised intervention.[^s1] The 2024 paper, with a combined sample of 17,184, adds pre-registered experiments and a replication showing receptiveness spreads between opponents and improves how people view one another.[^s2] Its authors note that training was brief and effects were mostly about a quarter to half a standard deviation; effects on attitudes toward the opposing group were about a fifth the size of the effects on how people rated each other, and only marginal in the replication on its own.[^s2] All studies used text-based, asynchronous exchanges with American participants and self-reported evaluations, so results may differ in live conversation, in other languages and cultures, and on behavioural outcomes; the work also did not test whether anyone's actual views on the issue moved.[^s2]

## Connections

Conversational receptiveness sits under **conflict resolution** and is most useful in a **difficult conversation**, such as a contentious policy discussion.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by** Yeomans et al. 2020 (`cm:yeomans-et-al-2020`)[^s1] and **supported by** the 2024 transmission studies (`cm:yeomans-et-al-2024`),[^s2] and the HEAR recipe was **introduced by** Julia Minson (`cm:julia-minson`).[^s5] By emphasising agreement it **addresses defensiveness**,[^s4] and its acknowledgement step **complements paraphrasing**, since both restate the other person's view before responding.[^s4] It is well tested in **asynchronous communication**, where all the studies took place.[^s2] It relates to **perspective-getting** and **humble inquiry**, which also treat the other person's view as information to understand, to **active listening**, a prerequisite for acknowledging accurately, to **I-statements**, another wording-level tool for conflict, and to **intellectual humility**, the disposition hedging expresses.[^s3][^s4]
