# Closed questions

A closed question restricts the range of possible answers: "yes" or "no", a pick from the options the asker offers, or one specific fact such as a date or a number. Communication advice often treats them as the poor cousin of open-ended questions, because they can steer people and shut down explanation.[^s4] Used deliberately, though, they are the right tool for confirming details, getting a commitment and cutting through evasion - and research on question wording shows that small choices inside a closed question can change what people tell you.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The open-versus-closed distinction was sharpened by survey researchers, who compared the two formats experimentally. Pew Research Center describes the choice between them as one of the most consequential in questionnaire design: after the 2008 US election, 58% of respondents picked "the economy" as their deciding issue when it appeared on a list, but only 35% named it when asked openly, and 43% of open answers fell outside the five listed options.[^s3] Brooks and John, writing in *Harvard Business Review* in 2018, carried the lesson into management, noting that offered options can import the asker's assumptions while also arguing that closed questions suit tense, guarded exchanges such as negotiations.[^s4] Conversation analysts and clinicians have studied the same question form in medical interviews, where a single yes/no question often decides whether a patient raises another concern.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Closed questions do two jobs that open questions do poorly. They pin things down: an open "How's the timeline looking?" can be answered with reassurance, while a closed "Will the build be ready by the 14th?" requires a position.[^s4] And with careful wording they can surface information people would otherwise hold back. In Minson and colleagues' 2018 experiments, questions that presumed a problem drew far more honest disclosure than general questions: when sellers of a used music player that had frozen twice were asked a problem-presuming question, 89% disclosed the defect, against 8% for a general "What can you tell me about it?"[^s2] The cost of getting closed questions wrong is equally concrete: a list of options or a leading "right?" can produce a tidy answer that is not the true one.[^s3][^s2]

## How to apply it

Sequence your questions. Use open questions to explore and to find out what matters, then move to closed questions to confirm specifics, test your understanding and close on decisions; the steps above give the order.[^s4] In a one-on-one or a status review, that might mean "What's worrying you about the release?" followed by "So the blocker is the security review - is that the only one?"[^s4]

Choose your words with care. In a 2007 study of 20 family physicians and 224 patient visits, doctors were randomly assigned to end the agenda-setting phase with one of two yes/no questions. "Is there something else you want to address?" eliminated about 78% of patients' unmet concerns, while "Is there anything else...?" - the version taught in many textbooks - did not differ significantly from no question at all; neither version lengthened visits.[^s1] The authors attribute the gap to "any" leaning toward a "no" answer.[^s1] The workplace equivalent is ending a meeting with "Is there something we haven't covered?" rather than "Any other business?"

When you suspect a problem, a question that presumes it ("How many complaints have you had since the outage?") tends to draw a more truthful answer than one that presumes all is well ("No complaints, right?") or one that mentions nothing.[^s2] When you offer choices, remember that the list frames the answer: Pew found that respondents chose very differently when one option was renamed, and that agree/disagree statements attract acquiescence, especially from less informed respondents and when an interviewer is present, so it recommends asking people to choose between alternative statements instead.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Closing too early.** Starting with yes/no questions before you know the terrain limits answers to what you already guessed; offered options can crowd out the reply people would have volunteered.[^s3][^s4]
- **Leading tags.** "It's on track, right?" presumes the comfortable answer and makes the honest one harder to give.[^s2]
- **Incomplete or unbalanced options.** A list that omits the real answer, or a vague "anything else?", produces false precision.[^s3][^s1]
- **Agree/disagree framing.** "Do you agree the plan is realistic?" invites polite assent; ask which of two descriptions fits better instead.[^s3]
- **Interrogation.** A rapid string of closed questions can feel like a cross-examination; Brooks and John note open questions help avoid that feeling.[^s4]
- **Presuming the rare.** Assuming an uncommon problem can make you look less warm and competent; Minson's team found this cost was largest for rare behaviours.[^s2]

## Evidence

Evidence is rated mixed: the specific findings are experimental and sizeable but come from small samples and particular settings. The "something versus anything" study was a randomized comparison within 20 physicians' practices in two US regions, and its authors call it small; it has not, as far as this article could find, been replicated at scale or tested in workplace meetings.[^s1] Minson and colleagues found the problem-presuming effect across four studies, including face-to-face recruitment role-plays, but disclosure differences vanished for rare behaviours and assertive questions sometimes lowered ratings of the asker.[^s2] Pew's open-versus-closed and option-wording findings come from large national surveys, which differ from live conversation.[^s3] Advice on when to switch from open to closed questions in management is practitioner synthesis rather than tested guidance.[^s4]

## Connections

Closed questions are a technique within **questioning** (`cm:questioning`) and the counterpart of **open-ended questions** (`cm:open-ended-questions`); most good conversations use both in sequence.[^s4] Their wording effects are **supported by Heritage et al. 2007** (`cm:heritage-et-al-2007`), the "something versus anything" trial in primary care,[^s1] and by **Minson et al. 2018** (`cm:minson-et-al-2018`), whose problem-presuming questions raised honest disclosure.[^s2] They are **applied in job interviews** (`cm:job-interview`), the setting of two of Minson's studies,[^s2] and in **negotiation** (`cm:negotiation`), where Brooks and John recommend them with guarded counterparts.[^s4] Badly framed agree/disagree versions **cause acquiescence bias** (`cm:acquiescence-bias`), the tendency to say yes to whatever is asked.[^s3] They pair naturally with **follow-up questions**, **summarizing**, **checking understanding** and **teach-back**, which confirm that a "yes" reflects real understanding.[^s1][^s4]
