steps:
  - Before the meeting, write down what you already know, which facts are disputed and what records could confirm them, and set your goal as learning what happened rather than getting an admission.
  - Open by explaining neutrally who you are, why you are meeting and how notes will be used, and spend a minute establishing rapport.
  - Ask for a full narrative with "tell me", "describe" or "walk me through", then stay quiet; do not interrupt or fill pauses.
  - Funnel down with narrower follow-ups on times, sequence, people involved and where each step would have left a trace (logs, emails, tickets).
  - Add one or two questions the person is unlikely to have rehearsed, such as the order of events in reverse or what a named colleague would say about their role.
  - Only after the account is complete, raise any conflicting evidence one item at a time and ask the person to explain it.
  - Record the answers, check the verifiable details afterwards, and base your conclusion on what the account and evidence show, not on how nervous the person seemed.

# Information-gathering interview

An information-gathering interview is a way of questioning someone whose account matters - a colleague in a grievance or incident review, a candidate describing past work, a supplier explaining a missed delivery - when you are not sure it is accurate. Instead of confronting the person or watching for "tells", you build rapport, let them give a full account, ask for specific and checkable details, and compare what they say with the evidence.[^s4][^s1] Its opposite is the accusatorial style, which presumes guilt and pushes for an admission.[^s1]

## Origin

The approach comes from research on police and intelligence interviewing. In a 2014 meta-analytic review, Christian Meissner and colleagues compared information-gathering methods (rapport, open questions, seeking the truth) with accusatorial ones (confrontation, assertions of guilt, pressure for a confession).[^s1] In 2016 the U.S. High-Value Detainee Interrogation Group (HIG), a federal interagency unit, published a review of the science that recommended non-coercive, rapport-based, information-gathering methods and drew together their components: narrative elicitation, the cognitive interview and the Strategic Use of Evidence (SUE).[^s4] Workplace investigation guidance points the same way: the UK conciliation service Acas tells investigators to keep questions neutral and non-confrontational and to look for evidence that supports what they are told.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Most people try to judge honesty from demeanour, and the HIG review notes that behavioural differences between liars and truth-tellers are few and weak, with observers averaging about 54% accuracy and police trained on nonverbal cues sometimes doing worse than untrained people.[^s4] An information-gathering interview moves your attention to what can actually be tested: the amount of detail, whether details can be checked, and whether the account fits the evidence.[^s4] It is also fairer. In Meissner's review, accusatorial methods raised false confessions along with true ones, while information-gathering preserved true confessions and reduced false ones.[^s1] At work, the equivalent of a false confession is a nervous, honest employee agreeing to a version of events just to end a hostile meeting.

## How to apply it

Prepare first: list what you know, which facts are disputed and what records could settle them. Acas suggests talking to the person concerned early, so the investigation can focus on the facts in dispute.[^s5] Open with your role and the purpose, and build rapport, which the HIG review calls the most widely recognised ingredient of a productive interview.[^s4] Then ask for a narrative ("walk me through...") without interrupting, and allow silence.[^s4] Funnel from broad to narrow questions, asking for times, names and where each step would leave a record; people telling the truth can usually supply details that others could check, while people inventing a story tend to offer details that cannot be checked.[^s4] A few unanticipated questions, such as recalling the events in reverse order, can expose prepared stories, because rehearsed answers do not cover them.[^s4][^s2] Hold your evidence back until the account is complete, then raise conflicts one at a time; in the Strategic Use of Evidence approach, revealing evidence gradually makes conflicts between the account and the evidence more likely to surface.[^s4] Record the answers and check them afterwards.[^s5]

Ethically, the method is about learning what happened, not tricking anyone: do not bluff about evidence, promise outcomes or frame the meeting as if guilt were already settled, which the HIG review links to false confessions.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Rapid-fire pointed questions.** The HIG review names a string of narrow, accusing questions as a common error that cuts off the narrative.[^s4]
- **Showing your hand first.** Leading with "the log says you never called" invites the person to fit their story to your evidence.[^s4]
- **Treating consistency as truth.** The review warns that consistency across retellings is not a reliable sign of honesty, nor is inconsistency a reliable sign of lying.[^s4]
- **Skipping the check.** An account full of verifiable detail is only useful if someone verifies it.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

Meissner and colleagues' 2014 review pooled 5 field studies and 12 laboratory experiments; information-gathering methods kept or increased true confessions while reducing false ones, though the authors cautioned that the number of independent samples was small.[^s1] For detecting lies, Vrij, Fisher and Blank's 2017 meta-analysis reported that a cognitive approach (adding mental load, encouraging elaboration and asking unexpected questions) reached 71% combined accuracy against 56% for standard approaches.[^s2] Timothy Levine and colleagues re-examined that analysis in 2018 and concluded the advantage was much smaller than claimed, partly because control-group accuracy was unusually low and the strongest results came from the least reliable data.[^s3] Hence `mixed`: the case for asking open questions and checking facts is solid, but no interview method turns a manager into a reliable lie detector, and most of the evidence comes from police and laboratory settings rather than workplaces.[^s3][^s4] Cross-cultural interviews are harder still; the HIG review reports cross-cultural lie judgments near chance.[^s4]

## Connections

The information-gathering interview is a form of **questioning** (`cm:questioning`) and builds on **open-ended questions** (`cm:open-ended-questions`) and **follow-up questions**.[^s4] It **addresses reading lies from body language** (`cm:reading-lies-from-body-language`) by replacing demeanour judgments with verbal content and evidence.[^s4] It is **demonstrated by Meissner et al. 2014** (`cm:meissner-et-al-2014`),[^s1] **supported by Vrij, Fisher and Blank 2017** (`cm:vrij-fisher-blank-2017`) for its lie-detection component,[^s2] and **challenged by Levine, Blair and Carpenter 2018** (`cm:levine-blair-carpenter-2018`), who argue that evidence was overstated.[^s3] It is **applied in workplace investigations** (`cm:workplace-investigation`), where Acas guidance calls for neutral questions and corroborating evidence,[^s5] and it **complements rapport** (`cm:rapport`).[^s4] Related ideas include **active listening**, **cognitive load**, **perception checking** and the **job interview**, where the same questions help test claims about past work.[^s4]
