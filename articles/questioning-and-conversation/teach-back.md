# Teach-back

Teach-back is a way of confirming that an explanation was understood: after explaining something important, you ask the other person to explain it back, or demonstrate it, in their own words.[^s1] The point is to test the explanation, not the listener. You say so openly, and when their version is wrong you explain again and check again.[^s5] It replaces the reflexive "Does that make sense?", which people tend to answer yes, either because they think they understood or because admitting confusion is awkward.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Teach-back comes from clinical health-literacy practice, where it is also called "closing the loop".[^s5] An influential early study by Dean Schillinger and colleagues at UCSF and San Francisco General Hospital audiotaped primary-care visits with diabetes patients who had limited health literacy. It found that physicians checked recall or comprehension in only 20% of visits and for 12% of new concepts.[^s2][^s6] Where physicians did check, patients were much more likely to have good blood-sugar control, and checking did not make visits longer.[^s2][^s6] The U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) now teaches it in its TeamSTEPPS team-communication curriculum, and Bodenheimer notes that major U.S. bodies, including the American Academy of Family Physicians and The Joint Commission, endorse it as a standard of care.[^s1][^s5]

## Why it matters

People who explain things routinely overestimate how much got through. Bodenheimer summarises evidence that about half of patients leave a visit without understanding what they were told, and that physicians often overestimate how well hospital patients understand their discharge plans.[^s5] This is the communicative side of the illusion of transparency: because you know what you meant, your message feels clearer to you than it is.[^s5] Workplaces have the same problem in handovers, delegation, client instructions and onboarding. A "yes, got it" is a weak signal, but a restatement shows the gap while there is still time to fix it.[^s1][^s5]

## How to apply it

1. Choose what to check. Bodenheimer recommends saving teach-back for key points, such as instructions someone must follow, and says a check takes a minute or two.[^s5]
2. Explain in plain, specific language without jargon, one important idea at a time, and check after each one rather than once at the end.[^s5]
3. Make yourself the subject of the check, for example "just to be sure I was clear...", and say plainly that you are not testing them.[^s5]
4. Ask open questions that make the person restate or plan, and encourage their own words rather than an echo of yours. For anything hands-on, ask them to show you.[^s1][^s5]
5. If the explanation back is incomplete, re-explain and ask again until it is accurate, then confirm it in writing where possible.[^s1][^s5]

The sources describe clinical use, so applying it elsewhere at work is an adaptation, not a tested protocol. The core rules still carry over: ask an open question and own the check.[^s5] With a peer or a direct report, "Walk me through what you'll do first" works. With an executive or a client, ask how they would use the information ("How would you put this to your board?"), which checks understanding without sounding like a quiz. In email or chat, ask for a one-line summary of the next step in the reply. Text gives you no cues at all, and AHRQ's point that a "yes" is unreliable applies even more strongly there.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- **Asking a yes/no question.** "Any questions?" and "Do you understand?" produce agreement, not evidence of understanding.[^s1][^s5]
- **Making it a test.** If the check sounds like an exam, people feel patronised. Owning the explanation keeps their dignity intact.[^s5]
- **Accepting a word-for-word echo.** Repeating your words back can hide a misunderstanding, so ask for their own words.[^s5]
- **Checking everything or nothing.** Checking every sentence wastes time, and checking nothing is the usual failure. Doctors in Schillinger's study rarely checked at all.[^s2][^s5]
- **Repeating rather than re-explaining.** If the first explanation failed, saying it again more slowly rarely fixes it. Try a different framing or an example.[^s5]

## Evidence

Most of the evidence comes from healthcare. Schillinger's 2003 study was observational and small (38 physicians, 74 patients), so it shows an association rather than a cause.[^s2] A 2016 systematic review of 12 studies of people with chronic disease found positive effects on disease knowledge, adherence and self-efficacy. Effects on readmissions were inconsistent, evidence for quality of life and knowledge retention was limited, and the studies were too varied to pool in a meta-analysis.[^s3] A 2020 review of 20 studies, rated moderate in quality overall, found teach-back effective in 19 of them across many settings and outcomes, but noted that few studies described how it was actually put into practice.[^s4] The status here is `mixed`: the direction is consistent, but effects vary by outcome, most trials are small, and no controlled workplace studies were found. Using it in non-clinical settings rests on the shared mechanism, not on direct tests.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** one way of checking understanding, as opposed to assuming it.[^s5]
- **Addresses the illusion of transparency:** it replaces the explainer's inflated sense of being understood with direct evidence.[^s5]
- **Applied in patient education**, its home setting, and taught by AHRQ for clinical teams.[^s1]
- **Complements plain language:** clear wording comes first, and teach-back checks whether it worked.[^s5]
- **Demonstrated by** Schillinger and colleagues' 2003 study linking comprehension checks to better patient outcomes.[^s2]
- **Supported by** the 2016 Ha Dinh and 2020 Talevski systematic reviews.[^s3][^s4]
- **Related:** paraphrasing is the listener's mirror image, where the listener restates on their own initiative; teach-back is the explainer asking for it. Open-ended questions are how the request is phrased, and the curse of knowledge is a reason explanations miss.[^s5]
