steps:
  - Write a question focus - a short statement, chart or fact, not a question - that names the issue you need to understand.
  - Set a timer for five to ten minutes and have everyone produce as many questions as they can, writing each one exactly as said, with no discussing, judging or answering, and turning any statement into a question.
  - Number the list and mark each question C (closed, answerable with yes/no or a single word) or O (open).
  - Discuss what each type is good and bad for, then rewrite at least one closed question as open and one open question as closed.
  - Choose the three questions that matter most against a stated criterion (for example, the ones that would most change the decision) and note why you picked them.
  - Agree who will take each priority question where - a stakeholder meeting, a data request, a customer interview - and by when.
  - Close by asking the group what they learned about asking questions and how they will use the process next time.
example:
  situation: A product team's kickoff after a quarterly report shows renewals fell sharply among small-business customers.
  before: "The lead opens with 'Why are renewals down?', the most senior engineer answers 'pricing', and the rest of the hour is spent debating a pricing fix."
  after: "The lead puts up the statement 'Small-business renewals fell this quarter' and asks for ten minutes of questions only. The list of 24 includes 'Did anything change in onboarding?' and 'Which customers renewed, and how are they different?'; the team converts closed questions to open, picks three priorities and assigns each to an owner before anyone proposes a fix."
  why: A statement instead of a question avoids anchoring everyone on one cause, the no-answering rule keeps the senior voice from closing the topic, and prioritising turns a long list into a short research plan.
selfCheck:
  - Did I give the group a statement to question, or did I smuggle my own hypothesis in as the prompt?
  - Did anyone, including me, start answering or judging questions before the production phase ended?
  - Which of our priority questions is open, and which is closed - and did we choose that deliberately?
  - Does each priority question have an owner and a place where it will actually be asked?

# Question Formulation Technique

The Question Formulation Technique (QFT) is a short, rule-bound exercise for getting better at asking questions. Instead of being handed questions, participants start from a prompt called a question focus, produce their own questions under four rules, sort and rewrite them, choose the most important and plan what to do with them.[^s1] Its designers frame it as teaching a skill that is rarely taught directly: knowing what to ask.[^s1]

## Origin

Dan Rothstein and Luz Santana, co-directors of the Right Question Institute in Cambridge, Massachusetts, developed the method from a dropout-prevention project in Lawrence, Massachusetts, around 1990, where parents said they stayed away from schools because they did not know what to ask.[^s1] They described the six-step classroom version in a 2011 *Harvard Education Letter* article and in their book *Make Just One Change* (Harvard Education Press, 2011).[^s1] The same question-formulation core was adapted into patient-activation programmes in community health and mental health clinics.[^s2][^s4]

## Why it matters

Most people ask fewer and narrower questions than a situation needs, and in groups the first plausible answer tends to end inquiry. The QFT separates producing questions from answering them, so a team sees the full range of what it does not know before committing to a view.[^s1] The authors report that a first session takes about 45 minutes and later ones 10 to 15, which makes it practical as a recurring habit.[^s1] Its value outside classrooms rests on the health-care adaptations: in a 2008 *Medical Care* study, mental-health patients who received three short coaching sessions built on the QFT stayed in care at higher rates than patients at a comparison clinic.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Use a statement, not a question, as the prompt, so you do not steer people toward your own hypothesis.[^s1] During production, enforce the four rules: ask as many questions as you can; do not stop to discuss, judge or answer; write each question down exactly as stated; and change any statement into a question.[^s1] Then label each question closed or open, talk through the trade-offs of each type and convert a few from one to the other, which is the step that most directly trains phrasing.[^s1] Prioritise against an explicit criterion, decide the next steps, and end with a reflection so the process transfers to the next meeting or conversation.[^s1] A lighter cousin for leaders is Hal Gregersen's "question burst": a four-minute round in which colleagues fire questions about your challenge while you write them down without answering.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

The commonest failure is letting someone answer during production, which turns the exercise back into an ordinary discussion; the no-answering rule is the one participants find hardest.[^s1][^s5] Another is a prompt so broad or so loaded that it dictates the questions. A third is stopping after the list: without prioritising and assigning next steps, the questions never reach the person who can answer them.[^s1] Finally, expect friction when questions reach their target; in the 2008 clinic study some providers found more active patient questioning hard to handle.[^s2]

## Evidence

The evidence is promising but limited, so the status is *mixed*. The strongest adult study is Alegría and colleagues' 2008 evaluation at two mental-health clinics (231 patients): the intervention group had higher odds of staying in treatment (adjusted odds ratio about 2.8), attended about 29% more of their scheduled visits and showed a small rise in patient activation, but the design was non-randomised, the sites differed, outcomes were self-reported and empowerment did not improve.[^s2] A briefer 10-minute version delivered by medical students in New York community health centres raised patient-activation scores, most for patients who started low, according to the Institute's summary of Deen and colleagues' study.[^s4] In education, a 2024 undergraduate biology study found that students produced more questions by the end of term and valued the process, but it was descriptive, with no control group.[^s3] Much classroom support is teacher testimony, and the 2011 article offers no controlled school data.[^s1] Workplace use, as in the example above, is an untested adaptation.

## Connections

The QFT **develops** the skill of **questioning** (`cm:questioning`), which is its stated purpose.[^s1] It was **introduced by** **Dan Rothstein** (`cm:dan-rothstein`) and **Luz Santana** (`cm:luz-santana`) and set out **in** their book ***Make Just One Change*** (`cm:make-just-one-change`).[^s1] It is **supported by** the clinic evaluation of **Alegría et al. 2008** (`cm:alegria-et-al-2008`) and is **applied in** the **medical appointment** (`cm:medical-appointment`), where patients prepare questions for their clinicians.[^s2] Its sorting step works with **open-ended** and **closed questions**, and its production phase resembles **brainstorming** with questions in place of ideas, as does the shorter **question burst**.[^s1][^s5]
