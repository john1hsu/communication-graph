steps:
  - List the objections your audience is most likely to raise - ask a sceptical colleague or two which ones they would lead with, and what your proposal costs others in time and money.
  - Pick the two or three strongest objections, not the easiest ones to knock down, and state each one fairly in a single sentence.
  - Answer each objection directly with evidence, a mitigation or a changed design - do not just acknowledge it and move on.
  - Concede plainly any point you cannot answer, and say what you would do about it or what would change your recommendation.
  - Place the objections and answers where the reader will be asking them - usually right after your recommendation and main reason, not buried at the end.
  - Afterwards, note which objections actually came up and add any you missed to your list for next time.

  situation: A team lead proposes moving the team's weekly status meeting to a written async update, and expects the department head to worry about losing visibility.

  after: "I propose we replace the Monday status meeting with a written update in our team channel, saving about six person-hours a week. The obvious worry is that you lose visibility into blockers. To cover that, each update has a required 'Blocked on' line, and anything blocked for more than two days triggers a 15-minute call. A second worry is that people won't write updates; we would track completion for a month and go back to the meeting if it drops below 90%."

# Refutational two-sided messages

A refutational two-sided message makes its case, names the main arguments against it, and then answers them. Persuasion researchers distinguish it from a one-sided message, which presents only supporting arguments, and from a nonrefutational two-sided message, which mentions opposing points but leaves them standing and relies on the supporting case to outweigh them.[^s2] The distinction matters because the two kinds of two-sided message have opposite effects on average.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The question of whether to mention the other side goes back to experiments by Carl Hovland and colleagues. Hovland, Lumsdaine and Sheffield (1949) proposed that two-sided messages work better for educated or initially opposed audiences, and one-sided messages for less educated or already favourable ones - a rule textbooks repeated for decades.[^s1] Later meta-analyses by Allen and colleagues did not confirm those audience moderators and pointed instead to whether the opposing arguments were refuted.[^s1] In 1990, Allen and nine co-authors ran three experiments with 17 different messages. Refutational two-sided messages persuaded most, one-sided messages came next, and nonrefutational two-sided messages persuaded least.[^s3] Daniel O'Keefe's 1999 meta-analysis then tested the same split across the whole literature.[^s1]

## Why it matters

At work, the people you most need to persuade already have objections in mind: cost, risk, workload, a past project that failed. Jay Conger's research on effective persuaders found they weigh their position from every angle and ask what it will cost others in time and money. They also test their answers with trusted colleagues before making the case.[^s5] O'Keefe's review shows why answering those objections beats both ignoring them and just nodding at them. Across 107 comparisons and about 20,000 participants, two-sided messages as a group were no more persuasive than one-sided ones. Refutational versions were more persuasive (r = .077, 42 effect sizes), while nonrefutational versions were somewhat less persuasive (r = -.049, 65 effect sizes).[^s1] Both kinds of two-sided message were rated as more credible than one-sided ones, so credibility alone does not explain the persuasion gap.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Start from the audience's objections, not your own argument. Conger recommends one-on-one conversations with the people you need to persuade, held to learn their concerns rather than to pitch. In one of his cases, an engineer learned from a colleague that the company president cared most about short-term profit, and redesigned his plan to cut costs instead of needing new investment.[^s5] Choose the strongest objections, state each one fairly, and answer it with evidence, a safeguard or a design change.[^s2][^s5] Where you cannot answer a point, say so and explain how you would handle it; Conger found audiences want to see that a persuader can adjust to their concerns.[^s5]

This works the same way in an email, a decision memo or a meeting. In writing, put the objection-and-answer pairs straight after your recommendation, where a sceptical reader is already raising them. Live, raise the objection yourself before someone else does, then invite any you missed.[^s5]

**Where the ethical line is.** Refutation earns its effect by engaging the reasons the audience actually holds.[^s2] Inventing weak "objections" no one holds, misstating the other side, or hiding a point you cannot answer crosses into manipulation; Conger stresses that persuasion rests on credibility and on a real willingness to adjust to the audience's concerns.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Acknowledge and move on.** Writing "some may worry about X, but the benefits outweigh it" is the nonrefutational pattern, which on average persuaded slightly less than not mentioning X at all.[^s1][^s2]
- **Knocking down straw men.** Answering easy objections while skipping the hard one leaves the real concern standing and costs you credibility with those who hold it.[^s5]
- **Using it as a one-shot script.** Conger lists treating persuasion as a single presentation, rather than listening, testing and revising, as one of four common mistakes.[^s5]
- **Expecting it to win on its own.** The average advantage is small, and it was weaker and less certain in advertising, where only nine refutational studies existed.[^s1]

## Evidence

The evidence is rated mixed. The direction is consistent across Allen's replications and O'Keefe's meta-analysis, which found that refutational messages also beat one-sided ones on credibility (r = .113).[^s1][^s3] But the effect is small, and most studies were laboratory experiments with written messages rather than workplace decisions.[^s1] O'Keefe and Hoeken's 2021 review of 30 message-design meta-analyses found that most such choices have small average effects with prediction intervals that include harm as well as help. It also listed one- versus two-sided messages overall as essentially zero (k = 107).[^s4] Treat refutation as a reliable default with modest gains, not a guaranteed win with every audience.[^s4]

## Connections

The technique **develops persuasion** (`cm:persuasion`); O'Keefe's overview of persuasion research tells persuaders to refute opposing arguments rather than ignore or merely mention them.[^s2] It is **supported by O'Keefe 1999** (`cm:okeefe-1999`), the meta-analysis that separated refutational from nonrefutational messages,[^s1] and **demonstrated by Allen et al. 1990** (`cm:allen-et-al-1990`), whose three experiments replicated the ranking.[^s3] It is **influenced by Carl Hovland** (`cm:carl-hovland`), whose 1949 studies with Lumsdaine and Sheffield first set one-sided against two-sided messages.[^s1] It **contrasts with inoculation** (`cm:inoculation-theory`), which also refutes counterarguments in advance but aims to make people resist later persuasion, not to change their minds now.[^s2] O'Keefe & Hoeken 2021 (`cm:okeefe-hoeken-2021`) sets its limits,[^s4] while issue selling, role reversal and perspective-taking help you find the objections worth answering.[^s5]
