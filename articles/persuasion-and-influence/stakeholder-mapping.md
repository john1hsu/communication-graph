steps:
  - Write down the decision or change in one sentence and set the boundary - which teams, customers, regulators and time frame are in scope.
  - List everyone who can affect it or will be affected by it, then ask two or three colleagues outside your team who is missing, including people with little voice.
  - For each stakeholder, note their main interest or concern, whether they currently support, oppose or are undecided, and what power they hold (budget, sign-off, expertise, reputation, ability to block).
  - Place each one on a power-versus-interest grid and pick an engagement level - partner closely, keep satisfied, consult, or keep informed.
  - For each group, decide who will talk to them, the one message that speaks to their interest, the channel and how often - then put that in a communication plan.
  - Test the map by talking to the stakeholders themselves, and revisit it at each milestone, because power, interest and stance shift.
example:
  situation: An operations manager is replacing the team's expense-claims tool and has a go-live date in six weeks.
  before: "Plan: send one all-staff email two weeks before go-live announcing the new tool, with a link to the user guide."
  after: "Map: Finance director (high power, high interest, undecided) - weekly check-in, co-own the cut-over date. Payroll lead (high power, low interest) - one briefing on the export file, sign-off by week 3. Frequent travellers in sales (low power, high interest, worried about slower refunds) - invite two to test in week 2, answer the refund-time question in every message. Everyone else - short email at go-live and a two-minute video."
  why: The single email treated everyone as one audience; the map shows who can block the launch, who will feel it most and what each needs to hear, so the manager spends attention where a surprise would be costly.
selfCheck:
  - Who could stop or slow this, and have I spoken to them directly rather than assuming their view?
  - Who will be most affected but has the least say, and how will they hear about it before it lands?
  - Does each group's message answer their concern, or is it the same message I would send everyone?
  - When did I last update this map, and has anyone's stance or influence changed since?

# Stakeholder mapping

Stakeholder mapping is the deliberate work of identifying everyone who can affect, or will be affected by, a decision or change, and then analysing their interests and influence so you can decide how to involve and communicate with each of them.[^s1][^s3] For a communicator it turns "the audience" from one undifferentiated crowd into a set of groups with different stakes, different power and different questions - each needing its own message, messenger and channel.[^s5]

## Origin

The modern stakeholder idea is usually credited to R. Edward Freeman's 1984 work on strategic management, which defined stakeholders as anyone who affects or is affected by an organisation's goals; the word itself once meant the person holding the stakes of a bet.[^s3] Business schools first used stakeholder analysis to manage people in service of organisational goals, while policy and development practitioners adapted it to bring marginalised groups into decisions.[^s3] The familiar power-versus-interest grid, which sorts stakeholders into players, subjects, context setters and the crowd, was popularised in strategy work by Colin Eden and Fran Ackermann, and John Bryson's 2004 review collected it alongside some fifteen related techniques for public and nonprofit managers.[^s1] Ronald Mitchell, Bradley Agle and Donna Wood added a theory of why some stakeholders get attention: those seen to hold power, legitimacy and urgency at once become the most salient.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Change efforts often fail not because the idea is wrong but because the people it touches were never brought along. Bryson argues that attending to stakeholders is how leaders frame proposals that are both workable and acceptable.[^s1] Paul Nutt's 1999 study of 356 senior-management decisions in U.S. and Canadian organisations found that about half were not fully adopted or sustained.[^s4] Decisions imposed by edict fared worst, and expert persuasion often failed when it ignored stakeholders' concerns such as job security.[^s4] Mapping makes those concerns visible before the announcement rather than after the resistance.[^s1][^s4]

## How to apply it

Start small and widen: Bryson recommends that a core group do a first-pass analysis, then bring in others to spot who is missing.[^s1] Rate each stakeholder's interest and influence, note their main objective and whether they are for, against or undecided, and plot them on the grid.[^s5] University of Essex project guidance then attaches an engagement level to each quadrant: high power and high interest means partner closely; high power and lower interest means keep satisfied; high interest and lower power means consult; and the rest are kept informed.[^s5] Each category feeds a communication plan that sets who is told what, when, through which channel and by whom - because, as the guidance puts it, not everyone needs everything but everyone needs something.[^s5]

Mitchell and colleagues' three attributes are a useful second filter. Someone with power and urgency but a weak claim to legitimacy - an influential group demanding an immediate change, say - needs different handling from someone with a legitimate, urgent claim but little power.[^s2] They stress that all three attributes shift over time and are matters of perception, so the map is a working hypothesis to test in conversation, not a verdict.[^s2]

The technique changes with seniority and channel. When you are managing up or selling an issue, the map is mostly about finding allies and anticipating who your sponsor will consult.[^s1] For broad audiences, the "keep informed" group can usually be reached asynchronously, while partners and potential blockers merit live conversation where concerns can surface.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

Treating the grid as a political scorecard is the biggest risk. Bryson warns that stakeholder analysis can be used to advance narrow causes and is no substitute for ethics; the aim is to understand and involve people, not to sideline them.[^s1] Never record private judgements about colleagues that you would be embarrassed to have them read. Other common mistakes: mapping once and filing the result, though interests and power change; filling the grid from your desk, which tends to surface only the usual suspects and reflect the analyst's bias; and leaving out low-power groups who will carry the change.[^s3][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

Stakeholder mapping is practitioner-validated rather than experimentally tested. Bryson notes there is little research on which techniques work best in which conditions and presents the benefit of competent analysis as a hypothesis.[^s1] Reed and colleagues' 2009 review found interest-influence matrices popular but subjective, built on hidden assumptions and hard to replicate, and recommended combining methods and repeating the analysis over time.[^s3] Nutt's findings support the broader point that ignoring stakeholders predicts failure, but they test decision tactics such as participation, not mapping itself.[^s4]

## Connections

Stakeholder mapping grows out of stakeholder theory, which defined who counts as a stakeholder, and is explained in part by stakeholder salience theory's account of power, legitimacy and urgency.[^s3][^s2] It addresses the anti-pattern of ignoring stakeholders, which Bryson identifies as a leading cause of failed decisions, and draws loose support from Nutt's 1999 study of why decisions fail.[^s1][^s4] It is applied in communication plans, where each stakeholder group gets its own message, channel and frequency.[^s5] It complements influence without authority because building a coalition starts with knowing whose support you need.[^s1] It sits under persuasion, requires audience analysis, and is related to issue selling, managing up and channel selection.[^s1][^s5]
