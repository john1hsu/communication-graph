# Norm of reciprocity

The norm of reciprocity is the shared expectation that people help those who have helped them and do not harm them. Sociologist Alvin Gouldner set it out in 1960 as a general moral rule that holds across societies, with exemptions mainly for the very young, the sick and the old.[^s1][^s2] In communication terms it is the reason a colleague who has helped you is more likely to say yes to your next request, and the reason a concession at the table tends to draw one back. Robert Robert Cialdini later made it a principle of influence for managers: give what you want to receive.[^s3]

## Origin

Gouldner's 1960 paper in the *American Sociological Review* argued that reciprocity is not just a feature of particular exchanges but a norm in its own right, one that stabilises groups and lets new relationships get started.[^s1][^s2] The norm makes two demands: return help to those who helped you, and do not injure them; it also licenses penalties for people who take without giving back.[^s2] What is returned need not be the same thing, only something the partners see as roughly equal in value, so a colleague's review of your slides can be repaid with an introduction or cover on a deadline.[^s2] Social psychologists then turned the idea into experiments on compliance, and Cialdini's 2001 *Harvard Business Review* article brought it to managers alongside social proof, consistency, liking, authority and scarcity.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Much of the influence that gets work done is lateral: you need help from people who do not report to you. Cialdini's advice is to give first, modelling the trust, cooperation and help you want back, for example lending a team member to a short-staffed peer who will remember it when you are the one short of people.[^s3] Flynn's 2003 survey of 161 employees at a large firm found that people seen as generous in exchanging favours had higher social status, that keeping exchanges in balance was linked to higher productivity, and that exchanging favours more often was associated with both, easing the tension between generosity and balance.[^s5] The same norm runs through negotiation: a concession invites one in return, the reciprocal-concessions mechanism that a 2021 replication of the door-in-the-face technique supported.[^s4]

## How to use it well at work

Reciprocity works best as a habit of genuine help, not as a tactic deployed just before a request.[^s3]

1. Give before you need anything: share information, unblock someone, credit their work. Help offered only when you want something reads as a transaction.[^s3]
2. Make the help meaningful to the receiver rather than convenient to you; Cialdini reports that a small personalised enclosure nearly doubled a charity mailing's response, from 18% to 35%.[^s3]
3. When someone thanks you, acknowledge the two-way relationship ("we'd do the same; we rely on your team too") instead of dismissing it, which Cialdini says reinforces the exchange.[^s3]
4. Keep exchanges frequent and roughly balanced; frequent, two-way favour exchange is the pattern Flynn found linked to both status and productivity.[^s5]
5. In negotiation, make concessions visible as concessions, since it is the perceived concession that triggers the urge to reciprocate.[^s4]

**Example.** A product manager needs a data engineer's time next quarter. *Before:* she emails him for the first time the week the work is due, asking him to "squeeze in" a pipeline change. *After:* two months earlier she shares a customer interview that explains why his team's backlog item matters, then later asks directly, names what she can offer in return (a clear spec and a demo slot for his team) and accepts a no. The help came first and was useful in its own right, so the later request rests on a real relationship rather than pressure.[^s3][^s5]

**Where the line is.** Favours given to create obligation, gifts from people who want your business, and pressure built on guilt are manipulation. Cialdini notes that purchasing managers admitted buying products they would otherwise have turned down after accepting a supplier's gift, which is why many organisations have gift policies; he also calls deceptive influence both unethical and foolhardy, because it invites the same treatment back.[^s3] Zlatev and Rogers found that letting people return an unsolicited gift actually raised compliance, partly through guilt, and that people preferred not to be put in that position, a psychological cost the authors counted against the tactic.[^s6]

## Evidence

The core compliance effect has held up well. In 2021, Genschow and colleagues directly replicated Cialdini's 1975 door-in-the-face study in Germany, roughly 45 years and a continent away from the original; refusing a large request still made people more likely to agree to a smaller one, at rates close to the original, and their analyses supported reciprocal concessions as the mechanism.[^s4] Field and lab work on gifts points the same way: across four main studies and two supplemental ones, including a large field experiment, Zlatev and Rogers found unsolicited gifts raised compliance with health and survey requests, and returnable gifts raised it further.[^s6] Gouldner's claim that the norm is near-universal has later support from evolutionary and primate research summarised in the *International Encyclopedia of the Social Sciences*.[^s2]

The limits matter as much. The workplace evidence on status and productivity is correlational and from single firms, so it shows association rather than cause.[^s5] Effects depend on the gift or favour being seen as genuine; Zlatev and Rogers show compliance can rise through guilt even when recipients dislike the experience, so a higher yes rate is not proof of goodwill.[^s6]

## Connections

The norm of reciprocity was named by Alvin Gouldner[^s1] and popularised as a persuasion principle by Robert Cialdini.[^s3] It is supported by the direct replication of Genschow et al. (2021)[^s4], by Flynn's (2003) study of favour exchange at work[^s5] and by Zlatev and Rogers (2020) on returnable gifts.[^s6] It complements social proof, the neighbouring principle in Cialdini's set.[^s3] It is a form of persuasion; it helps explain why concession strategy, influence without authority and Charles Osgood's unilateral-concession proposals work, and reciprocal self-disclosure is its conversational case. Help that is visibly genuine also protects your credibility.[^s3]
