# Principles of persuasion

The principles of persuasion are a short list of social-influence regularities, popularised by psychologist Robert Cialdini, that describe what tips people towards saying yes: reciprocity, scarcity, authority, consistency, liking, social proof and unity.[^s2] Each works as a mental shortcut. Rather than weighing every request from scratch, people lean on cues such as "they helped me first", "an expert recommends it" or "people like me are doing it".[^s2] For a workplace communicator the list is most useful as a checklist of true, relevant reasons a colleague or client might have to agree, which you can make visible instead of leaving them buried.

## Origin

Cialdini, a social psychologist at Arizona State University, grouped the findings of laboratory and field research on compliance into six principles, first set out in his book *Influence*; unity was added as a seventh principle in 2016.[^s2][^s3] His review with Noah Goldstein in the *Annual Review of Psychology* (2004) traced compliance and conformity to three underlying motives: wanting an accurate view of reality, wanting to build and keep relationships, and wanting to protect a positive self-image.[^s1] The principles can be read as ways those motives get triggered, often subtly and outside awareness.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Most workplace influence happens without formal authority: asking another team for data, getting a sponsor to back a proposal, or winning a client's agreement to a change of scope. Influence At Work reports examples where small, honest changes moved outcomes, such as staff at an estate agency mentioning a colleague's real experience before transferring a call, which it links to more appointments and signed contracts, and MBA negotiators who first looked for something they had in common reaching agreement far more often than those told to get straight to business.[^s2] These are practitioner-reported results, so treat the numbers as illustrations rather than benchmarks.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Run a proposal or request past each principle and ask what is genuinely true:[^s2][^s3]

- **Reciprocity:** have you already helped this person or team? Give first, and make the help useful and personal rather than generic.
- **Scarcity:** is there a real deadline, limited slot or cost of delay? State it plainly; never invent one.
- **Authority:** does relevant expertise or evidence back you? Establish it before the ask, ideally through someone else introducing it.
- **Consistency:** does the request follow from something the person has already said or committed to? Link to it, and get small commitments in their own words.
- **Liking:** what real common ground, shared goal or sincere compliment exists?
- **Social proof:** are peers similar to this audience already doing it? Name them truthfully.
- **Unity:** do you share an identity, team or long partnership? Cialdini's own example is reminding a reluctant colleague of a decade of working together, and asking for advice rather than an opinion builds a sense of partnership.[^s3]

**Example.** A product analyst needs another team's usage data. *Before:* "Can you send me the Q3 usage export? I need it for my analysis." *After:* "We've worked on the onboarding metrics together for two years, so I'd value your advice: the pricing review the VP asked for closes on Friday, and finance and support have already shared their numbers. Could you send the Q3 usage export, or tell me who should?" The rewrite surfaces true unity (shared history and advice-seeking), a real deadline and real social proof, without inflating any of them.[^s2][^s3]

## Where the ethical line is

The same cues can be faked: a false deadline, a fabricated testimonial or borrowed credentials. Cialdini's position is that the principles should be used in an ethical manner, which in practice means pointing out what is already true rather than exaggerating or manufacturing it.[^s2][^s3] Honest use, by contrast, builds the trust and long-term relationships that make later requests easier.[^s3] Pressure that people notice can also provoke resistance, so heavy-handed use of several principles at once is a risk in itself.

## Evidence

The evidence differs by principle. The underlying mechanisms are well documented in the compliance literature reviewed by Cialdini and Goldstein (2004).[^s1] A 2022 meta-analysis of 131 studies (416 effect sizes) found that scarcity cues generally raise perceived value and purchase intentions, with the most effective kind of scarcity depending on the product.[^s5] The consistency-based foot-in-the-door technique, by contrast, produced only a small average effect (r = .17) in an early meta-analysis, and worked mainly for prosocial requests.[^s4] Social proof has failed to replicate cleanly: a German hotel study found that descriptive-norm messages did no better than a standard environmental appeal, and the authors pointed to cultural context and higher baseline behaviour.[^s6] Many headline examples in popular accounts come from single studies or firm case reports. Treat the list as a set of tendencies whose strength depends on audience, culture and context, not as guaranteed levers.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

The principles are a framework within persuasion, introduced and popularised by Robert Cialdini.[^s2] They draw support from the Cialdini and Goldstein (2004) review of compliance and conformity research[^s1] and from the Barton, Zlatevska and Oppewal (2022) scarcity meta-analysis[^s5]. They are qualified by the Dillard, Hunter and Burgoon (1984) meta-analysis of sequential-request techniques[^s4] and challenged by the Bohner and Schlüter (2014) hotel replication.[^s6] They apply in negotiation, where establishing similarity first helped MBA negotiators reach agreement.[^s2] They complement influence without authority, because shared identity and advice-seeking help when you have no formal power over the colleague you need.[^s3] Two principles have their own articles here: the norm of reciprocity and social proof. Authority overlaps with credibility, and pushing the principles too hard risks psychological reactance. The elaboration likelihood model explains when such cues matter most, and the classical rhetorical appeals offer an older map of the same territory.[^s1]
