steps:
  - Write the issue in one sentence and link it to a goal, metric or priority your leaders already own, framed as an opportunity where honestly possible.
  - Find out how the decision maker likes to receive proposals - data or story, a written pre-read or a conversation - and what evidence counts in your organisation.
  - Build a short business case - cost, benefit, risk and what happens if nothing changes - and bring at least one concrete proposed solution, not only the problem.
  - Test the idea with peers and one or two respected senior people first; fold in their objections and ask who should be involved or informed.
  - Read the context before you raise it - is leadership listening, under pressure, or recently burned by a similar idea? - and pick a moment and channel that fit.
  - Raise it calmly, acknowledge any past decision you are revisiting without blaming whoever made it, and ask for one specific next step.
  - Follow up, report what happened, and decide deliberately whether to persist, re-bundle the issue with another priority, or let it go for now.
example:
  situation: A support team lead wants leadership to fund a fix for a confusing billing page that drives a large share of tickets.
  before: "Hi all, I wanted to flag (again) that the billing page is a mess. My team is drowning in tickets and nobody seems to care. Can we please prioritise this?"
  after: "Proposal for Thursday's ops review: about a third of last month's support tickets trace to the billing page. A two-sprint redesign, already scoped with product, would cut handling costs and supports this year's customer-retention goal. I'd like a decision on adding it to Q1. One-page case attached; finance has checked the numbers."

selfCheck:
  - Can I state in one sentence which of leadership's existing goals this issue helps, and is that link honest?
  - Am I bringing a proposed solution and the evidence my leaders usually ask for, or only a complaint?
  - Who else have I involved, and would any of them be surprised to hear me raise it?
  - Is this the right moment and forum, or am I raising it because I am frustrated right now?
  - If they say no, what will I do next - and am I prepared to accept that answer?

# Selling issues to leadership

Issue selling is the deliberate effort to get senior leaders to pay attention to an issue - a risk, an opportunity, an outdated practice, a resource need - that you can see from where you sit but they cannot.[^s1][^s3] It is a communication technique, not a sales trick: you are competing for scarce executive attention, so the way you frame, time and route the issue largely decides whether it is heard at all.[^s1]

## Origin

The term comes from organisational scholars Jane E. Dutton and Susan J. Ashford of the University of Michigan, whose 1993 *Academy of Management Review* paper argued that top managers' limited time and attention make issue selling a key early step in organisational decisions.[^s1] They built their framework from three research traditions: upward influence, claiming behaviours and impression management.[^s1] Field studies followed, including a 1997 study of how middle managers judge whether the context is safe for selling an issue and a 2001 study of the moves used in successful and unsuccessful attempts.[^s4][^s2] Ashford and James Detert later turned the findings into practical advice for selling ideas to a boss, published in *Harvard Business Review* in 2015.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Leaders cannot see everything; many of the problems and opportunities that drive change are first noticed by people in the middle or on the front line.[^s1] If those people stay quiet, or raise issues in ways leaders tune out, the organisation loses the information.[^s3] For the individual, being able to get a hearing upward is a core part of managing up: Ashford reports that managers who use these tactics succeed more often than those who do not.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Dutton and colleagues grouped what successful sellers did into three kinds of moves.[^s2][^s3] **Packaging**: present the issue as a business case and bundle it with goals or issues leaders already care about.[^s3] **Involvement**: bring in peers and senior allies, formally or informally, rather than going it alone.[^s3] **Process**: do your homework, use the appropriate channels, and pay attention to timing - including when to keep pushing and when to bring others in.[^s3] Ashford and Detert add that you should tailor the pitch to your audience's goals and preferred evidence, frame it as an opportunity, keep emotions in check, and bring proposed solutions along with the problem.[^s5]

Before you sell, read the context. In the 1997 study, managers saw leaders' willingness to listen and a supportive culture as signs it was safe to raise an issue, and fear of negative consequences, downsizing and uncertainty as warning signs.[^s4] They felt more at risk of looking bad when they broke the norms for raising issues, sold in a politically exposed way, or had a distant relationship with top management.[^s4] That is why involvement and a working relationship with your manager come before the pitch, not after it.[^s4][^s2]

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is pitching from frustration. Ashford and Detert advise keeping passion in check so it does not tip into anger on either side of the conversation.[^s5] Other mistakes: bringing a problem with no proposed fix, ignoring the organisation's norms for evidence and format, going it alone, and raising the issue at a moment when leaders are absorbed by something else.[^s5][^s3] Ethically, packaging means connecting an issue honestly to real goals. Overstating the data, or hiding costs to make the case look better, is spin. It damages trust, and the impression-management research the framework draws on shows that how you are seen is part of what is at stake.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The core evidence is a 2001 study of 82 issue-selling accounts from managers at a single nonprofit hospital, which found that packaging, involvement and timing moves were associated with whether issues gained attention.[^s2][^s3] The accounts were retrospective and came from one organisation, and the authors note that the findings largely confirm conventional change-management advice.[^s3] Work on context is somewhat stronger: a 2002 study combined interviews with an experiment and found that an exclusive organisational culture was the strongest cue reducing women managers' willingness to raise gender-equity issues.[^s6] The evidence therefore supports the moves as sensible and associated with success, but no large controlled trials test them as a technique, so the status here is mixed.[^s2][^s6] The technique also has limits: it cannot make a closed or punitive culture safe, and sometimes the right call is to wait or let an issue go.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

Issue selling was introduced by Jane E. Dutton and Susan J. Ashford in "Selling Issues to Top Management", drawing on upward influence and impression management.[^s1] Its main empirical demonstration is Dutton and colleagues' 2001 study of issue-selling moves, supported by their 1997 work on reading the context.[^s2][^s4] It develops managing up, because selling ideas to your boss is one of the skill's most visible tasks, and it is typically applied in executive briefings and other pitches to senior leaders.[^s5] It sits under persuasion and requires audience analysis, since tailoring to leaders' goals and evidence norms is audience analysis applied upward.[^s5] It is related to influence without authority and stakeholder mapping (finding allies and their interests), bottom line up front (leading with the ask), and psychological safety, which shapes whether people feel able to raise issues at all.[^s4][^s6]
