steps:
  - Write the claim you need the audience to accept in one sentence, then pick the one or two numbers that bear most directly on it - not every metric you have.
  - State each number with its denominator and time frame ("41 of 300 tickets last quarter"), and give absolute changes alongside any relative ones ("from 4 to 6 in 1,000, a 50% rise").
  - Add the comparison the audience needs to judge size - last year, a benchmark, the alternative option - using the same denominator and period for every option.
  - Say where the number comes from and what it does not cover, in a clause, so a sceptic can check it rather than dismiss it.
  - Pair the figures with one typical example that makes them concrete, and show which the example represents ("she is one of the 41").
  - Show the key comparison as a simple chart or icon array when it matters, test the slide or paragraph on a colleague, and ask what number they would repeat.
example:
  situation: A support lead asks the operations director to fund a second on-call engineer.
  before: "Escalations have increased 100% and customers are really unhappy. One enterprise client told me last week this is the worst support they've had. We urgently need another on-call engineer."
  after: "Weekend escalations went from 9 to 18 a month between Q1 and Q3 - 18 of our 210 tickets a month now wait over 4 hours, against our 1-hour target. One enterprise client waited 6 hours on a Saturday outage last week; that is the typical case, not the worst. A second on-call engineer would cover both weekend shifts; I'd like to start in January and report the wait-time figure monthly."
  why: The before version's "100%" hides a small base and leans on one angry quote; the after version gives absolute counts, the denominator, the target as a comparison and a representative example, so the director can judge size and typicality and is more likely to trust the request.

# Statistical evidence

Statistical evidence supports a claim with numbers that summarise many cases - how often something happens, how big a change is, how one option compares with another - instead of resting on a single example. Argumentation researchers treat it as one of four common evidence types, alongside anecdotes, causal explanations and expert opinion.[^s2] At work it is the difference between "customers hate the new flow" and "41 of 300 new customers abandoned setup last month, up from 18".

## Origin

Comparing the persuasiveness of evidence types became a sustained question in communication research in the 1990s. A 1994 review by Baesler and Burgoon counted 19 studies: anecdotes won in 13 and statistics in 2, but once vividness, length and comprehensibility were held equal, statistics came out ahead; later work pointed out that anecdotes in older studies were often simply longer and livelier.[^s2] Mike Allen and Raymond Preiss's 1997 meta-analysis found a small average advantage for statistical over narrative evidence (r of about .07), and Allen and colleagues' 2000 experiment tested that claim directly.[^s1] Gerd Gigerenzer and colleagues at the Max Planck Institute later showed that *how* numbers are presented decides whether people understand them at all.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Decision-makers are asked to fund, approve or change things on the strength of claims, and numbers let them judge size and typicality - whether a problem is common or a vivid exception. In a 2002 Dutch study using 20 claims, anecdotal evidence was less persuasive than statistical, causal or expert evidence, which did about equally well.[^s2] How the numbers are shown matters as much as having them: relative changes without base rates make benefits look bigger and harms smaller than they are, and widespread statistical illiteracy among professionals and the public means many listeners will not spot the difference.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Lead with the claim and choose the few numbers that bear on it; experts selecting only the critical figures improved understanding in risk-communication studies, while extra detail distracted.[^s4] Give numbers rather than verbal labels such as "rare" or "a lot", which different people read very differently.[^s4] Use absolute figures, a constant denominator and a constant time frame for every option you compare, and add a simple chart or pictograph for the key comparison.[^s4] Then pair the numbers with a representative case: in Allen and colleagues' experiment with 1,270 students and 15 messages, messages combining statistics with a narrative produced the most agreement, statistics alone came next, then narrative alone, then no evidence.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

The classic error is the dramatic relative figure on a small base. Gigerenzer and colleagues describe a 1995 UK warning that a contraceptive pill "doubled" clot risk; in absolute terms risk rose from about 1 to about 2 in 7,000, and the scare was followed by an estimated 13,000 extra abortions.[^s3] Other mistakes are burying the figure that matters among many others, switching denominators or time frames between options, and quoting survival-style rates that can improve without any real change in outcomes.[^s3][^s4] The ethical line is transparency: choose the fair comparison, show the base rate and source, and do not pick the frame that exaggerates your case.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but modest and context-dependent. The meta-analytic advantage for statistics is small, and the 2000 experiment's differences between conditions were small too.[^s1] Results vary by claim: earlier studies often tested one claim and one example of each evidence type, so findings did not generalise well, and causal evidence beat statistics in one study but not in another.[^s2] Numbers can also dampen emotion-driven action: in a 2007 set of experiments, adding statistics to an appeal featuring one identifiable person cut donations from about $2.38 to $1.43, close to the $1.14 for statistics alone.[^s5] So statistics tend to win on belief and judgement, while a single case may do more to move people to act; most of this research uses students, health and charity messages, so workplace effects are an extrapolation.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

Statistical evidence is a form of persuasion and *complements* narrative persuasion: combining the two outperformed either alone in Allen and colleagues' 2000 experiment, which *demonstrates* the technique.[^s1] It is *supported by* Hoeken and Hustinx's 2002 comparison of evidence types.[^s2] It *contrasts with* the identifiable victim effect, in which one named person moves people more than statistics about many.[^s5] It is *influenced by* Gerd Gigerenzer's work on transparent statistics, which also underpins presenting risk numbers as natural frequencies and connects to gain-loss framing, since the same figure can be framed to look larger or smaller.[^s3] Honest, checkable numbers build credibility, slides built around a single evidence-backed claim carry them well, and choosing which number matters requires audience analysis.[^s4]
