# Rhetorical appeals

The rhetorical appeals are the three kinds of persuasive means that a speaker or writer builds into a message: **ethos**, the speaker's character as it comes across in the message; **pathos**, the emotional state the message puts the audience in; and **logos**, the argument itself.[^s1] As a working framework, they give you a quick test for any proposal, pitch or update: have I given people a reason to trust me, a reason to care, and a reason to believe the claim?[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The division comes from Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (4th century BCE), which calls these the "technical" proofs: means the speaker invents through the speech itself, as opposed to pre-existing evidence such as witnesses or contracts.[^s1] Aristotle says the speaker's character must be established within the speech, through visible practical intelligence, virtue and goodwill, rather than borrowed from a prior reputation.[^s1] He treats emotions as relevant because they change how people judge, and he makes the enthymeme (a compact deductive argument) and the example the core of logical proof.[^s1] Later teaching shortened all this to "ethos, pathos, logos", and writing programmes such as the Purdue OWL add **kairos**, how a message uses its moment and setting, and **telos**, its purpose.[^s2] The popular version is looser than Aristotle's: the OWL, for instance, recasts pathos as appealing to any of an audience's sensibilities, not only emotion.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Workplace messages usually fail by leaning on one appeal. Analysts send pure logos (a table and a recommendation), so readers do not see why it matters now; sales-style pitches lean on enthusiasm with thin proof; new managers rely on their title instead of showing judgement.[^s1][^s2] Persuasion research finds that each appeal does real, but different and conditional, work. A 2010 meta-analysis of 18 reports found that credible sources persuade more, mostly when the audience has no prior view and little motivation to scrutinise, and that the advantage fades within weeks.[^s3] A 2015 meta-analysis of 127 articles found that fear appeals produce small but reliable changes in attitudes and behaviour, roughly doubling when the message also says how to act.[^s4][^s6] And a test of evidence types found that combining a story with statistics moved attitudes more than either alone.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Plan with the steps above, then check the draft against the audience. A sceptical expert audience will scrutinise logos, so lead with evidence and answer objections; an audience new to the issue leans more on whether they trust you, so establish ethos early.[^s3] For emotional content, attach every concern to a concrete, doable action, because threat without a path forward is what weakens fear appeals.[^s4] Pair one representative case with the aggregate numbers so the story makes the figure felt and the figure shows the story is typical.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating ethos as job title.** Aristotle's point is that credibility is shown in the message: sound judgement, honesty and visible goodwill toward the audience.[^s1]
- **Pathos as pressure.** Aristotle criticised appeals that stir pity or anger unrelated to the matter being judged; inventing urgency or inflating risk is manipulation, and it collapses once people check.[^s1]
- **Evidence without a reason to care.** A lone statistic persuades less than the same number tied to a case the audience recognises.[^s5]
- **Counting on reputation to carry a hard sell.** Source credibility does least with people who already hold a view, and its effect decays.[^s3]

## Evidence

The appeals are a classical analytic framework, not a tested intervention, and no body of studies shows that "balancing" all three beats other planning methods, so this article rates it `practitioner`.[^s1][^s2] Its components map onto well-studied effects: source credibility (moderate, short-lived, strongest for low-involvement audiences), fear appeals (small overall effect, d = 0.29, larger with efficacy statements), and evidence type (combined narrative and statistical evidence highest, though differences were small).[^s3][^s4][^s5] Scholars also note an unresolved tension in Aristotle himself between rejecting and accepting emotional appeals.[^s1]
