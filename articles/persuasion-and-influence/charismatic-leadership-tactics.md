steps:
  - Write the one decision or goal your talk is about, and check that you believe it and can back it with facts.
  - Frame it with a contrast (what we will do and not do, or where we are versus where we are going) and a three-part list of reasons or steps.
  - Add one short, true story or anecdote and at most one metaphor that makes the abstract point concrete.
  - State the shared stake and the values involved, set a high but credible expectation, and say plainly that you are confident the group can meet it.
  - Use one rhetorical question to bring the audience into the problem, then answer it.
  - Rehearse aloud on video with open gestures, varied facial expression and an animated voice; count the tactics you used and cut any that sound forced.
  - Get feedback from a colleague on whether it sounded like you, and repeat over several weeks rather than cramming before one talk.

# Charismatic leadership tactics

Charismatic leadership tactics (CLTs) are a defined set of speaking behaviours that make a leader's message vivid, emotional and value-laden, so that listeners see the speaker as more charismatic and more leader-like.[^s1] There are nine verbal tactics - metaphors, stories and anecdotes, contrasts, lists (usually of three), rhetorical questions, expressing moral conviction, reflecting the group's sentiments, setting high expectations and communicating confidence that they can be met - and three nonverbal ones: body gestures, facial expressions and an animated voice.[^s1] The point of the technique is that charisma is treated not as a gift but as behaviours you can learn, practise and count.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Organisational psychologist John Antonakis and colleagues at the University of Lausanne drew the tactics from earlier research on charismatic rhetoric and tested whether they could be taught.[^s1] In a field experiment with 34 middle managers at a large Swiss company, those randomly assigned to a five-hour workshop plus feedback and coaching were rated by co-workers as more charismatic three months later than untrained managers.[^s1][^s2] In a second study, 41 MBA students gave a speech, trained for about 20 hours over seven weeks with filmed practice and feedback, then gave the same speech again: their use of CLTs roughly doubled and independent raters scored the second speeches higher on leadership.[^s1] Their 2011 paper in *Academy of Management Learning & Education* reported a medium overall effect, about d = 0.62.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Much workplace communication fails not because the facts are wrong but because listeners cannot see why they matter. CLTs package the same content so it is easier to remember and act on, and they give vague feedback such as "be more inspiring" a concrete practice list.[^s1] In the Lausanne MBA study, speeches with more tactics were rated higher on trust, competence and influence.[^s2] Charismatic messages can also change what people do: in a 2020 replication in *The Leadership Quarterly*, a larger study (274 participants) found that a charismatic appeal raised individual work performance, working mainly through its vision content and independently of performance pay.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Start from substance: a decision, a goal or a change you believe in and can support with facts.[^s1] Then build the message around a few tactics rather than all twelve - a contrast to frame the choice, a list of three reasons, one true story and one clear statement of confidence usually do most of the work.[^s1] Practise delivery separately, because the nonverbal tactics (gesture, expression, vocal variety) were trained with filmed rehearsal and feedback, not by reading about them.[^s1] Count your tactics in a recording, as the researchers' coders did, and repeat over weeks; the BPS summary of the study stresses there is no quick fix.[^s1][^s2]

Ethical line: because the tactics raise how trustworthy and competent a speaker seems, they are only as honest as the content.[^s2] Use them to make a true case clearer and more memorable, not to inflate a weak one, invent stories, or pressure people into a decision they have not had time to weigh.

## Common mistakes

- **Overdoing it.** Inexperienced users can tip into self-parody - exaggerated gestures and clumsy metaphors - which damages credibility.[^s2]
- **Vision without follow-through.** Across nearly 600 business leaders, Vergauwe and colleagues found that very charismatic leaders were rated less effective than moderately charismatic ones, mainly because they were weaker on day-to-day operational behaviour.[^s5] Pair the inspiring message with clear owners, dates and next steps.
- **Assuming it travels online.** A preregistered set of experiments in five countries found the tactics improved performance in person but had essentially no effect on performance in virtual settings (pooled d = 0.01).[^s3]
- **One-off cramming.** The trained gains came from hours of practice with recorded feedback, not a single read-through.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Evidence here is mixed. The original experiments were small, the control managers also improved in Study 1, and the MBA study had no control group and measured ratings rather than on-the-job behaviour.[^s1] Replications are encouraging in person: Meslec and colleagues fully replicated a charisma effect on performance in their larger second study, though not in a first study that delivered the speech by video.[^s4] Ernst, Banks and colleagues' prospective meta-analysis (2021) found moderate-to-large effects on performance face to face (about d = 0.52 across four studies) but near zero in virtual contexts.[^s3] And more is not always better: very high charisma is linked with weaker rated effectiveness.[^s5]

## Connections

Charismatic leadership tactics were introduced and tested by John Antonakis and colleagues, demonstrated in their 2011 two-study training paper.[^s1] They are a practical route to developing executive presence, since that skill is judged partly on projected confidence and communication.[^s1] The approach is supported by Meslec and colleagues' 2020 replication, and challenged by Ernst and colleagues' finding that the effect vanishes online and by Vergauwe and colleagues' evidence that too much charisma backfires.[^s3][^s4][^s5] It is applied most often in presentations and speeches.[^s2] It sits under persuasion, requires audience analysis, and is related to narrative persuasion, gesturing, paralanguage, credibility, presentation rehearsal and self-recording playback, which supply the stories, delivery and practice the tactics rely on.[^s1]
