  - Decide what you want the listener to think, feel or do at the end, and where they start from today.
  - Pick one true case that is typical of the problem, not the most dramatic one you can find, and check you may share it (anonymise people and clients).
  - Build it around a specific character with a goal, an obstacle, what happened and why, and the stakes - concrete enough that the listener can picture it.
  - Open with the story or a hook from it, keep it short, and cut details that do not move the cause-and-effect chain forward.
  - Put the numbers right after it to show the story is representative, and say so explicitly ("this happened to roughly one in five new customers last quarter").
  - Close by linking the story to your specific request, then test it on a colleague and ask what stuck and what felt unconvincing.

# Narrative persuasion

Narrative persuasion means making your case through a story - a particular person facing a problem, with events and their consequences unfolding over time - instead of relying only on arguments and figures. Communication researchers define narrative by three features: causality, time and character, which is why the same story can be told in a hallway conversation, a slide or an email.[^s5] At work it is the difference between "churn is up 7 points" and "here is what happened to one customer last month".

## Origin

The modern research programme rests on Melanie Green and Timothy Brock's 2000 studies at Ohio State University. They proposed *transportation* - absorption into a story through attention, imagery and emotion - as the mechanism by which narratives change beliefs, and built a scale to measure it. Across four experiments, more transported readers held more story-consistent beliefs, judged the sympathetic characters more favourably and spotted fewer passages that rang false; reducing transportation through instructions weakened those effects. Labelling a story as fact or fiction made little difference.[^s1] Practitioner teaching, such as Jennifer Aaker's at Stanford, draws on this work and on related research on identifiable victims and emotion.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Most workplace persuasion leans on data, yet a meta-analysis found that statistics moved beliefs and attitudes more than narratives, while narratives moved *intentions* more - the willingness to act.[^s4] Stories also help non-expert audiences: narrative text is associated with better comprehension and recall than expository text, which matters when you brief people outside your speciality.[^s5] And a well-told story is easier for listeners to retell, so the case travels to people who were not in the room.[^s6]

## How to apply it

Start from the goal: what should the audience think, feel or do, and where are they now? Then choose a protagonist (not necessarily you), a clear challenge with real stakes and a short arc, and weave the facts into the story rather than appending them as a separate section.[^s6] The meta-analytic evidence points to what makes people get absorbed: an imaginable plot, identifiable characters and verisimilitude - the sense that it could really happen - each predicted greater transportation.[^s3] Because stories and statistics persuade different outcomes, pair them: the story to make the problem vivid and motivate action, the numbers to show it is typical.[^s4] Finally, test the story on a real listener and ask what resonated and what confused them.[^s6]

## Common mistakes

The most serious is choosing an unrepresentative story. When a vivid case and statistics are presented together, people's judgements tend to follow the case even when the overall evidence points elsewhere, so a worst-case anecdote can mislead an audience about how common a problem is.[^s5] That same power is where the ethical line sits: use true stories (or clearly labelled composites), keep the causes and facts accurate, choose typical cases, and give the audience the figures they need to judge the story for themselves rather than using it to slip past their scrutiny.[^s5] Other mistakes are practical: a story with no clear link to the ask, a long preamble before the point, and an obvious "now let me tell you a story" pitch - narratives persuade less when the persuasive intent becomes conspicuous.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The effect is real but modest. A 2016 meta-analysis of experiments comparing narrative exposure with controls found small positive effects on beliefs (r = .17, 37 studies), attitudes (r = .19, 40 studies), intentions (r = .17, 28 studies) and behaviours (r = .23, only 5 studies); medium and research design did not change the size, and unidentified moderators remain.[^s2] A 2014 meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes from 76 articles found transportation raised affective responses, beliefs, attitudes and intentions and somewhat reduced critical thoughts.[^s3] Narratives do not beat statistics across the board: for beliefs and attitudes, statistical evidence did better in a 2015 health-communication meta-analysis.[^s4] Much of this research uses health, consumer and fiction messages with students and consumers, so effects in workplace decisions are an extrapolation; treat storytelling as one tool to combine with evidence, not a substitute for it.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

Narrative persuasion is a form of persuasion and is *explained by* narrative transportation, demonstrated in Green and Brock's 2000 experiments.[^s1] It is *supported by* the Braddock and Dillard (2016) and van Laer et al. (2014) meta-analyses.[^s2][^s3] It *complements* statistical evidence, which better shifts beliefs and attitudes while stories better shift intentions.[^s4] It relates to the elaboration likelihood model, since absorbed listeners generate fewer counterarguments, and to credibility, which an unrepresentative story can quickly erode.[^s5][^s3] It is a natural tool when selling issues to leadership or giving a presentation, and it requires audience analysis to choose a story that fits where the listeners start.[^s6]
