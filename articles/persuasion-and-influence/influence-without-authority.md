steps:
  - Treat the person whose help you need as a potential ally, even if past dealings were tense, and drop any story that they are simply difficult.
  - Write down exactly what you need from them, which parts are essential and which are nice to have, and check that the request serves the work rather than only your own ambitions.
  - Diagnose their world - how they are measured and rewarded, what their boss expects, what their workload and pressures are this quarter.
  - List the currencies they are likely to value (resources, information, help, recognition, visibility, a link to a larger purpose) and the ones you can actually offer, including cheap ones you control yourself.
  - Assess the relationship; if trust is low, repair it first, and adapt to how they prefer to work (data or story, email or a quick call).
  - Make a concrete, honest offer that trades something they value for what you need, then deliver on it and keep in touch afterwards so the relationship exists before you need it again.
example:
  situation: A product analyst needs an engineer on another team to add tracking events before a launch, but has no authority over the engineer's priorities.
  before: "Hi, I need these six tracking events added before Thursday's release. It's important for the launch, so please prioritise it. Thanks!"
  after: "Hi - I know your team is measured on the release shipping cleanly, so I don't want to add risk. Of the six events, two are essential for the launch review (sign-up and first purchase); the rest can wait a sprint. I've written the event specs and test cases so it should be about an hour of work, and I'll credit your team in the launch metrics readout to the VP. Would Wednesday morning work, or is there a better slot?"
  why: The rewrite shows an understanding of the engineer's goals, cuts the ask to what is essential, lowers the cost by doing the preparatory work, and offers currencies the engineer may value (less effort, visibility with leadership) instead of borrowing urgency the analyst has no standing to impose.

  - Would I be comfortable if they saw exactly how I planned this request, or does it rely on spin?

# Influence without authority

Influence without authority is the everyday work of getting help from people you cannot order to help you: peers in other teams, specialists, your own boss, or a client. In the model developed by Allan R. Cohen and David L. Bradford, influence in these situations rests on exchange - you obtain what you need by giving the other person something they value in return, explicitly or implicitly.[^s1] The authors treat familiar tactics such as reasoned argument, inspirational appeals and coalition-building as different forms of that same trade, rather than as separate tricks.[^s1]

## Origin

Cohen, a management professor at Babson College, and Bradford, an organisational-behaviour lecturer at Stanford's Graduate School of Business, began writing about influence in organisations in the 1980s and developed the model in their book *Influence Without Authority*, published by Wiley.[^s2][^s5] A condensed version appeared in the *Journal of Organizational Excellence* in 2005, alongside the second edition, and a third edition followed in 2017, presenting the model as a checklist for getting help from colleagues, managers and customers.[^s1][^s2][^s5] The core of the model is the "Law of Reciprocity": people broadly expect to be paid back for what they do for others.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Much of what a person needs to get work done - resources, information, support - is controlled by colleagues, managers or customers who are outside their reporting line, which is exactly the gap the model is meant to close.[^s5][^s2] Cohen and Bradford argue that people often abandon sound influence habits exactly when stakes rise: they write off a colleague after a few refusals, fail to learn what that colleague values, or blur personal ambition with the organisation's needs.[^s1] Their book illustrates the alternative with a new hire asked to get feuding product and technology groups to cooperate: he interviewed everyone without assigning blame, shared credit with his manager, visited engineers to learn their concerns and delivered early releases on time, and the groups eventually worked together.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Work through the model as a checklist, much as a pilot does before take-off; the authors say it is most useful when you face resistance, a poor relationship, or one chance to get it right.[^s2] Start by assuming the other person is a potential ally, clarify your goals and priorities, and then diagnose their world: how they are measured and rewarded, what their boss expects and what pressures they face.[^s2] Next, map currencies - the things they value. Cohen and Bradford group them as inspiration-related (vision, excellence, doing the right thing), task-related (resources, assistance, information, fast response), position-related (recognition, visibility, reputation, contacts), relationship-related (inclusion, understanding, personal support) and personal (gratitude, ownership, comfort).[^s1] Inventory what you can offer, including low-cost currencies you control without anyone's permission, assess and if necessary repair the relationship, then make the trade while attending to both the task result and the relationship.[^s1] Finally, build connections early, before you need them.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

The commonest failure is appearing manipulative: calculated favours, excessive flattery, or dressing up self-interest as "what's best for the company" erode the trust that makes exchange possible.[^s1] Other pitfalls are overusing one currency until it loses value (praise from someone who praises everything), overpromising what you cannot deliver, and opening with negative currencies such as escalating to a shared boss or withholding support, which can spiral into mutual retaliation.[^s1] The ethical line is honesty: the model works by finding genuine overlaps of interest, and the authors advise ending gracefully when values truly cannot be bridged rather than forcing the deal.[^s1]

## Evidence

The model itself is a practitioner framework built from the authors' consulting and teaching cases, and has not been tested as a package.[^s2] Field research on influence tactics gives partial support. A 1992 study of 128 managers rated by over 1,000 subordinates, peers and bosses found rational persuasion, inspirational appeals and consultation most likely to win task commitment in every direction, while exchange and ingratiation helped moderately with peers and subordinates but not with bosses; pressure, coalitions and appeals to legitimacy were least effective.[^s3] That study was correlational and relied partly on the same respondents for tactics and outcomes.[^s3] A 2017 meta-analysis of 49 samples (about 9,000 people) found rational persuasion, inspirational appeals, apprising, collaboration, ingratiation and consultation positively related to task and relationship outcomes and pressure negatively related; rational persuasion was the only tactic consistently positive across all conditions, and explicit exchange was not among the reliably positive tactics.[^s4] So the evidence favours the model's emphasis on understanding the other side and offering task-relevant value, but is weaker for overt quid-pro-quo bargaining, which is why its status here is mixed.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

The technique was introduced by Allan R. Cohen and David L. Bradford in their book *Influence Without Authority*, and it is explained by the norm of reciprocity, which the authors place at its centre.[^s1][^s2] It is typically applied in cross-functional collaboration, where help must be won from groups with their own goals.[^s2] It is supported, with caveats, by Yukl and Tracey's (1992) study of tactics used with peers, subordinates and bosses and, more loosely, by Lee and colleagues' (2017) meta-analysis of influence tactics.[^s3][^s4] It sits under persuasion and requires audience analysis, since diagnosing the other person's world is the same discipline applied to one stakeholder; it relates to stakeholder mapping, principled negotiation's focus on interests, and reciprocity as a principle of persuasion.[^s1]
