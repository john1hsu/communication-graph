# Monroe's motivated sequence

Monroe's motivated sequence is a five-step outline for a message that asks people to act: win **attention**, establish a **need**, offer a solution that **satisfies** it, help the audience **visualize** the result, and close with a specific **call to action**.[^s1][^s2] It is the default organising pattern for persuasive speeches in introductory speech courses, and it also turns up in sales pitches, advertising and public-health appeals.[^s1][^s7][^s2]

## Origin

Alan H. Monroe taught speech at Purdue University and developed the sequence in the mid-1930s, presenting it in his textbook *Principles of Speech*.[^s1][^s3][^s2] By one textbook account he worked inductively, noticing that successful persuasive messages tended to share a common shape and abstracting five steps from them.[^s7] The order is meant to track how people think when they face a problem they want resolved: notice it, feel that it matters, then look for a way out.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Workplace proposals often fail not because the idea is weak but because the speaker jumps straight to the solution before the audience accepts there is a problem, or ends without saying what they want. The sequence forces both: a need the audience recognises as theirs, and a closing request small and concrete enough to act on.[^s4][^s3] Because it is a checklist rather than a script, it scales from a 20-minute budget proposal to a 60-second pitch; school-leadership training material, for example, has learners use the five steps to draft an elevator pitch for governors, parents or colleagues.[^s6]

## How to apply it

1. **Attention.** Open with something tied to the audience's concerns - a figure, a short incident, a question - rather than background about yourself.[^s1][^s4]
2. **Need.** Show the problem, who it affects and how badly, in terms this audience cares about.[^s1][^s2]
3. **Satisfaction.** Present the solution clearly enough that someone new to the problem could follow it, show it works, and answer the strongest objection.[^s1][^s3]
4. **Visualization.** Describe the concrete future with the solution, without it, or both.[^s3][^s4]
5. **Action.** Ask for one specific, easy next step and remove the obvious reason to delay.[^s4][^s1]

For example, a facilities coordinator proposing a shared desk-booking tool might open with last month's count of staff who came in and found no desk (attention), show how that cost hybrid teams their in-office days (need), outline the tool and its four-week pilot cost (satisfaction), contrast a predictable Tuesday team day with the current scramble (visualization), and ask the operations director to approve a pilot for one floor by Friday (action). The same content delivered as "here's a tool I found" tends to stall at "why do we need it?"[^s3][^s4]

## Common mistakes

Spending most of the time on attention and need and rushing the solution and ask; a weak or off-topic hook, which can lose listeners before the need is stated;[^s4] and visualizations that inflate benefits. One textbook draws the ethical line plainly: use the outline only to move people toward something that actually serves their interest, not to manufacture urgency.[^s3] A practical caution: for senior readers who expect the conclusion first, a delayed solution can read as stalling, so consider stating the ask up front and using the steps as the body.

## Evidence

The sequence is widely taught as the most effective structure for persuasion, but that claim rests on Monroe's analysis of successful speeches and on illustrative examples, not on testing.[^s2][^s7] The one controlled test found little to support it: Micciche, Pryor and Butler (2000) gave students a written argument for a $50 parking-fee increase with the five steps in Monroe's order, in reverse order, or in random order. Attitude change did not differ across the groups, and the Monroe order was rated more comprehensible in only one of four comparisons.[^s5] That study is small, used written rather than spoken messages and a single topic, so it does not show the sequence is useless; it shows that the order of the steps is not the active ingredient. Hence `contested`: treat it as a coverage checklist - did I establish need, offer a solution, and ask for something? - rather than a proven persuasion formula.[^s5]

## Connections

The framework was introduced by Alan H. Monroe at Purdue in the 1930s.[^s1] It is applied in presentations and persuasive speeches,[^s2] and gives structure to the elevator pitch, where it supplies a compact attention-need-solution-ask outline.[^s6] It is challenged by Micciche, Pryor and Butler (2000), whose test found no persuasive advantage for the canonical order.[^s5] It belongs under persuasion and depends on audience analysis, because the need step only works if it is framed in the audience's own terms.[^s2][^s4] It overlaps with narrative persuasion in the visualization step, with refutational two-sided messages where satisfaction answers objections,[^s3] and contrasts with answer-first structures such as bottom line up front and the pyramid principle, which put the request before the case.
