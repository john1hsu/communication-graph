# Impression management

Impression management is the process by which people try to influence how others see them, whether deliberately or out of habit.[^s2] It runs from honest selection of true facts, such as which wins to mention in a status update, to outright deception.[^s2][^s4] At work the useful question is rarely *whether* to manage impressions - everyone does - but whether the image you project is accurate and whether your tactics will read as credible to this audience.[^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Sociologist Erving Goffman introduced the idea in *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*, first published in Scotland in 1956 and widely read after its 1959 American edition.[^s2] Goffman used a theatrical analogy: each person is an actor choosing which parts of themselves to put forward, and the performance is a joint effort that depends on everyone sustaining a shared definition of the situation and sparing each other embarrassment.[^s2]

Psychologists later turned the idea into testable models. In a 1990 *Psychological Bulletin* review, Mark Leary and Robin Kowalski split it into two components: *impression motivation*, how much someone cares about the image they create, and *impression construction*, which image they try to create and how.[^s1] Motivation rises when the impression bears on your goals, when those goals are valuable, and when there is a gap between how you are seen and how you want to be seen; construction is shaped by your self-concept, the images you want and want to avoid, your role, the audience's values and your current reputation.[^s1]

Organizational researchers mostly use Jones and Pittman's 1982 taxonomy of five tactics, each aimed at an image: self-promotion (competent), ingratiation (likeable), exemplification (dedicated), intimidation (dangerous) and supplication (needy).[^s3] Bolino and Turnley's 22-item scale measures these five, and a 2007 validation with three samples of full-time employees confirmed the five-factor structure.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Other people's impressions decide a lot at work: who gets hired, who is rated as a high performer, whose idea gets heard.[^s4] A meta-analysis found that impression management is used more in interviews than in day-to-day job settings, and that it is related to the ratings people receive in both.[^s4] Getting it wrong is also costly: tactics that look self-serving can lower how much people like and trust you.[^s3][^s5]

The model also explains why the same behaviour works in one setting and fails in another. Motivation spikes in high-stakes, evaluative moments such as a job interview, a promotion panel or a first meeting with a senior leader, and the right image depends on what that audience values.[^s1]

## Using it well at work

Treat it as a planning lens, not a script. Before a high-stakes conversation, name the one honest impression you most need to leave (for example, "reliable on deadlines") and the one you most need to avoid (for example, "defensive"), then pick evidence that supports the first.[^s1] Prefer concrete, verifiable facts over self-description: specific results let listeners judge competence for themselves.[^s4]

Avoid disguised boasts. When asked "What is your biggest weakness?", 77% of 122 students in one study humblebragged (for example, "I'm a perfectionist"), yet raters were more interested in hiring those who named a real weakness.[^s5] Across the same research, humblebraggers were liked less and judged less sincere than people who simply bragged or complained.[^s5]

A workplace before and after: a new team lead opens a kickoff with "I'm probably too obsessive about detail, ha." That is a humblebrag that invites eye-rolls.[^s5] A better version is "On my last project we shipped all four releases on schedule; I'll be honest that I tend to over-check, so tell me if I'm slowing you down." It offers a checkable result and a real, workable weakness.[^s4][^s5]

Keep it ethical. Selecting which true facts to stress is ordinary communication; fabricating credentials, faking agreement or intimidating colleagues crosses into deception or coercion.[^s2][^s3] Intimidation and supplication cast the actor in a negative light, and several tactics, including ingratiation and exemplification, are associated with perceptions of office politics.[^s3]

## Evidence

The core claim - people adjust their self-presentation to the audience and the stakes - is well established, but effects on outcomes are moderate and depend on the tactic and setting, so the evidence is best described as mixed.[^s1][^s4] A 2017 meta-analysis by Peck and Levashina found that self-focused tactics (such as self-promotion) related to interview ratings (corrected r = .24) and other-focused tactics (such as ingratiation) somewhat less (.17).[^s4] For job performance ratings the overall correlation was .24, driven by other-focused tactics (.25), with self-focused tactics not significant; only ten studies were available, so these estimates are tentative.[^s4] The authors also summarise an earlier meta-analysis by Barrick and colleagues (2009) in which impression management correlated more strongly with interview ratings (.47) than with later job performance ratings (.15), a warning that what impresses interviewers does not always predict performance.[^s4]

Two limits matter. First, most studies measure correlations with supervisors' or interviewers' ratings, not objective performance.[^s4] Second, research rarely separates honest from deceptive impression management; one study the meta-analysis cites found that most students admitted to at least slight image creation in interviews.[^s4] The humblebragging studies (Sezer, Gino and Norton) show a specific backfire: a tactic that tries to collect credit for competence and modesty at once is judged insincere, and in an economic game people gave humblebraggers less money than plain braggers.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Erving Goffman in **The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life**, which framed self-presentation as a theatrical performance.[^s2]
- **Studied in** social psychology, where Leary and Kowalski's two-component model organised the field.[^s1]
- **Applied in** the job interview, the setting where impression management is used most and affects ratings most.[^s4]
- **Supported by** Peck and Levashina's 2017 meta-analysis of interview and performance ratings.[^s4]
- **Demonstrated by** Sezer, Gino and Norton's humblebragging studies, which show how a self-presentation tactic backfires.[^s5]
- **Complements** face and facework: both describe how people protect and repair the image others hold of them, including saving face for others.[^s2]
- **Related** to executive presence, issue selling and managing up, which apply impression management to senior audiences, and to credibility, which honest self-presentation builds.[^s1][^s4]
