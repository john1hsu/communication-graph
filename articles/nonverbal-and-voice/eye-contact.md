# Gaze and eye contact

Eye contact is the moment two people look at each other's eyes, and the skill is using it, and looking away, so that the other person feels heard without feeling watched. Gaze helps regulate who speaks next, shows interest and carries status and emotional signals, so it shapes how a one-to-one, an interview or a meeting feels even when nobody mentions it.[^s6] The common workplace rule, "always hold eye contact to look confident and persuasive", is only partly supported by the research.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Eye behaviour is studied within nonverbal communication under the name oculesics, which covers gaze, blinking and pupil changes.[^s6] Textbook accounts describe stable conversational patterns: listeners look at speakers more than speakers look at listeners, speakers tend to hold gaze as they finish a turn, and the next speaker often glances away as they start.[^s6] Higher-status people are described as looking more while speaking and less while listening, the reverse of lower-status people, so gaze can quietly signal rank.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Eye contact appears to mark the moments when a conversation feels most engaging. In a 2021 study, 47 student pairs talked for ten minutes wearing eye-tracking glasses; when they later rewatched the conversation, they rated it as more engaging during moments of mutual eye contact.[^s3] Shared attention, measured by synchronised pupil changes, built up before eye contact, peaked as it began and then fell until the pair looked away, which the authors read as eye contact helping people step back and contribute independent ideas. They stress that the data are correlational.[^s3]

More eye contact can backfire when you are trying to change someone's mind. In two experiments in 2013, people who watched speakers argue on controversial issues shifted their attitudes less the more they looked at the speakers' eyes, and people told to watch the eyes of speakers who disagreed with them were persuaded less than those told to watch the mouth.[^s1] The authors suggest that pushing for more eye contact may be counterproductive when the listener already disagrees.[^s1]

## What good looks like

- **Moderate, not maximal.** In a study of nearly 500 Science Museum visitors from 56 countries watching clips of actors, the average preferred stretch of direct gaze was about three seconds, and most people preferred two to five; nobody preferred more than nine seconds.[^s4] People wanted shorter eye contact with actors they found more threatening.[^s4]
- **More while listening, less while thinking.** Looking at the speaker shows you are attending, while looking away during a hard explanation is normal: in a 26-person *Cognition* study, eye contact slowed people down on a word task only when the task was hardest, suggesting it draws on general mental resources.[^s5][^s6]
- **Matched to the person.** Finnish and Japanese students had similar bodily reactions to a live person's direct gaze, but Japanese students rated direct-gaze faces as angrier and less approachable than Finnish students did.[^s2] Textbook surveys add that sustained gaze can read as confrontational or disrespectful in some cultures and across some status or gender lines.[^s6]

## How to practise it

1. **Listen with your eyes.** In your next one-to-one, keep your gaze mostly on the speaker while they talk, and notice whether they say more.[^s6]
2. **Look in phrases, not stares.** Aim for natural glances of a few seconds, then let your gaze move; if you catch yourself fixing on someone for much longer, look down at your notes or the shared screen.[^s4]
3. **Look away to think.** When you are working through a complex answer, it is fine to look away, then come back to the person's eyes to land your main point.[^s5]
4. **Share it in groups.** In meetings, spread your gaze across the people you are addressing rather than only the most senior person, since gaze carries status signals.[^s6]
5. **Don't force it to persuade.** With a sceptical colleague, focus on the substance and their concerns instead of trying to hold their eyes.[^s1]
6. **Follow their lead across cultures.** If a counterpart looks away often, mirror a lower level of direct gaze rather than reading it as evasion or dislike.[^s2][^s6]

**Example.** *Before:* a project lead presenting a contested budget change to a peer group locks eyes with the most doubtful manager for every point, and the manager grows more resistant. *After:* the lead looks round the group, makes brief eye contact while asking the doubtful manager for their concern, then listens while watching them speak. *Why:* gaze is spread and paired with listening rather than used as pressure, which fits what the studies say about attention, discomfort and resistance.[^s1][^s3][^s6]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but scattered across small or artificial set-ups: pre-recorded actor clips, a laboratory shutter, video speakers and student samples.[^s1][^s2][^s4] Akechi and colleagues note that their study compared only one Western and one East Asian country and confounded culture with ethnicity.[^s2] Binetti's comfort window comes from pre-recorded clips rather than live talk.[^s4] The persuasion finding comes from one paper of two experiments; it challenges the folk rule but has not, as far as these sources show, been replicated at scale.[^s1] Treat the guidance as "moderate, responsive and culturally aware" rather than as a fixed number, and treat popular claims that averted gaze reveals lying with caution: the textbook calls the eye-direction lie rule a myth.[^s6]

## Connections

Eye contact is one channel of nonverbal communication alongside personal space (proxemics) and sits close to rapport.[^s6] Wohltjen and Wheatley's 2021 conversation study demonstrates its link to engagement in face-to-face conversation.[^s3] Chen and colleagues' 2013 experiments challenge the idea that more eye contact makes you more persuasive, while Binetti and colleagues' preferred-duration study supports moderate gaze.[^s1][^s4] Kajimura and Nomura's finding that eye contact taxes general mental resources helps explain why speakers look away when thinking, a cognitive-load account.[^s5] It complements active listening, since attentive gaze is how listeners show they are following.[^s6] Cultural differences connect it to intercultural communication and high- and low-context cultures, and its gap between folk rules and evidence echoes the Mehrabian myth.[^s2][^s6]
