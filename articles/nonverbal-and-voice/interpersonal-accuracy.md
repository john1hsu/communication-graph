# Interpersonal accuracy

Interpersonal accuracy is the ability to judge correctly what is going on in another person: the emotion behind a colleague's flat "fine", whether a client is losing interest, how confident a candidate really is, or who holds influence in a room.[^s2] Researchers measure it against a criterion, such as what the person actually felt or how they score on a personality test, rather than against how sure the perceiver feels.[^s1][^s5] It is the receiving half of nonverbal communication: most advice tells you how to send cues, while this skill is about reading them without over-reading them.[^s2]

## Origin

Psychologists have tried to measure and train the ability to read others since the 1920s and 1930s, when Floyd Allport and Arthur Jenness ran early training studies.[^s4] Over roughly six decades of later research, Judith Hall, Marianne Schmid Mast and colleagues built standard tests of judging emotions, traits and status from face, voice and body, and in 2016 gathered the field in an edited Cambridge volume on perceiving others accurately.[^s2][^s4] The field uses several overlapping names: interpersonal sensitivity, person perception accuracy and emotion recognition ability.[^s1][^s4]

## Why it matters

People who score higher on these tests tend to function better socially. A 2009 meta-analysis of 215 studies from 96 sources found significant average correlations for 27 of 40 categories of psychosocial variables, spanning personality, mental health and social and work-related competence, with higher sensitivity generally going with more adaptive functioning.[^s1] The likely mechanism is adaptability rather than any one behaviour: Schmid Mast and Hall's 2018 review found no single action that marks accurate perceivers, and argued that accurate reads let people adjust what they do to match what a partner expects.[^s2] At work that means pitching an explanation at the right level, slowing down when a direct report goes quiet, or noticing that a stakeholder's "sounds good" is not agreement.[^s2]

Power and status complicate it. A 2015 meta-analysis found that higher socioeconomic status went with better inference of cue meanings and that people placed in higher positions recalled more of what others said, while empathic, responsible dominance went with greater accuracy and egoistic, aggressive dominance with less.[^s6]

## What good looks like

Accurate perceivers treat a read as a hypothesis. Suppose a project lead notices a developer answer "should be fine" with a pause and a falling voice during stand-up. A weak reader either ignores it or announces "you're clearly unhappy". A skilled one checks it privately: "You sounded unsure about the date; what's worrying you?" and learns the API dependency is late.[^s2] Good reading also knows its limits. In a 2011 Swiss study, 51 professional recruiters judged applicants' overall personality profiles from short mock-interview clips more accurately than 80 students did, and spotted lies at 64% against the students' 47%.[^s5] Yet recruiters were not accurate about extraversion or conscientiousness, where students were, and recruiters' confidence in their own skill tracked only their lie detection.[^s5] Experience sharpens some reads and blunts others, so calibrated humility is part of the skill.[^s5]

## How to practise it

1. **Practise with feedback, not just exposure.** In four randomised studies with 531 participants, a short self-paced program covering 14 emotions in face, voice and body raised scores on an independent emotion test by a large margin, and the gain held four weeks later; the authors credit the mix of instruction, practice and right-or-wrong feedback.[^s3]
2. **Attend to voice and body as well as face.** The same program's gains transferred more to multichannel and voice tests than to a face-only test, a reminder that faces are only one channel.[^s3]
3. **Check, then act.** Turn reads into tentative questions or labels and let the other person correct you, which feeds your accuracy over time.[^s2]
4. **Watch your position.** When you are the senior person, make a point of listening for what others say and avoid self-focused dominance, which is linked to lower accuracy.[^s6]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is rated mixed. Accuracy tests correlate with many positive traits and can be trained, but Schmid Mast and Hall note that much of the research never measured real-life outcomes directly.[^s1][^s2] A meta-analysis summarised by Blanch-Hartigan, Andrzejewski and Hill found that whether training works depends on what is being judged and how it is taught, and they call for tests in real settings such as medicine, policing and customer service.[^s4] The 2017 training program did not help adults aged 59 to 90, and its effect on real behaviour is untested.[^s3] Lie detection is a weak spot: the students in the recruiter study did no better than chance, so this skill does not license claims to detect deception from body language.[^s5]

## Connections

Interpersonal accuracy sits under **nonverbal communication** (`cm:nonverbal-communication`) as its decoding side and is studied mainly in **social psychology** (`cm:social-psychology`).[^s1] It is supported by **Hall, Andrzejewski and Yopchick 2009** (`cm:hall-andrzejewski-yopchick-2009`), whose meta-analysis links it to adaptive functioning, and by **Hall, Schmid Mast and Latu 2015** (`cm:hall-schmid-mast-latu-2015`) on how power and dominance shape it.[^s1][^s6] **Schlegel et al. 2017** (`cm:schlegel-et-al-2017`) demonstrates that a short program can train emotion recognition.[^s3] Its benefits are explained by **behavioral adaptability** (`cm:behavioral-adaptability`), and it is applied in the **job interview** (`cm:job-interview`), where recruiters' first impressions are only partly accurate.[^s2][^s5] It draws on reading **facial expressions** and **paralanguage**, pairs with **labeling emotions**, **perspective-taking**, **empathy** and **active listening** as ways to check a read, and is distinct from the anti-pattern of **reading lies from body language**.[^s3][^s5]
