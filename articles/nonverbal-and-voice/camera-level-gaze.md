# Camera-level gaze on video calls

On a video call, you cannot look at someone's eyes and into the camera at the same time, because the lens sits above or beside their face on your screen. Camera-level gaze is the habit of arranging your camera, window and notes so that your natural gaze reads as eye contact to the other person, and of choosing when to look straight into the lens.[^s1][^s2] It is how the general skill of eye contact carries over to remote meetings, client calls and video interviews.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

The problem was studied by engineers long before remote work made it everyday. In a 2002 CHI paper, Stanford's Milton Chen reported that people are about ten times less sensitive to someone looking just below their eyes than to someone looking to the side or above, and that viewers tend to assume eye contact unless they are sure it is missing.[^s1] Chen argued that this asymmetry suggests design parameters for videoconferencing and built a simple prototype that supports eye contact from ordinary components.[^s1] Later work summarised by Holub and colleagues puts the angle at which people notice broken eye contact at roughly 3 to 10 degrees, depending on direction and viewing conditions.[^s4] The practitioner version, raise the camera and look into the lens, now appears in university interview coaching such as Wharton's on-camera workshop.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Where you seem to look shapes first impressions on video. In a 2022 study from the University of Gothenburg and Stanford, about 4,000 online raters judged 288 video-call screenshots; people looking into the camera were seen as more sociable and sympathetic, and a slightly high camera angle was also judged more positively, while distance from the camera made no difference.[^s3] Camera position also affects how calls feel: in a 2022 *IEEE Access* experiment with 33 people in three-person business-style calls, moving the camera sideways from the centre of the screen lowered how effective participants rated the conversation, by about a quarter of a point on a five-point scale at the widest offset.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Start with the hardware. Put the camera at eye level or a little above, sit about an arm's length away, and frame yourself from the chest up.[^s2] A low camera that looks up at you is a common mistake that coaches say can make you look arrogant.[^s2]

Then use the asymmetry Chen found. Because looking slightly below the lens is hard to notice while looking sideways is not, place the window of the person you are addressing directly under the camera, not on a second monitor off to one side.[^s1][^s4] A small 2024 vision-science study with four actors and 17 raters found that gaze about 2 degrees below the camera was judged the most convincing eye contact, slightly better than staring straight into the lens.[^s6]

Save direct lens gaze for the moments that count: your main point, a request, a question, or the close of an interview answer.[^s2][^s3] A sticker beside the lens helps, and notes placed just under the camera mean that when you look away, you look down rather than to the side.[^s2] While others speak, watch their face under the lens so you can still read reactions.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- **Speaking to the screen.** Talking to the faces on screen while the camera sits well above or to one side makes you look as if you are looking away; Wharton's guide calls this a common mistake.[^s2]
- **Notes and slides on a side monitor.** Sideways glances are the ones viewers notice most.[^s1][^s4]
- **Staring at the lens all the time.** Bailenson argues that video calls already create unnaturally constant, close-up gaze, and that looking into the camera to simulate eye contact is extra work on top of that, so constant lens-staring adds to fatigue and stops you seeing reactions.[^s5]
- **Sitting too close.** Large faces on screen mimic intimate distance, so sit back and frame yourself from the chest up.[^s5][^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence supports the direction of the advice rather than precise rules. The Gothenburg-Stanford finding comes from judgements of still screenshots, not live conversation.[^s3] The *IEEE Access* study was small, used one set of business scenarios and only reached significance for rated effectiveness, not engagement; its authors call the results not fully conclusive.[^s4] The 2-degree finding comes from a conference poster with 17 raters and has not, as far as these sources show, been published in full.[^s6] Chen's asymmetry is a classic laboratory result about perception, not a test of workplace outcomes.[^s1] Treat the technique as a low-cost way to remove a distraction, not as a persuasion trick, and remember that video calls already deliver far more direct gaze than in-person meetings do.[^s5]

## Connections

Camera-level gaze develops the skill of eye contact for remote settings, and it is a technique within video-call communication and nonverbal communication more broadly.[^s2][^s3] It is applied in video calls and video job interviews.[^s2][^s3] It is supported by the Gothenburg-Stanford screenshot study (Fauville and colleagues, 2022) and by Chen's 2002 work on asymmetric eye-contact sensitivity.[^s1][^s3] It sits next to Zoom fatigue and the camera-optional norm, because Bailenson's nonverbal-overload account explains why constant camera gaze is tiring and why a camera is not always needed; the effort of holding lens gaze is a cognitive-load cost.[^s5] Good camera gaze also supports rapport on remote calls by making you seem more sociable.[^s3]
