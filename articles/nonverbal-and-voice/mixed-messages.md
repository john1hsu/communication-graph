# Mixed messages

A mixed message is one where the verbal and nonverbal parts point in opposite directions: a colleague says she is not upset while her shoulders drop and her mouth turns down, or a manager says "great work" in a flat voice without looking up.[^s1] The listener now has two readings and has to choose one. Adults usually trust the nonverbal side, so the words the speaker chose so carefully are the part that gets discarded.[^s1][^s5] At work this turns reassurance into suspicion, praise into faint criticism and agreement into apparent resentment, without either person saying so.

## Origin

The research began with Albert Mehrabian's 1967 experiments on "inconsistent communications", which asked how people judge a speaker's feelings when the word, the tone and the face disagree. In the best-known study, 37 participants heard a single word, "maybe", in different tones and saw it paired with different faces.[^s4] Mehrabian later said his results concerned feelings and attitudes in inconsistent messages, not communication in general, but they were inflated into the claim that 93% of all meaning is nonverbal.[^s4] Developmental and cross-cultural studies since then have refined the picture: which cue wins depends on the listener's age, culture and what the message is about.[^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

When words and manner agree, listeners pay more attention to the words; when they disagree, people almost always side with the nonverbal message.[^s1] Congruent messages come across as more direct and honest and cause less confusion, so a speaker whose face contradicts an upbeat line ("I'm so happy for you") is believed less.[^s5] The cost is highest in exactly the conversations that matter: giving feedback, announcing change, accepting a decision you disagree with or reassuring a worried client. Because the listener resolves the conflict silently, the speaker rarely learns that their message was overruled.

## Evidence

The core effect for feelings is consistent. When Morton and Trehub (2001) played sentences with happy content in a sad voice (and the reverse), every one of the 20 adults judged the speaker's emotion by how she sounded, not what she said.[^s2] Four-year-olds did the opposite and went by the words; reliance on tone grew with age, and by about ten roughly half the children followed the voice.[^s2] Even the youngest children answered more slowly when cues conflicted, showing they noticed the clash even when they could not describe it.[^s2]

Culture moves the balance. In Stroop-style tasks, Americans found it harder to ignore the meaning of a word than its tone, while Japanese participants found tone harder to ignore; Tagalog-English bilinguals in the Philippines showed a tone bias in both languages, suggesting culture rather than language drives it.[^s3]

The popular generalisation does not hold. The 7-38-55 figures come from two small 1967 studies that never compared all three channels at once and used single words, so they say little about ordinary workplace talk, where content carries most of the meaning.[^s4] That is why the status here is `mixed`: listeners reliably weight tone when judging a speaker's feelings, but how much depends on age, culture and topic, and no study supports a universal percentage.[^s2][^s3][^s4]

## How to spot it

- **In yourself:** you are about to say "that's fine", "happy to" or "no concerns" about something you are not fine with. The gap is usually audible even when you think it is hidden.[^s1]
- **In others:** agreement delivered with a flat voice, a pause, crossed arms or no eye contact; praise that sounds rehearsed; "sure" that arrives a beat late.[^s1][^s5]
- **Across cultures:** a colleague from a culture that attends closely to tone may weigh how you sounded more than the words you chose.[^s3]

## How to fix it

**When you are the speaker**, decide which message is true, then make words and manner match it. If you have reservations, say them in words ("I'll back this, though I'm worried about the timeline") rather than letting your tone leak them; a stated reservation is easier to handle than a sensed one.[^s1][^s5] Before praise or reassurance that matters, ask whether you would be believed with the sound off.

**When you are the listener**, do not silently pick a side. Use a perception check: describe what you observed without judging it, offer two possible readings, and ask the other person to clarify, for example "You said the plan works, but you went quiet when I mentioned the deadline. Is it the timing, or something else?"[^s6] Offering two readings keeps it from sounding like an accusation and gives the other person an easy way to correct you.[^s6]

## Connections

Mixed messages are a problem within **nonverbal communication** (`cm:nonverbal-communication`), arising most often through **paralanguage** (`cm:paralanguage`) and **facial expressions** (`cm:facial-expressions`).[^s1] The adult preference for tone when judging feelings was **demonstrated by Morton and Trehub 2001** (`cm:morton-trehub-2001`),[^s2] and its cultural variation by **Ishii, Reyes and Kitayama 2003** (`cm:ishii-reyes-kitayama-2003`), which links to **high- and low-context cultures** (`cm:high-and-low-context-cultures`).[^s3] The topic is often **confused with Mehrabian's 7-38-55 rule** (`cm:mehrabian-myth`), which overgeneralises the original inconsistent-message studies.[^s4] Mixed messages **undermine credibility** (`cm:credibility`), because congruent speakers are judged more honest.[^s5] On the receiving side, **perception checking** (`cm:perception-checking`) and **checking understanding** (`cm:checking-understanding`) resolve the ambiguity out loud.[^s6] Speakers who think their true feelings are hidden are close kin to the **illusion of transparency** (`cm:illusion-of-transparency`), and in text the same problem shows up as **emoji and tone in text** (`cm:emoji-and-tone-in-text`).
