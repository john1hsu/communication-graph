  - Record a baseline of three to five minutes of a real upcoming talk and note the tool's readings for pitch variation, pace and fillers.

  - Pull out five to ten sentences from your own talk that carry the key points, and decide which word in each should stand out.
  - Repeat each sentence aloud while glancing at the live meter, aiming to push the reading past your baseline without the sentence sounding forced.

# Acoustic speech-analysis coaching

Acoustic speech-analysis coaching means rehearsing with software that measures your voice and feeds the numbers back: how much your pitch moves, how fast you talk, where you pause and how often you fill silence. Instead of relying on your own impression or a colleague's vague "you sound a bit flat", you see a reading, change something, and watch the reading move.[^s1] It is a practice method for the broader skill of vocal delivery, aimed above all at the flat, monotone presenter.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The approach grew out of computer-assisted pronunciation training in language teaching, where tools that display pitch traces or spectrograms have been studied since the late 1990s; a 2024 systematic review in *ReCALL* found 30 such studies published between 1999 and 2022.[^s3] Rebecca Hincks and Jens Edlund at KTH in Stockholm applied the idea to presentation skills in 2009, building a meter that lit up as a speaker's pitch variation rose above their own baseline.[^s1] They made the lights flicker and vanish, reasoning that speech is heard in the moment, so momentary feedback may be easier to absorb than a pitch graph that has to be studied afterwards.[^s1] Oliver Niebuhr and colleagues later used a scoring tool called Pascal to rate seven prosodic parameters in business presentations during charisma courses.[^s2] Mainstream presentation software now offers a version: PowerPoint's Speaker Coach flags monotone pitch, filler words and pace outside a band of roughly 100 to 165 words per minute.[^s5]

## Why it matters

People are poor judges of their own voices: a speaker who feels animated can sound flat, and recording playback alone does not tell you how much to change. In Hincks and Edlund's study of 14 engineering students presenting in English, those who trained with the pitch meter for about three hours over four weeks increased their pitch variation by more than 2.5 semitones relative to a control group who practised the same sentences with recordings only, and the gain held in a ten-minute presentation given afterwards.[^s1] In a before-after study of 72 course participants, Niebuhr's team found that acoustic charisma scores rose after both 12-week and 4-hour courses, with women starting lower and improving faster than men.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Start with a baseline recording of a real upcoming talk, then pick one parameter to work on; the tools report many numbers, and the published training targeted a single feature at a time.[^s1][^s5] Drill sentences taken from your own talk, as Hincks and Edlund did by extracting ten utterances from each student's baseline presentation and having them repeat each one 20 to 30 times against the meter.[^s1] Spread practice over short sessions across weeks, then deliver a full run-through and compare readings with the baseline.[^s1] Because automated feedback can misfire and is often hard to interpret, reviewers recommend pairing it with a teacher, coach or peer who listens to the result.[^s3] Speaker Coach's own documentation notes that it transcribes speech before scoring, so errors can creep in, and advises reviewing your recordings too.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing every metric.** Trying to fix pace, pitch and fillers in one session dilutes practice; train one parameter until it shifts.[^s1]
- **Over-correcting.** A higher number is not always better. In the KTH study, expert raters heard only slight gains in liveliness, and naturalness did not reliably improve, so check by ear that the change still sounds like you.[^s1]
- **Drilling only scripted text.** Nearly all studies use read-aloud or listen-and-repeat drills, and reviewers question whether such drilling transfers to spontaneous speech, so finish with an unscripted run.[^s3]
- **Trusting the tool's judgment on content.** Pitch meters cannot tell whether your emphasis lands on the right word; you still decide which word matters.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is promising but thin, hence *mixed*. Hincks and Edlund's result rests on seven trainees and seven controls, all Chinese engineering students, with a preliminary listener test from two raters who only moderately agreed; the authors stress that more pitch variation does not prove someone became a better presenter.[^s1] The Niebuhr study had no control group and measured acoustic scores, not audience reactions.[^s2] The 2024 review found that most computer-assisted pronunciation studies focus on individual sounds rather than prosody, use small samples and controlled speech, and report too little detail for pooled effect sizes.[^s3] A 2025 mini review adds that indirect displays such as pitch graphs usually need training to read, and that results vary.[^s4]

## Connections

This technique develops vocal delivery and targets monotone delivery, as shown in Hincks and Edlund's controlled study (`cm:hincks-edlund-2009`), which trained speakers for oral presentations.[^s1] It is weakly supported by Niebuhr, Tegtmeier and Schweisfurth's before-after training study.[^s2] It is a specialised form of presentation rehearsal and pairs naturally with self-recording playback, since the tools themselves advise listening back to recordings;[^s5] related delivery skills include thought groups, silence and pauses, and cutting filled pauses, and the broader idea behind all of them is paralanguage.[^s1][^s3]
