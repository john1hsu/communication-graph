# Facial expressions

Facial expressions are the movements of the face (a smile, a frown, raised eyebrows, a scowl, a tightened mouth) that people make and watch for during conversation. At work they do real jobs: they show that you are listening, soften or sharpen a message, and invite someone to keep talking. The popular belief behind much "body language" training is stronger than that: that each emotion has a signature face, so a skilled observer can read what a colleague feels. A large 2019 review concluded that this common view is not well supported: people do smile, frown and scowl more often than chance when happy, sad or angry, but not reliably or specifically enough to infer the feeling from the face alone.[^s1] The practical lesson is to treat a face as a cue to check, not a verdict.

## Origin

The modern idea of universal expressions traces mainly to the psychologist Paul Ekman. In cross-cultural studies published around 1970-1972, including fieldwork with an isolated, preliterate community in New Guinea, he asked people to show or recognise faces for situations such as a friend arriving or a child dying, and concluded that a small set of expressions (anger, contempt, disgust, enjoyment, fear, sadness and surprise) is universal.[^s4] To explain why expressions still look different across cultures, he coined "display rules": norms learned in childhood about when, how and to whom it is acceptable to show a feeling.[^s4] That framework fed decades of research, training courses and, more recently, automated "emotion recognition" software.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Faces are often the first and loudest signal in a meeting or a video call, and people act on them quickly: a manager who reads a flat face as boredom may cut a report short, and an employee who reads a frown as anger may stop raising a problem. If those readings are unreliable, decisions built on them inherit the error, which is why the 2019 review warned against using facial analysis for high-stakes judgements in hiring, education, clinical work and law enforcement.[^s1] Expressions also travel the other way: on a small video tile your own resting face may be read as disengaged, so deliberate, visible reactions help colleagues see that you are following.[^s5]

## Using it at work

Describe before you interpret: note "she frowned when I showed the timeline" rather than "she was annoyed", because the same facial movement appears with several emotions and with non-emotional states such as concentration or confusion.[^s1] Read the whole person and situation, since the body and the setting often carry more information than the face; when emotion is intense, the face alone can be misleading.[^s3] Then check with words, for example "I noticed a pause there; is the timeline a concern?", which turns a guess into a question the other person can answer.[^s1]

A brief example: in a project review, a colleague scowls while a new deadline is presented. Before: the presenter concludes the colleague is hostile, speeds up and avoids them afterwards. After: the presenter says, "You look like you're working something through; what are you seeing?", and learns the colleague is calculating whether the test environment will be ready. The scowl signalled concentration, not anger, which the review notes is a common reading error.[^s1]

On camera, sit about an arm's length from the screen with your head and shoulders visible, and make reactions such as nods, smiles and raised eyebrows slightly larger than in person so they register on a small tile.[^s5]

## Evidence

The strongest critique is the 2019 *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* review by Lisa Feldman Barrett and colleagues, which assessed six emotion categories against four tests: reliability, specificity, generalisability and validity.[^s1] It found, for instance, that the scowl-anger link sits in the weak range (very roughly 20-39% of instances), that the same configurations appear across several emotions, and that results vary across cultures, contexts and individuals, including in studies of remote small-scale societies.[^s1] A 2012 *Science* study by Hillel Aviezer, Yaacov Trope and Alexander Todorov found that at peak intensity, isolated faces of people winning or losing could not be told apart, whereas bodies could, and that pairing a face with a body shifted how the face itself was perceived.[^s3]

Evidence for some cross-cultural regularity remains. A 2021 *Nature* study by Alan Cowen, Dacher Keltner and colleagues used machine learning on roughly 6 million videos from 144 countries and found that 16 kinds of facial expression were tied to similar sets of social contexts, with about 70% of those associations preserved across 12 world regions, while how often each expression appeared varied by region.[^s2] Read together, the evidence supports a middle position: faces carry meaningful, partly shared social signals, but the mapping to a specific inner feeling is loose and context-dependent, so the status here is *contested* rather than settled either way.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Facial expressions are one channel of nonverbal communication, alongside eye contact, gestures and paralanguage (tone of voice); the review stresses that voice, posture, gaze and situation all shape what a face is taken to mean.[^s1] The universality account was introduced chiefly by Paul Ekman.[^s4] It is challenged by Barrett and colleagues' 2019 review, which questions inferring emotion from facial movements,[^s1] and by Aviezer, Trope and Todorov's 2012 study, which shows body cues outperforming faces at high intensity.[^s3] It draws partial support from Cowen, Keltner and colleagues' 2021 study, which finds expression-context links largely shared across regions.[^s2] The guidance is applied in video-call communication, where faces are small and resting expressions are easily misread.[^s5] Treating a face as a cue to check pairs naturally with labeling emotions and active listening, and the overclaiming popular version is a cousin of the Mehrabian 7-38-55 myth.[^s1]
