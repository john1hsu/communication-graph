# Paralanguage

Paralanguage, also called vocalics, is the part of nonverbal communication carried by the voice: pitch and intonation, volume, speaking rate, pauses, emphasis, accent and qualities such as resonance or breathiness.[^s1] The same sentence ("Sure, I can take that on") can sound willing, resigned or sarcastic depending only on these cues, which is why a voice can add meaning to the words or contradict them.[^s1]

## Origin

The linguist George L. Trager gave the field its influential first description in "Paralanguage: a first approximation" (1958), and the label spread through linguistics as a cover term for vocal effects that sit outside words and grammar.[^s6] David Crystal later argued that the category had been defined mainly by what linguistics left out, and that its functions are not only emotional: pitch and tempo also mark attitudes such as interest, sympathy, irony or seriousness, and help signal grammatical structure.[^s6] Communication textbooks now treat vocalics as one code of nonverbal communication alongside gesture, gaze, touch and space.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Listeners judge people partly by voice. In Schroeder and Epley's five experiments (Psychological Science, 2015), professional recruiters and other evaluators rated job candidates as more thoughtful and intelligent, and were more interested in hiring them, when they heard an elevator pitch than when they read the identical words; the effect held even when actors voiced the pitches.[^s3] The candidates themselves expected written pitches to do as well, so people underestimate what voice adds.[^s3]

Voice also affects persuasion. Van Zant and Berger (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2019) recorded speakers trying to persuade and had roughly 4,200 online listeners rate them; louder speech and more variation in volume made speakers seem more confident, and that perceived confidence shifted attitudes, while pitch and speaking rate did not reliably predict persuasion.[^s2] The effects were small (attitude differences of roughly d = 0.15 to 0.28), and listeners often noticed the attempt but were persuaded anyway, apparently because the speaker did not sound insincere.[^s2]

The voice also carries emotion. Kraus's five experiments with more than 1,800 US participants (American Psychologist, 2017) found people judged others' feelings slightly more accurately when they could only listen than when they could also see, and least accurately when a computer voice read the words.[^s4] A reanalysis by Rossiter (2018) argued the voice-only advantage is too small to matter in practice, so the safer conclusion is that voice is an important channel, not that it beats the face.[^s5]

## How to use it at work

Match the voice to the message. Textbook guidance is to check that tone, volume, rate and emphasis fit the words, because a flat "great job" or a clipped "fine" reads as the opposite of its content.[^s1] Slow down for complex or new material and vary your pace and emphasis rather than speaking in a monotone; a moderate, assertive voice (neither loud nor tentative) is rated more positively in conflict.[^s1] When you need to sound confident, research points to projecting steadily with some variation in loudness rather than raising pitch or speeding up.[^s2]

Choose a channel that carries voice when impressions or feelings matter. For job seekers and anyone pitching an idea, a call or a recorded message conveys competence that the same words in email may not.[^s3] For a sensitive one-to-one, listen closely to tone, pauses and hesitations, not only to faces, while remembering that single cues are ambiguous.[^s4][^s5]

For example, a team lead asks in a stand-up whether the release date still works. "Yes, should be okay," said quickly and evenly, means something different from the same words with a long pause and falling pitch. Noticing the second version and asking "What would make you more confident about the date?" turns a vocal cue into a real conversation.[^s1][^s6]

Treat vocal cues as hypotheses. Crystal notes that a tone's meaning depends on contrast with what came before and cannot be read in isolation, and misreading sarcasm or abruptness is a common problem across languages and cultures.[^s6] Accents and voice qualities also trigger listener biases that say more about the listener than the speaker.[^s1]

## Connections

Paralanguage is a part of nonverbal communication and is closely tied to vocal delivery and filled pauses in presenting.[^s1] George L. Trager introduced its influential first definition.[^s6] Schroeder and Epley's study demonstrates that hearing a voice raises judgments of intellect, which applies directly to the job interview.[^s3] Van Zant and Berger demonstrate how volume and confidence carry persuasion, linking it to credibility.[^s2] Kraus's experiments demonstrate the voice's role in reading emotion and apply to the phone call as a channel; Rossiter's comment limits how far that result should be pushed.[^s4][^s5] It is the vocal part of the Mehrabian myth's misused formula, a common source of mixed messages, and a reason channel selection and active listening matter.[^s1]
