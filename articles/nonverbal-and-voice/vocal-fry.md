# Vocal fry

Vocal fry, also called creaky voice or glottal fry, is a low-pitched, crackly voice quality, the "popping" sound many speakers drift into as their pitch and breath fall at the end of a sentence.[^s1][^s2] It is a normal part of speech: phoneticians describe it across many languages, where it can mark the end of a phrase or signal rapport, and voice clinicians call it a way of producing sound rather than a disorder.[^s4][^s6] For practitioners it matters because some listeners judge it harshly, while the evidence for how much and for whom is far shakier than the headlines suggested.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

The workplace debate traces to a 2014 *PLOS ONE* paper by Rindy Anderson, Casey Klofstad, William Mayew and Mohan Venkatachalam of Duke University and the University of Miami.[^s1][^s5] They described vocal fry as increasingly common among young American women and asked whether it helps or hurts their job prospects.[^s1] The study drew wide press coverage and turned a phonetics term into common career advice about how women should speak.[^s5][^s4] Linguists have since pushed back on both premises: a 2020 systematic review by Katherine Dallaston and Gerard Docherty found only ten usable prevalence studies, mostly small samples of American college students, and concluded that claims of a recent rise among young women lack empirical support.[^s2]

## Why it matters

In the 2014 experiment, about 800 American adults each heard seven young male or seven young female voices, alternating between a normal voice and fry.[^s5] Listeners rated the fry versions as less competent, educated, trustworthy, attractive and hireable, and the penalty was larger for female voices than for male ones, with women listeners the harshest judges.[^s1][^s5] If that holds in real hiring, a voice habit could cost a candidate on a phone screen or a presenter in a pitch for reasons unrelated to what they say.[^s1]

The practical lesson cuts two ways. As a speaker, fry usually appears when breath and energy run out at the end of long phrases, so it is a sign to support the voice rather than to change who you are.[^s6] A speech disorders expert at University of Utah Health suggests projecting the voice forward and raising pitch slightly instead of squeezing harder, and advises a clinical evaluation if any voice change lasts more than two weeks.[^s6] In a high-stakes interview or a large room, keeping enough breath to finish key sentences audibly helps listeners hear the final words, where fry tends to sit.[^s6][^s2] As a manager or interviewer, treat fry as a speech style, not evidence of low competence or effort: a 2022 letter in an American Speech-Language-Hearing Association journal argues that rating candidates on it amounts to penalising the groups who use it, and that a skill should be tested directly rather than inferred from casual speech.[^s4]

## Evidence

The 2014 study is a single online experiment with recorded voices, not a field study of hiring.[^s1][^s5] Its authors concluded that young women should avoid fry, and that advice is what spread.[^s1] Later work has not consistently backed the effect. In a 2022 University of Pennsylvania working paper, Katie Conner had 125 listeners rate five young women from the US Midland region; overall pitch and listeners' attitudes toward women in the workplace predicted competence ratings, but creak did not reproduce the earlier penalty.[^s3] Conner suggests that studying creak in isolation, without pitch, prosody and dialect, may explain why results disagree.[^s3]

Two further cautions apply. Dallaston and Docherty found that prevalence studies use inconsistent definitions and detection methods, so even basic claims about who uses fry most are poorly established.[^s2] And views on voice health differ: the University of Utah expert links habitual fry to fatigue and strain, while the 2022 letter says it found no studies showing vocal fold damage from fry itself.[^s6][^s4] The status is rated contested: some listeners do judge fry negatively, but the popular claim that it reliably costs women jobs outruns a thin and inconsistent evidence base, and the bias may sit more in listeners' attitudes than in the voice.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

Vocal fry is a voice quality and so part of **paralanguage** (`cm:paralanguage`), and it is studied in **sociolinguistics** (`cm:sociolinguistics`) and phonetics, where Dallaston and Docherty's review sits.[^s2] The listener penalty was shown by **Anderson et al. 2014** (`cm:anderson-et-al-2014`), and it is challenged by **Conner 2022** (`cm:conner-2022`), which did not replicate the creak effect, and by **Dallaston and Docherty 2020** (`cm:dallaston-docherty-2020`), which found no evidence for the claimed rise in use.[^s1][^s3][^s2] It is a close neighbour of **uptalk** (`cm:uptalk`), another end-of-phrase pitch pattern that attracts gendered criticism, and a part of **vocal delivery** (`cm:vocal-delivery`) alongside **filled pauses** (`cm:filled-pauses`).[^s4][^s6] Because listeners may read it as low energy, it bears on **credibility** (`cm:credibility`), **executive presence** (`cm:executive-presence`) and the **job interview** (`cm:job-interview`), the setting the 2014 study simulated.[^s1][^s5] **Self-recording playback** (`cm:self-recording-playback`) is the simplest way to hear whether your phrase endings fade into creak.[^s6]
