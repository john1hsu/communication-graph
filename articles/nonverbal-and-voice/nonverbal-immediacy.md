# Nonverbal immediacy

Nonverbal immediacy is the set of cues that make you seem approachable and present, such as sitting a little closer, nodding, smiling and speaking with an expressive rather than flat voice.[^s3] Communication researchers define immediacy as behaviour that increases physical or psychological closeness and reduces interpersonal distance, and they treat it as a relational signal rather than a single gesture.[^s3] At work it is the difference between a manager who turns from the screen to listen and one who keeps typing while saying "go ahead".[^s3]

## Origin

The term comes from the psychologist Albert Mehrabian, who first studied immediacy in language in a 1966 *Journal of Personality* paper and a run of papers with Morton Wiener, summarised in *Language within Language: Immediacy, a Channel in Verbal Communication* (1968).[^s1] He then extended the idea to body orientation, distance and other nonverbal cues in papers such as "Orientation behaviors and nonverbal attitude communication" (1967) and in the book *Silent Messages* (1971).[^s1] From the late 1970s, instructional and organizational communication researchers, notably Virginia Richmond and James McCroskey, turned immediacy into a measurable construct and tested it in classrooms and workplaces.[^s2][^s4]

## Why it matters

Immediacy tends to be returned. Richmond and McCroskey's study of supervisors and subordinates predicted that more nonverbal immediacy from supervisors would improve how subordinates saw them, raise satisfaction with the supervisor and with communication, and increase motivation and job satisfaction; all hypotheses were supported.[^s2] They concluded that immediacy from either side invites reciprocity and accommodation, which builds a more positive work climate.[^s2] A 2017 survey of 608 employees across roles and industries found that supervisors' nonverbal immediacy was linked to employees feeling more emotional support and needing to do less emotional work, and to employees seeking warmer, more relaxed contact with the supervisor.[^s3]

In teaching, where most of the research sits, a 2006 meta-analysis tested a causal model in which perceived teacher immediacy raises students' motivation, which in turn raises their mastery of the material; the pooled data fitted that model.[^s4] An earlier experiment that manipulated teachers' behaviour found that higher nonverbal immediacy improved both recall and affect, whereas adding verbal immediacy gave no extra cognitive benefit.[^s5]

## Putting it to work

The research points to a short list of behaviours you can adjust before a one-to-one, a hard conversation or a talk: nod and smile when it fits, sit closer rather than across a wide desk, and let your voice vary rather than stay flat.[^s3] Touch also appears on research lists of immediacy cues, but in most workplaces it is the cue to leave out. Before a meeting, decide which one or two of these you tend to drop under pressure and watch for that one. Because immediacy invites reciprocity, the first move usually has to come from the person with more power, typically the manager.[^s2] Use it to signal real attention, not to fake it: the evidence links immediacy with how supported people feel, which collapses if the warmth is not matched by follow-through.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is consistent but narrower than popular advice suggests. Most studies come from classrooms and rely on surveys of perceived immediacy, and the best-supported effect runs through motivation and liking rather than directly through performance.[^s4][^s5] The workplace findings are also mainly correlational surveys of supervisor-employee pairs, so warm supervisors may be rated warmly for other reasons too.[^s2][^s3] Culture matters less than often assumed for measurement: immediacy scales held up in the United States, China, Germany and Japan, though with some variation, so the behaviours that read as warm still need local calibration.[^s6] For these reasons the evidence status here is mixed rather than robust.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

Nonverbal immediacy is one relational function of [[nonverbal-communication]], built from cues covered in [[eye-contact]], [[facial-expressions]], [[proxemics]], [[gestures]] and [[paralanguage]].[^s3] It was introduced by [[albert-mehrabian]].[^s1] It should not be confused with the [[mehrabian-myth]], the separate and widely overstated claim about how much meaning words carry. Its reciprocal pattern in the workplace is demonstrated by [[richmond-mccroskey-2000]] and explained partly by [[communication-accommodation-theory]], which the study used to predict that people adjust toward each other's immediacy.[^s2] It is applied in [[one-on-one-meetings]], where supervisor immediacy shapes how supported employees feel.[^s3] Its learning effects are supported by [[allen-witt-wheeless-2006]] and the experiment [[witt-wheeless-2001]].[^s4][^s5] It is related to [[rapport]] and [[intercultural-communication]], since the cues that read as warm vary by culture.[^s6]
