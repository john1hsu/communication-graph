example:
  situation: A finance analyst reports quarterly results to a leadership team on a video call.
  before: "So revenue in Q3 was um four point two million which is up eight percent and costs were flat um so margin improved to eighteen percent and we expect Q4 to be similar."
  after: "Revenue in Q3 / was four point two million / [pause] up eight percent. / Costs were flat, / so margin improved / to eighteen percent. / [pause] For Q4, / we expect the same."

# Thought groups

Thought groups are short chunks of speech - a short sentence, a clause or a phrase - that a speaker delivers as one unit and separates from the next with audible markers.[^s1] Using them as a technique means deliberately planning where those chunks begin and end, then marking each boundary with a brief silent pause, a small fall in pitch and a slightly lengthened final stressed syllable.[^s1] The aim is to be listener friendly: to show by sound alone which words belong together.[^s1]

## Origin

The technique comes from English pronunciation teaching. Judy Gilbert, author of the Cambridge *Clear Speech* series, put the thought group at the base of her "prosody pyramid", with a focus word inside each group, a stressed syllable inside that word and a clear vowel at its peak.[^s1] Open course materials for international students and teaching assistants teach the same idea, framing pausing as a balance between pausing too often and too rarely.[^s4] The research behind it comes from studies of how pauses and intonation shape listeners' judgements and comprehension.[^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

Speech has no punctuation, and listeners cannot go back and reread, so they rely on prosody to see how words group into ideas.[^s1] When a speaker pauses too seldom, listeners must work out how the ideas connect on their own; when a speaker pauses too often, the speech breaks into illogical fragments and adds to listener effort.[^s4] In a study of 26 recorded TOEFL speaking samples rated by 188 U.S. undergraduates, measures of speech rate, pausing and intonation together explained about half the variation in comprehensibility and proficiency ratings; a cluster of boundary cues - more silent pauses and low, final-sounding tones - was a positive predictor, while frequent filled pauses such as "um" were a negative one.[^s2] The authors recommend pausing silently at the ends of idea units and not inside them.[^s2] An earlier experiment found that inserting half-second pauses at intonational and syntactic junctures in authentic radio news raised second-language listeners' comprehension scores, with the largest benefit for lower-level learners.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Before a high-stakes talk, take the passage that must land - the recommendation, the key numbers - and mark a slash after each meaningful chunk, keeping grammatical units such as prepositional and verb phrases intact.[^s1][^s4] Underline one focus word per chunk.[^s1] Read aloud, linking the words within each chunk, letting the pitch drop slightly at each boundary and pausing silently at every slash; use a bigger drop at the end of a whole point.[^s1] Punctuation is a useful first guide, since sentence ends and commas usually coincide with group boundaries.[^s4] Record a run-through and fix any pause that splits a phrase or that you filled with a filler.[^s2]

Numbers are the best place to start. Gilbert's classroom drills use telephone numbers and simple arithmetic, where the grouping changes the meaning, to make speakers feel how a pause and pitch drop carry structure.[^s1] At work, the same habit stops a string of figures - revenue, growth, margin - from blurring together, as in the finance example above.[^s2]

The technique matters most when listening is hard: on a poor video connection, with a large audience, or when some listeners work in a second language, the group in which pause insertion helped most.[^s3] It is a delivery skill, not a substitute for clear content; short sentences written in plain language are easier to chunk.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Pausing everywhere.** Over-chunking makes speech choppy and harder to follow, not easier.[^s4]
- **Pausing in the wrong place.** A pause inside a phrase ("in the / third quarter") breaks a unit that listeners need whole.[^s1][^s2]
- **Filling the gap.** Replacing the silent pause with "um" or "so" removes the boundary cue and is linked to lower comprehensibility ratings.[^s2]
- **Pausing with no pitch cue.** In faster speech the pause may shrink, so the pitch drop and final lengthening carry more of the signal.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is rated **mixed**. Studies support the parts of the technique - silent pauses at boundaries, falling tones and fewer fillers relate to better comprehensibility ratings, and pauses added at junctures improved comprehension - but they come from second-language speaking tests and listening tasks, not workplace presentations.[^s2][^s3] The rating study is correlational and its pause effects were small once other measures were included; the comprehension result comes from a single master's thesis.[^s2][^s3] The prosody-pyramid method itself is a teaching framework drawn from classroom practice, and its claim that rhythm and grouping matter more than individual sounds is the author's position rather than a tested result.[^s1]

## Connections

Thought groups are part of **paralanguage**, the vocal layer of a message.[^s1] The technique **develops** the skill of **silence and pauses**, giving speakers a concrete way to place short silent pauses where they help, and **addresses filled pauses** by replacing fillers at boundaries with silence.[^s2] It is **supported by Kang, Rubin and Pickering (2010)** on what listeners reward, and by **Van Loon (2000)** on pause insertion and comprehension.[^s2][^s3] It is **applied in** any **presentation**, especially one heavy with numbers.[^s1] It **complements presentation rehearsal** and self-recording, since marked scripts are something to rehearse and review aloud.[^s4] Shorter chunks also ease listeners' **cognitive load**, and they are easier to form from **plain language**.[^s3][^s4]
