# Silence and pauses

Silence and pauses is the skill of handling the gaps in spoken communication on purpose. It has two sides. As a speaker, you place short silent pauses to let a point land and take longer silences to think instead of answering on reflex. As a listener, you read other people's gaps as information: in ordinary talk a late reply often means hesitation or disagreement, not assent.[^s5][^s1] The skill is not "pause more"; it is knowing which kind of silence helps in which moment, and which kind hurts.[^s3]

## Origin

There is no single inventor. The skill draws on three research strands that each looked at unfilled time. Psycholinguists studying disfluency asked what listeners do with a silent gap mid-sentence; MacGregor, Corley and Donaldson's 2010 brain-recording study showed that silent pauses, like "er", change how the next word is processed.[^s4] Social psychologists at Groningen asked how a gap feels: Koudenburg, Postmes and Gordijn's 2011 flash report showed that a brief silence can register as rejection.[^s3] Negotiation researchers led by Jared Curhan at MIT asked what a long pause does to the person who takes it, publishing four studies in 2022.[^s1][^s2] Speaking clubs have long taught the deliberate pause as a delivery tool.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Conversation runs fast. A Dartmouth study of 322 stranger conversations reported a typical reply gap of roughly 200 milliseconds, and found that quicker replies went with feeling more connected; when listeners heard the same recorded exchange with its gaps lengthened, they judged the speakers less connected.[^s5] Because people expect smooth flow, a gap after your proposal is noticed, even if not consciously. In the Groningen experiments, a four-second silence in a video of three people talking left viewers feeling more rejected and less sure the group agreed, although they did not report noticing the pause.[^s3]

Yet the same silence that unsettles a social chat can improve thinking. In Curhan's studies, negotiators who paused for three seconds or more, naturally or on instruction, reached more value-creating deals; the pause shifted them into a deliberative mindset and loosened the belief that one side's gain must be the other's loss.[^s1] Breakthroughs were more likely to follow a silent pause than any other moment.[^s2] For presenters, a pause before or after a key word gives listeners processing time: words that followed silent pauses were better recognised in a later memory test.[^s4]

## What good looks like

- **Pauses mark structure in a talk.** The speaker stops briefly after the headline, before a key number and at the end of a section, and does not fill the gap with "um".[^s6][^s4]
- **Hard questions get a beat of thought.** Asked "Why should we pay more?", the speaker takes a few seconds, perhaps saying they want a moment to consider it, then answers the interest behind the question.[^s2][^s1]
- **Status is read before pausing.** Long strategic silence works for the party with standing in the room; in Curhan's data a lower-status candidate pausing with a higher-status recruiter gained nothing and felt worse.[^s1][^s2]
- **Others' silence is checked, not assumed.** After proposing a change in a meeting, a manager who hears a gap asks, "I'm hearing some hesitation - what's the concern?" instead of taking quiet as consent.[^s3][^s5]
- **Gaps after someone's risk are closed.** When a junior colleague raises a concern, the chair responds promptly, since silence there reads as rejection.[^s3]

A workplace before/after: in a budget review, a team lead asked "Can you do it for 20% less?" used to reply instantly, "We could maybe cut scope, I guess." After practising, she pauses, says "Let me think about that", and returns with a trade: a lower price for a longer contract term. The pause turned a concession into a joint-gain offer, the pattern Curhan's studies found.[^s1][^s2]

## How to practise it

1. **Rehearse with marked pauses.** Mark a slash in your notes after each key sentence and before each important figure; rehearse with a two-breath stop at every slash until it feels normal.[^s6]
2. **Replace fillers with silence.** When you notice an "um" coming, close your mouth and breathe instead; the pause is less costly for listeners than you fear.[^s6][^s4]
3. **Practise the thinking pause in low-stakes talks.** In your next routine negotiation or scoping call, deliberately hold three seconds of silence after the toughest question and use it to ask yourself what both sides could gain.[^s1][^s2]
4. **Name the pause when stakes or status make it risky.** Saying you need a moment signals thinking rather than stonewalling; this framing is Curhan's suggestion, not something his studies tested.[^s2]
5. **Log other people's gaps.** For a week, note after meetings where replies to your proposals came slowly, and follow up on each; you will learn which silences meant doubt.[^s5][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but rated **mixed**, because silence has opposite effects in different settings. Curhan's four studies are the strongest support for strategic pausing in negotiation, but their benefit vanished for lower-status parties and specific durations were not tested.[^s1][^s2] The Groningen results come from two small student samples (102 and 60 people), and the memory benefit of silent pauses comes from a laboratory listening task, so neither has been tested in large field replications.[^s3][^s4] The connection findings are from American undergraduates in friendly get-to-know-you talk; the authors note that gap norms may differ in arguments and across cultures.[^s5]

## Connections

Silence and pauses is part of **paralanguage** and **nonverbal communication**, the non-word parts of a message.[^s4] It is **explained by conversational turn-taking**, the fast reply norm that makes any gap meaningful.[^s5] It is **supported by Curhan et al. (2022)** and helps **address the mythical fixed pie** in negotiation, with salary talks a key **setting** where status matters.[^s1][^s2] It is **demonstrated by Koudenburg, Postmes and Gordijn (2011)**, which shows the social cost of a gap, and **supported by MacGregor, Corley and Donaldson (2010)** on listener memory.[^s3][^s4] In a **presentation**, deliberate pauses are the practitioner remedy for **filled pauses**.[^s6] **Wait time** is the questioning-specific use of this skill.[^s2]
