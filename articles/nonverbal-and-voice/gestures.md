# Gesturing while speaking

Gesturing while speaking means moving your hands and arms in time with what you say: tracing the shape of a process, showing that one number is bigger than another, or counting off three options on your fingers. Communication textbooks call these speech-linked movements illustrators and note that they are the most common kind of gesture, mostly unplanned, and produced even on the phone when nobody can see them.[^s6] The practical skill is letting your hands carry information your words do not, while cutting the nervous movements that distract.[^s1][^s6]

## Origin

Textbooks on nonverbal communication sort hand movements into three broad kinds. Illustrators accompany and depict speech; emblems are signs with an agreed meaning of their own, such as a thumbs-up or a rolling hand for "move on"; and adaptors are touching or fidgeting movements, such as twirling hair or playing with a phone, that tend to signal arousal or anxiety.[^s6] Because emblems get their meaning from shared convention, the same sign can mean something different, or something rude, in another culture.[^s6] Experimental psychology took up co-speech gesture in earnest from the 1990s, asking two questions: does gesture help listeners understand, and does it help speakers think?[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

Gestures help listeners. A 2011 meta-analysis of 63 samples comparing speech alone with speech plus gesture found a significant, moderate benefit to comprehension.[^s1] The benefit was larger when gestures depicted physical actions rather than abstract ideas, and when they added information the words left out rather than repeating them.[^s1] A larger 2019 meta-analysis of 83 samples again found a moderate benefit to comprehension, varying with the type of gesture, the information it carried and how understanding was measured.[^s2]

Gestures may also help the speaker, though here the evidence is more mixed. In a 2001 study, adults and children explaining how they solved maths problems remembered more letters or words from a separate memory task when they gestured than when they kept still, which the authors read as gesture lightening the mental load of explaining.[^s3] A 2020 study using verbal analogies instead found the reverse: people told to gesture recalled slightly fewer digits, suggesting the effect depends on the task and the gestures involved.[^s4]

## What good looks like

- **Gestures that add, not echo.** Showing the shape of a timeline or the relative size of two budgets gives listeners something the words alone do not, which is where the comprehension benefit is largest.[^s1][^s2]
- **Concrete over abstract.** Hands work best for actions, positions and sizes; a gesture for "strategic alignment" carries little.[^s1]
- **Few adaptors.** Fidgeting with a pen or laptop tends to read as anxiety, and speakers often only discover these habits on video.[^s6]
- **Emblems used with care.** In a mixed-culture room, avoid relying on signs such as the "OK" circle whose meaning is set by local convention.[^s6]

## How to practise it

1. **Find the visual in each point.** Before a talk or briefing, mark the two or three ideas that have a size, shape, sequence or contrast, and plan a simple movement for each.[^s1][^s2]
2. **Free your hands.** Put down the pen and clicker between slides so your hands are available, since gestures you suppress cannot help the listener.[^s2]
3. **Record and review.** Film a rehearsal and watch it with the sound off, noting fidgets and repeated self-touches to cut.[^s6]
4. **Let gesture support hard explanations.** When you are walking someone through a tricky process, let your hands move rather than holding still; it may ease the effort for some kinds of explanation.[^s3][^s4]
5. **Back it up for absent listeners.** If people are on audio only or will read the slides later, put the size, order or comparison into words or a diagram as well.[^s1]

**Example.** *Before:* an operations lead explains a new three-step approval flow to a regional team while gripping the lectern, saying "first it goes to finance, then legal, then the sponsor" in a flat list. *After:* she steps beside the slide, counts each stage on her fingers and moves her hand left to right along an imaginary line as each hand-off happens. *Why:* the gesture adds a picture of order and direction that the words leave implicit, the kind of non-redundant information that meta-analyses link to better understanding.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The listener-comprehension finding is the strongest part of the evidence, with two meta-analyses agreeing on a moderate benefit, though much of the research uses children, students and laboratory tasks, and children benefit more than adults.[^s1][^s2] The speaker-load finding has not held across tasks: the 2020 analogy study reversed the 2001 maths result, and its authors call their own small, single-university results preliminary.[^s3][^s4] Evidence that gestures make speakers more persuasive is thinner. A 2017 experiment with 116 students found a speech video with spontaneous gestures rated as more persuasive on most measures than the same audio over still images, mainly for less involved viewers, but the comparison also removed facial and body movement and the sample was almost entirely female.[^s5] Treat confident claims that particular gestures signal power or honesty with caution; the research supports gestures that clarify, not a body-language code.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

Gesturing is one channel of nonverbal communication and sits beside eye contact, and, as with the Mehrabian myth, it is worth separating what studies show from body-language folklore.[^s6] Hostetter's 2011 meta-analysis and Dargue, Sweller and Jones's 2019 meta-analysis demonstrate its benefit to listeners' comprehension.[^s1][^s2] Goldin-Meadow and colleagues explain part of its value through cognitive load, and Overoye and Wilson's 2020 analogy study challenges that account as a general rule.[^s3][^s4] Peters and Hoetjes framed their persuasion study with the elaboration likelihood model, finding gestures mattered most for viewers taking the peripheral route, which is why the skill is applied in presentations and links to presentation rehearsal.[^s5] Nervous adaptors overlap with other delivery habits such as filled pauses, and cultural differences in emblems tie it to intercultural communication.[^s6]
