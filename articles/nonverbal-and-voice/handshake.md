steps:
  - Watch the other person's hands and posture as you approach, and let them show whether they are offering a hand, a nod or a wave.
  - If a handshake is on offer, extend your right hand with the thumb up and slide in until the web between thumb and forefinger meets theirs, so palms make full contact.
  - Grip with about the same firmness they use, enough to feel solid but never enough to squeeze, and give two or three short shakes.
  - Look at their face, smile and say your name or a short greeting while you shake, then release and step back to a comfortable distance.
  - If they decline or hesitate, switch at once to a nod, a small wave or a hand on your chest, say something warm, and carry on without comment.

# Handshake

A handshake is the brief clasp and shake of right hands that opens or closes many professional encounters. It lasts a couple of seconds, but trained observers rate handshakes consistently, and those ratings line up with the first impressions people form.[^s2][^s3] At work it matters most in moments where an impression is being formed quickly: interviews, first client meetings, and the start and end of a negotiation.[^s1][^s4] It is also a greeting many people now skip for health, cultural or personal reasons, so the skill includes offering it well and letting it go gracefully.[^s6]

## Origin

The handshake's precise origin is unknown. One popular account says it began as a way of showing an empty, weapon-free hand, but that story is offered as a possibility rather than established history.[^s4] Systematic research began much later. In a 2000 study at the University of Alabama, William Chaplin and colleagues had four trained coders shake hands twice with each of 112 students and rate the grip, then compared the ratings with personality questionnaires.[^s2][^s3] The coders described a good handshake by its strength, vigour, duration, completeness of grip and accompanying eye contact.[^s3]

## Why it matters

In Chaplin's study, a firm handshake went with higher self-reported extraversion and emotional expressiveness and lower shyness and neuroticism, and handshake quality was linked to the coders' first impressions.[^s2] In a 2008 *Journal of Applied Psychology* study of 98 students in mock employment interviews, five raters scored each handshake; handshake quality was associated with interviewers' hiring recommendations even after accounting for appearance and dress, and it partly carried the link between extraversion and those recommendations.[^s1] Women's handshakes were rated lower on average, yet a firm handshake appeared, in exploratory analysis, to help women's interview ratings more than men's, a pattern Chaplin's team had also suggested.[^s1][^s3]

In negotiation, a Harvard Business School summary of research by Juliana Schroeder, Jane Risen, Francesca Gino and Michael Norton reported that pairs who shook hands before a mock deal reached more cooperative, mutually better agreements, and that the handshake seemed to work by signalling cooperative intent.[^s4] When a partner might be ill, the handshake lost its effect, and a partner who declined to shake out of concern about germs drew more cooperation, not less.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Treat the handshake as a short ritual you adapt to the other person rather than a test of strength. Read their cues first, then offer a full-palm grip matched in firmness, two or three shakes, eye contact and a spoken greeting, which together make up what observers rated as a good handshake.[^s3] When someone prefers not to touch, a nod, smile, small wave or slight bow can carry the same welcome, and being prepared, pleasant and conversational counts for more than the grip.[^s6] In negotiations, if no handshake happens, look for another early cooperative signal, such as a warm opening remark about shared goals.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Limp or fingertip grips.** These score poorly on the strength and completeness dimensions observers used.[^s3]
- **Crushing or pumping.** The research rewards a firm, complete grip, not dominance; matching the other person's pressure keeps it comfortable.[^s3]
- **Looking elsewhere.** Eye contact was part of how a good handshake was defined, so a grip while scanning the room loses most of its value.[^s3]
- **Making a decline awkward.** Joking about or judging someone who does not shake hands works against the cooperative signal the greeting is meant to send.[^s4][^s6]
- **Over-weighting it as an interviewer.** A handshake partly signals the candidate's extraversion, which is how it came to sway hiring recommendations in the mock-interview study, so it says little about most job skills.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but thin, so this article rates it as mixed. The interview and personality studies are correlational, used student samples of about 100, and were run in the United States.[^s1][^s2] The negotiation paper later came under scrutiny: a co-author audit found missing raw data for several studies and a repeated participant ID in Study 3, which led to a published correction in 2024, although a preregistered direct replication of Study 3 with 901 online participants reproduced the hypothesised results.[^s5] Norms also vary by culture and changed during the COVID-19 pandemic, when HR guidance began recommending touch-free greetings, so the "firm handshake wins the job" advice should not be applied rigidly.[^s6]

## Connections

The handshake is a form of nonverbal communication that relies on touch and depends on eye contact, which Chaplin's coders treated as part of a good grip.[^s3] It is applied in the job interview, where Stewart and colleagues linked it to hiring recommendations, and in negotiation, where Schroeder and colleagues linked it to cooperative deals.[^s1][^s4][^s5] It sits next to proxemics, gestures and rapport as part of how people manage closeness when they first meet, and alternatives such as bows fit settings shaped by high- and low-context cultures.[^s6]
