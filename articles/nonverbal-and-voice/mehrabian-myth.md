# Mehrabian's 7-38-55 rule

The "7-38-55 rule" says that when people talk, only 7% of the meaning comes from the words, 38% from the voice and 55% from the face, so "93% of communication is nonverbal". It is one of the most repeated numbers in presentation and leadership training. The figures do come from real research by psychologist Albert Mehrabian, but the research was about something much narrower: how listeners judge whether a speaker *likes* them when the words, the tone and the face send conflicting signals.[^s1][^s2] Read as a law about all communication, the rule is a myth; read as a finding about mixed emotional signals, it is a useful warning.

## Origin

Mehrabian combined two 1967 laboratory studies into a weighted formula, total liking = 7% verbal + 38% vocal + 55% facial, and presented it in his book *Silent Messages* (1971; revised 1981).[^s1] In one study listeners heard single words spoken in positive, neutral or negative tones; in the other, the single word "maybe" was paired with photographs of facial expressions.[^s2] As David Lapakko's review points out, the verbal channel was close to empty by design, the main study drew on 37 female psychology majors, and no study ever compared words, voice and face at the same time; the three percentages were stitched together afterwards.[^s2]

Mehrabian has repeatedly distanced himself from the popular version. His own site states that the equations came from experiments on communicating feelings and attitudes, and that they do not apply unless a speaker is talking about feelings or attitudes.[^s1] Lapakko also reports a 1995 exchange in which Mehrabian called it absurd to claim that words carry only 7% of every message.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The myth shapes real workplace advice. It is used to argue that slides and scripts barely matter, that delivery outweighs content, or that written channels such as email are nearly useless for conveying meaning.[^s2][^s4] Lapakko found the formula repeated in communication textbooks and widely online with little awareness of where it came from, and urged educators to correct it.[^s2] Even careful open textbooks still reproduce the 7/38/55 pie chart, while hedging that the exact percentages are uncertain and that words carry more of the load in information-focused settings such as a work briefing.[^s4] Believing the myth can lead a presenter to polish gestures while leaving an unclear argument untouched, or a manager to dismiss a well-written proposal because it "lacks the 93%".

## What the evidence actually supports

Later research supports a narrower, more useful claim: the weight of each channel depends on the situation, not on a fixed ratio. In a 1982 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* study of judging honesty, Zuckerman and colleagues noted that the face's apparent dominance in earlier work, including Mehrabian and Ferris, appeared when spoken content was filtered out or reduced to a single word. When full speech was present, adding the face told judges less, and adding verbal content substantially improved their accuracy at spotting deception.[^s3] Tone of voice carried more deception cues than the face, and the face conveyed liking better than tone did, so the authors concluded that no single channel always wins.[^s3] Communication scholars also argue that words, voice and gesture work together rather than competing for a fixed share of meaning; reviewers such as Hegstrom (1979) warned that formulas like 7-38-55 imply more precision than the research can give.[^s2][^s5]

What survives is the core of Mehrabian's actual finding: when words and nonverbal cues about feelings clearly contradict each other, listeners tend to trust the tone and the face.[^s1][^s4] Mixed messages of that kind can prompt people to look for more information, react negatively or withdraw, and repeated mixed messages erode credibility.[^s4]

## How to use the finding correctly

- **Quote it narrowly or not at all.** If you cite Mehrabian, say it concerns judgments of liking from inconsistent cues in single-word lab tasks, not all communication.[^s1][^s2]
- **Fix the content first.** For briefings, reports and decisions, the words carry most of the information; delivery cannot rescue an unclear message.[^s3][^s4]
- **Watch for mismatch when emotions are in play.** Praising someone in a flat voice while checking your phone, or saying "I'm fine with that" with crossed arms, invites people to believe the nonverbal signal.[^s1][^s4]
- **Choose channels on the task, not the percentage.** Text loses tone and face, which matters most for sensitive or emotional messages, not for routine information.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

The rule sits under nonverbal communication and is often cited in discussions of paralanguage and facial expressions.[^s4] It was introduced by Albert Mehrabian and popularised through his book *Silent Messages*.[^s1] Lapakko's 2007 review challenges how the formula is generalised, and Zuckerman and colleagues' 1982 study challenges the idea of a fixed channel hierarchy by showing that channel weight depends on what is being judged and whether verbal content is available.[^s2][^s3] The surviving point about conflicting cues links it to mixed messages, and the myth is relevant to channel selection, where it is sometimes used to overstate what text cannot do.[^s4] Like Hall's high- and low-context distinction, it is a widely taught idea about where meaning lives that has been simplified well beyond its evidence.[^s2]
