# Nonverbal communication

Nonverbal communication is making meaning with behaviour other than words: facial movement, gesture, posture, gaze, voice quality, touch, the space you keep and how you use time.[^s1] It works alongside what you say rather than against it, reinforcing, replacing or contradicting your words and steering who talks next.[^s1] At work it shapes first impressions, rapport and credibility, yet the popular promise that you can "read" a colleague's hidden feelings or lies from their body is far stronger than the evidence.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Textbook accounts treat nonverbal communication as mostly biologically based and older than language, whereas verbal language is mostly cultural; some nonverbal signals are shared across cultures, while no spoken language is.[^s1] Scholars sort it into codes: kinesics (gestures, posture, facial expression and eye behaviour), vocalics or paralanguage (pitch, volume, rate, fillers), haptics (touch), proxemics (space, including Edward T. Hall's 1968 four personal-distance zones) and chronemics (time).[^s1] The field now spans social psychology, communication and technology research, from facial expression and voice to emoji, synchrony and machine coding of behaviour.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Brief nonverbal impressions can predict real outcomes. In Ambady and Rosenthal's 1993 "thin slices" studies, strangers rated 13 college teachers from three silent 10-second clips; their combined impression correlated .76 with end-of-term student evaluations, and two- or five-second clips predicted almost as well.[^s3] Global impressions such as warmth and confidence predicted better than counts of single behaviours, and fidgeting went with lower ratings.[^s3] The samples were small and silent, so treat this as evidence that people form fast, consequential impressions, not that clips measure competence.[^s3]

Nonverbal cues also carry relational meaning. Immediacy behaviours such as smiling, nodding and attentive posture build rapport, and when channels conflict with words, listeners become uncertain and look for more information to decide which message to believe.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Reading lies from behaviour barely beats chance. A 2006 meta-analysis of 206 documents and 24,483 judges found average accuracy of about 54%, with truths spotted more often than lies; judges did better with audible than visible lies, and accuracy varied little from study to study.[^s2] A 2021 editorial on the field likewise notes that liars do not necessarily look more nervous and that relying on such cues can distort credibility judgements.[^s5]

Reading exact emotions from faces is also shakier than assumed. A 2019 review in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* found that people smile, scowl and frown in line with emotions more often than chance, but not reliably or specifically enough to infer a particular feeling, because expression varies with context, person and culture.[^s4] The authors still see facial movement as socially meaningful; their objection is to treating it as a readout of inner states.[^s4]

Decoding skill is partly trainable. In four studies with 531 adults, a roughly 35-minute course on face, voice and body cues with practice and feedback improved emotion recognition in young and middle-aged adults, with the gain still present four weeks later; it did not help older adults, and effects on real behaviour were untested.[^s6] Popular statistics also outrun the research: the textbook repeats a claim that 65% or more of meaning is nonverbal, a figure in the same family as the overstated Mehrabian rule.[^s1]

## Using it at work

1. **Read clusters, not single cues.** Treat crossed arms or a frown as a question to explore, not a verdict, since single behaviours take their meaning from context.[^s3][^s4]
2. **Check, don't diagnose.** If someone's face or tone seems at odds with their words, ask ("You went quiet when I mentioned the deadline. Is it workable?") rather than concluding they are upset or lying.[^s1][^s2]
3. **Listen to the voice.** When stakes are high, weight what you hear over what you see; in the deception research, audio carried more usable information than video alone.[^s2]
4. **Align your own channels.** Before a hard message, make your face, tone and posture match your words, because mismatched cues make listeners doubt you.[^s1]
5. **Use immediacy deliberately.** Smile, nod and face the speaker to signal attention, and cut fidgeting and vocal fillers in formal settings.[^s1][^s3]
6. **Adjust for culture and person.** Expect norms for touch, space, time and expression to vary, and take your lead from the other person.[^s1][^s4]

**Example.** *Before:* after a project review, a team lead tells their manager that a quiet analyst "obviously disagreed — she kept frowning and wouldn't look at me." *After:* the lead messages the analyst: "I noticed you were quiet in the review. Anything in the plan you'd change?" She replies that she was working through the numbers and spotted an error in one estimate. *Why:* a frown and averted gaze do not specify an emotion or intent, so checking turns a guess into information.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

Nonverbal communication is a broad part of interpersonal communication and is used most fully in face-to-face conversation; its codes have their own articles on gaze and eye contact, proxemics, paralanguage and facial expressions, and it underpins rapport.[^s1] Ambady and Rosenthal's thin-slice studies demonstrate how much brief nonverbal behaviour shapes impressions.[^s3] Bond and DePaulo's deception meta-analysis and Barrett and colleagues' review of facial expressions challenge the popular idea that body language reveals lies or exact feelings.[^s2][^s4] Research on it sits largely in social psychology.[^s5] Related articles cover the Mehrabian myth, mixed messages, filled pauses, and the screen versions of these cues in video calls, camera-level gaze and nonverbal overload.[^s1][^s5]
