# Reading lies from body language

Reading lies from body language is the habit of deciding that someone is being dishonest because of how they behave while talking - looking away, shifting in their seat, touching their face, hesitating - rather than because of what they say. It shows up in hiring interviews, internal investigations, negotiations, performance conversations and client disputes. Across decades of research, people who judge honesty this way are right only a little more often than a coin toss, and the cues they watch are weak or unrelated to lying.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The belief is folk wisdom, not the product of any single author. In two worldwide studies spanning 75 countries and 43 languages, the Global Deception Research Team found one dominant, pan-cultural stereotype: liars avert their gaze.[^s5] Popular body-language books and training courses repeat the idea, and the authors of a 2010 review for *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* note that even lie-detection professionals tend to overweight nonverbal cues and to be overconfident in their reads.[^s4] The field's large meta-analyses (DePaulo and colleagues in 2003, Bond and DePaulo in 2006, Hartwig and Bond in 2011) are what overturned the folk view.[^s2][^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

At work, a wrong read of dishonesty is expensive. A capable candidate who is nervous gets marked down; an employee in an investigation is treated as guilty because they avoided eye contact; a supplier is distrusted because they paused before quoting. Truth-tellers are especially exposed: anxiety about being disbelieved can produce exactly the "tells" observers look for, and the 2010 review identifies the assumption that liars are more nervous as a central misconception.[^s4] Gaze aversion, the most widely believed sign, is weakly related even to *perceived* deception and is not a valid indicator of actual deception.[^s3][^s5] Because people also feel confident in these judgments, the error rarely gets corrected.[^s4][^s6]

## How to spot it

- **You cite manner, not content.** Your reason for doubt is "she wouldn't look at me", "he was sweating" or "too many ums", not a fact that conflicts with their account.[^s3]
- **You are confident after one conversation.** Professionals in the research were no more accurate than laypeople, yet tended to be sure of themselves.[^s1][^s4]
- **You read nervousness as guilt.** High-stakes settings make honest people tense; the review literature treats "liars look nervous" as a myth, not a rule.[^s4]
- **You ignore the person's baseline.** You have no idea how this person behaves when telling the truth; neglecting such individual variation is one of the pitfalls the 2010 review names.[^s4]
- **You decide from video or appearance.** In the 2006 meta-analysis, people judged audible lies better than lies they could only see.[^s1]

## How to fix it

- **Shift attention from behaviour to information.** Verbal approaches outperform nonverbal ones; information-gathering interviews with open questions get people talking and let you compare their account with what you know.[^s4]
- **Ask specific, unexpected follow-ups.** Questions about concrete details a person is unlikely to have rehearsed - sequence, names, what someone else would say - are more diagnostic than watching their hands.[^s4]
- **Check facts, not faces.** Test one or two claims against documents, data or another person before forming a view, and keep "I'm not sure" as a legitimate conclusion.[^s1][^s4]
- **Discount your confidence.** Treat a gut sense of deceit as a prompt to ask better questions, not as a finding.[^s3][^s6]

## Evidence

Bond and DePaulo's 2006 meta-analysis of 206 documents and 24,483 judges found average lie-truth accuracy of about 54%, against 50% chance; people classified 61% of truths correctly but only 47% of lies, and expert judges did no better than non-experts.[^s1] DePaulo and colleagues' 2003 synthesis of 158 possible cues found that many behaviours had no or only weak links to deceit; the more reliable differences were that liars were somewhat less forthcoming, less compelling and more tense, with cues stronger when lies concerned transgressions.[^s2] Hartwig and Bond's 2011 lens-model meta-analysis concluded that judges mostly do use the cues that exist; accuracy is low because behavioural cues to lying are weak, so training people to watch "better" body language is unlikely to help much.[^s3] A 2019 *Annual Review of Psychology* paper called nonverbal deception cues "faint and unreliable" and noted that misbeliefs about them persist.[^s6] The status is `contested` in this graph's sense: the advice is widely repeated well beyond its evidence. The meta-analytic evidence against it is large and consistent; the alternatives the reviews favour, such as information-gathering interviews and unexpected questions, are promising but are offered as directions to develop rather than proven lie detectors.[^s4][^s3]

## Connections

Reading lies from body language is a misuse of **nonverbal communication** (`cm:nonverbal-communication`), which is real and informative about emotion and attitude but not a reliable honesty meter.[^s6] It is **challenged by Bond and DePaulo 2006** (`cm:bond-depaulo-2006`),[^s1] **Hartwig and Bond 2011** (`cm:hartwig-bond-2011`),[^s3] and **DePaulo et al. 2003** (`cm:depaulo-et-al-2003`),[^s2] and its pan-cultural reach is **demonstrated by the Global Deception Research Team's 2006 study** (`cm:global-deception-research-team-2006`).[^s5] It **contrasts with the information-gathering interview** (`cm:information-gathering-interview`), which relies on open questions and checking accounts against evidence.[^s4] In practice the fixes draw on **open-ended questions**, **follow-up questions** and **perception checking**, and the mistake is most costly in a **job interview**; related myths include the **Mehrabian 7-38-55 rule**, and related cues include **eye contact**, **facial expressions** and **paralanguage**.[^s4][^s5]
