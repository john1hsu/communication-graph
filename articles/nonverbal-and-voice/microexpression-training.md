steps:
  - Take a validated recognition course that shows brief expressions, explains the cues for each emotion and gives right-or-wrong feedback on every item, and record your pre-test score.
  - Practise in short sessions and retest after a couple of weeks, because the gains are measured over weeks, not years.
  - In real conversations, treat a fleeting expression as a prompt to pay attention, not as a verdict about what the person feels or whether they are honest.
  - Check the read out loud with a tentative, open question or label, and let the other person correct you.
  - Compare the read with voice, words and context before acting on it, and drop it if they do not line up.

# Microexpression training

Microexpression training is a short, usually computer-based course that teaches people to recognise facial expressions of emotion that flash by in well under half a second.[^s1] Learners see a neutral face, a brief expression and a neutral face again, name the emotion, and get immediate feedback plus instruction on the facial cues that distinguish, say, fear from surprise.[^s2] It is one of the few named, testable ways to build the decoding side of **interpersonal accuracy**, and also one of the most oversold.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

The best-known version, the Micro Expression Training Tool (METT), grew out of a research test of brief expression recognition and was released as METTv1 by Paul Ekman, David Matsumoto and Mark Frank in 2003, followed by METTv2 from Ekman and Matsumoto in 2007.[^s2] Matsumoto and Hwang later published the main evidence that such training works and lasts, and their study now sits within a longer tradition of training person perception that goes back to the 1920s.[^s1][^s6] Researchers have explored applications in medicine, law enforcement and customer service.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Many workplace moments turn on a reaction that is quickly hidden: a flicker of worry when a deadline is announced, or a brief frown at a pricing slide.[^s1] Training reliably improves the narrow skill of naming these expressions. In a 2014 pair of studies with 327 students and 127 airport behaviour-detection officers, sessions of 25 to 30 minutes followed pre-tests where accuracy averaged about 61% and 68%, and training improved recognition.[^s2] Officers who gained most were younger, less confident and had no prior facial training.[^s2] Matsumoto and Hwang's 2011 field study is the main link to work: department-store employees in South Korea were randomly assigned to training or comparison, and two weeks later colleagues rated the trained group higher on some social and communication skills, with gains retained in a second study two to three weeks after training.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Use the training to sharpen attention, then rely on conversation to find out what is going on.[^s1][^s4] Practise with feedback rather than just reading about faces, since practice with feedback was one of the conditions compared in the 2014 study.[^s2] At work, treat a noticed expression as a reason to ask, because the same expression can come from many feelings and many people show no visible expression of what they feel.[^s4] A short, open check such as "I'm not sure that lands well; what's your reaction?" turns a read into information the other person can confirm or correct.[^s1][^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Using it as a lie detector.** In a 2019 randomised test with 90 participants, METT training did no better than a bogus training or no training at spotting lies, and overall accuracy was slightly below chance.[^s3] Claims that it equips managers, interviewers or salespeople to catch deception are not supported.[^s3][^s4]
- **Hunting for something rare.** In a 2008 study of 697 coded expressions, true microexpressions made up only 2% and appeared in about 22% of participants, always in only the upper or lower face.[^s5] Longer, inconsistent emotional leakage was more common than the textbook flash.[^s5]
- **Reading faces in isolation.** Burgoon's 2018 review notes that liars and truth-tellers can feel many different emotions, that faces often do not show the emotion people report, and that smiles can mislead.[^s4]
- **Over-trusting the course.** Matsumoto and Hwang flag that workplace raters may not have been blind to who was trained and that only some ratings differed, so a certificate is not proof of better conversations.[^s1]

## Evidence

The evidence is rated contested. Gains on recognition tests are consistent across studies and last at least a few weeks, and Blanch-Hartigan and colleagues include this work among applied person-perception training studies.[^s1][^s2][^s6] But the only workplace outcome study is small, short-term and open to halo effects, and the strongest popular claim, that it helps detect lies, failed a direct randomised test.[^s1][^s3] Microexpressions are also rarer and less diagnostic than the popular picture suggests.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Microexpression training **develops** **interpersonal accuracy** (`cm:interpersonal-accuracy`) by improving emotion recognition, and is **demonstrated by** **Matsumoto & Hwang 2011** (`cm:matsumoto-hwang-2011`).[^s1] It was **introduced by** **Paul Ekman** (`cm:paul-ekman`) with colleagues as the METT.[^s2] It is **challenged by** **Jordan et al. 2019** (`cm:jordan-et-al-2019`), which found no lie-detection benefit, and by **Porter & ten Brinke 2008** (`cm:porter-ten-brinke-2008`), which found true microexpressions rare.[^s3][^s5] It is **studied in** **social psychology** (`cm:social-psychology`) as part of person-perception training.[^s6] It builds on knowing **facial expressions**, works best paired with **perception checking** and **labeling emotions** so reads get tested, and is easily misused as the anti-pattern of **reading lies from body language**.[^s1][^s4]
