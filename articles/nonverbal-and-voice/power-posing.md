# Power posing

Power posing is the idea that standing or sitting in an expansive, "high-power" posture - feet apart and hands on hips, or leaning back with hands behind the head - for a minute or two before a stressful event changes your body chemistry and makes you bolder. The original 2010 study reported that two such poses raised testosterone, lowered the stress hormone cortisol, increased feelings of power and made people more willing to gamble.[^s1] The hormonal and risk-taking results did not hold up in a much larger replication, and the study's first author later said she does not believe the effects are real.[^s2][^s3] What survives is a small, disputed effect on how powerful or confident people *say* they feel.[^s4][^s5]

## Origin

The phenomenon was introduced by Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy and Andy Yap in *Psychological Science* in 2010. Forty-two participants held two high-power or two low-power poses for one minute each; saliva samples taken about 17 minutes later showed testosterone rising and cortisol falling in the high-power group, and 86% of high-power posers took a gamble compared with 60% of low-power posers.[^s1] The authors framed the result as embodiment reaching physiology and behaviour, and suggested people could "fake it 'til they make it" before job interviews, public speaking or disagreeing with a boss.[^s1]
## Why it matters

The appeal is obvious for anyone facing a pitch, an interview or a tense meeting: a free, two-minute ritual that promises courage. But a technique taught on false premises costs credibility when people learn the evidence, and it can crowd out preparation methods that work better. The case is also a useful lesson in how one small, striking study can become workplace folklore: the original sample was small, and the first author later listed flexible analysis choices - checking results batch by batch, choosing between p-values, keeping the self-report items that "worked" - that made a false positive more likely.[^s3]

## Evidence

- **Hormones and risk did not replicate.** Ranehill and colleagues (2015) ran the same paradigm with 200 men and women, holding poses for three minutes. They found no significant effect on testosterone, cortisol, risk-taking in gains or losses, or willingness to compete; high-power posers did report feeling somewhat more powerful (d ≈ 0.34).[^s2]
- **The first author withdrew support.** In a statement on her Berkeley faculty page, Carney wrote that she does not believe power-pose effects are real, called the original sample tiny, listed the analytic flexibility and confounds in the 2010 study, and said she discourages others from studying it.[^s3]
- **Felt power: a smaller, contested effect.** Cuddy and colleagues argued in 2018 that a wider body of postural-feedback studies showed evidential value for effects on feelings of power. Credé (2019) replied that most of those studies compared expansive poses with *contractive* (slumped, closed) ones rather than a neutral posture, and that in the few studies with a proper baseline the effect came almost entirely from slumping making people feel worse, not from expansive poses making them feel better.[^s5]
- **2022 meta-analysis.** Körner, Röseler, Schütz and Bushman pooled about 130 experiments and nearly 10,000 participants in *Psychological Bulletin*. Upright or dominant postures were associated with more positive self-assessment, behavioural effects likely reflected study design, and there was no evidence that posing raises testosterone or other hormones. Most studies lacked a neutral control and nearly all came from Western, industrialised samples.[^s4]

So the evidence status is **failed replication** for the headline claims (hormones and risk-taking), with a modest, design-dependent effect on self-reported confidence that is better described as "don't slump" than "stand like a superhero".[^s4][^s5]

## What to do instead

Before a presentation, interview or difficult meeting, there is no harm in standing or sitting upright if it makes you feel steadier, but do not expect it to change your hormones or how others judge you.[^s4] Spend the time on things with better support: rehearse your opening, and reframe pre-talk nerves as excitement - in Brooks's experiments, people who said "I am excited" before a videotaped persuasive speech were rated more persuasive, competent and relaxed than those who said "I am calm".[^s6] In the room itself, open, relaxed posture still matters as ordinary nonverbal communication with your audience; the debunked part is the private two-minute pose as a physiological hack.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a claim about nonverbal communication - specifically about the effect of your own posture on you, rather than on observers.[^s1]
- **Introduced by / demonstrated by:** introduced by Dana Carney and Amy Cuddy in the Carney, Cuddy and Yap 2010 study, which reported the original hormonal and risk-taking effects.[^s1]
- **Applied in:** promoted for job interviews, public speaking and other evaluative situations.[^s1]
- **Explained by:** the proposed mechanism was embodied cognition - the body's posture feeding back into feelings and physiology.[^s1]
- **Challenged by:** Ranehill and colleagues' 2015 large replication, which found no hormonal or behavioural effect, and Credé's 2019 critique, which attributes the remaining self-report effect to contractive comparison poses.[^s2][^s5]
- **Supported by (weakly):** the Körner and colleagues 2022 meta-analysis supports only a self-perception effect of upright posture, not the hormonal claims.[^s4]
- **Related:** speaking anxiety and anxiety reappraisal (a better-supported pre-performance method), the Mehrabian myth (another overextended nonverbal claim), presentations and gestures.[^s6][^s3]
