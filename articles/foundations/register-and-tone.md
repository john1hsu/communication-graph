# Register and tone

*Register* is the variety of language you pick to suit a situation: the vocabulary, sentence shape, greetings and level of formality that fit a status report to a board, a chat thread with your team or a note to a new client. *Tone* is the attitude the reader hears in those choices - warm or cold, respectful or offhand, serious or playful. Linguists treat register as a property of situations of use rather than of individual people, so the same competent writer shifts register several times a day.[^s1][^s2] Formality is a scale, not a switch: between formal and casual sits a semi-formal register for readers you know but still owe deference to.[^s3]

## Origin

The modern concept comes from linguistics. Michael Halliday defined a register as a variety of language matched to a variety of situation, and described that situation in three terms: *field* (what is going on), *tenor* (who is taking part and how they relate) and *mode* (the part language plays, including the channel).[^s2] A 2009 Cambridge textbook by Douglas Biber and Susan Conrad sets out methods for analysing registers, comparing kinds of texts - spoken interpersonal registers, written registers and the newer registers of electronic communication - by their situation and their typical linguistic features.[^s1] The social side of shifting register was studied in communication accommodation theory, developed by Howard Giles and colleagues, which explains why speakers move their style toward or away from a partner's.[^s5]

## Why it matters

A message can be accurate and still fail because its register signals the wrong relationship. Accommodation research finds that listeners generally rate a speaker who converges toward their style as more attractive, cooperative and efficient, and that convergence can raise compliance; but over-accommodation, such as patronising simplification, is resented, and moving "down" to a lower-status listener's style can read as condescending.[^s5] Lower-status speakers are also expected to converge upward toward higher-status ones, which is why getting register wrong with a senior reader or client carries more risk than with a peer.[^s5]

In workplace email the pattern is visible at scale. A 2011 analysis of about 233,000 messages in the Enron email corpus, using a classifier trained on hand-labelled emails, found business emails were far less often informal than personal ones (21% vs 56%), emails to people four or more ranks above the sender were informal about half as often as emails four or more ranks below (16% vs 32%), messages containing requests were more formal, and informality rose with the amount of prior contact. The authors read this as broad support for the politeness model's factors of power, distance and imposition.[^s4]

Tone affects trust as well as warmth. In a 2016 Nielsen Norman Group test, 100 US adults rated near-identical web pages that differed only in tone: casual, conversational versions were usually rated friendlier, and in a bank example also more trustworthy, but a playful insurance page lost trust, and trustworthiness predicted willingness to recommend far more strongly than friendliness did.[^s6]

## How to choose your register

- Start from the reader and purpose, not your habit: an unfamiliar or senior reader, a high-stakes request or a formal document calls for the formal end of the scale; an established working relationship allows semi-formal.[^s3][^s4]
- Raise formality a notch when you are writing upward, making a request, or writing to several people at once - the conditions under which workplace email was most formal.[^s4]
- Converge toward the other person's level once you know it, but stop short of mimicry or of simplifying in a way that implies they cannot follow.[^s5]
- Keep the tone warm and plain even when the register is formal; formality is about deference and precision, not stiffness, and a serious subject is where playfulness costs most trust.[^s6]
- Re-check register when a thread changes channel or audience - a chat message forwarded to a client keeps the register it was written in.[^s2][^s4]

**Before:** "hey - can u send the cost numbers by EOD? need them for the deck, thx" (to a client's finance director the writer has never met).
**After:** "Hello - could you send the Q3 cost figures by 5 pm Thursday? We need them for the steering-committee pack. Thank you for your help."
The rewrite keeps the request short but signals the deference a new, senior, external reader expects; the original's shortcuts read as presumptuous to someone who does not know the writer.[^s3][^s4][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The Enron findings come from a single company and channel over about three and a half years, rely on classifier labels rather than hand coding for most messages, and include anomalies the authors could not explain; even trained annotators agreed on formality only about 44% of the time on a four-level scale, though 86% on formal versus informal.[^s4] The tone study used fictional sites and only US participants.[^s6] Accommodation effects depend on how listeners explain the shift: the same convergence can read as respect or as ingratiation.[^s5] Treat the guidance as well-supported direction, not a formula, and expect norms to differ by organisation, profession and culture.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Register is studied in linguistics, and the Hallidayan field-tenor-mode model shaped how it is defined.[^s1][^s2] Variation in formality is explained by politeness theory's power, distance and imposition factors, demonstrated in the Enron email study, and by communication accommodation theory's convergence and divergence.[^s4][^s5] It is applied constantly in email, where the channel strips out voice and the register of the words must carry the relationship.[^s4] It builds on audience analysis, sits close to pragmatics and facework, overlaps with the deliberate tone markers discussed under emoji and tone in text, and is related to, but not the same as, mirroring a partner's behaviour.[^s5]
