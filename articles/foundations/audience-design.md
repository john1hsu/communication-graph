# Audience design

Audience design is the theory that people do not simply say what they mean: they shape how they say it for the person in front of them. Speakers choose referring expressions, amount of detail and even accent and formality according to who they are addressing, and, more weakly, according to who else is listening.[^s1][^s3] It is the research idea behind the everyday advice to "know your audience", and it explains both why skilled communicators sound different to a new client and to a long-time teammate and why that tailoring so often fails.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

The term has two roots. In psycholinguistics, Herbert Clark and Gregory Murphy (1982) used it for the way speakers tailor referring expressions to a specific addressee, a view that later work on conversation treats as the classic contrast to egocentric accounts of language use.[^s3] In sociolinguistics, Allan Bell's 1984 article in *Language in Society* argued that a speaker's shifts in style are mainly a response to the audience.[^s1] Bell proposed that the style differences within one speaker mirror the differences between social groups, that addressees have the strongest effect, and that auditors and overhearers have a smaller but consistent one.[^s1] He extended the same account to which language or dialect bilingual speakers choose, and argued that topic and setting change style only indirectly, through the kinds of addressee they are associated with.[^s1]

Bell also separated two modes. Most shifts are *responsive*, prompted by a change in who is listening. In *initiative* shifts, which he called referee design, speakers diverge from their actual addressee towards an absent group they want to sound like, something he saw as especially common in mass media.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Workplace messages rarely have a single reader. A status update read by a teammate, copied to a director and forwarded to a client has an addressee, auditors and overhearers in exactly Bell's sense, and the theory predicts that writers will tune mainly to the addressee and underserve the rest.[^s1] It also predicts the most common failure in hand-offs: people shorten what they have already said, which helps someone who heard the first version and leaves a newcomer behind.[^s2]

Galati and Brennan's experiment shows the useful side. In 20 groups of three, a speaker retold the same cartoon story twice, once to a listener who had already heard it and once to one who had not. Retellings to the familiar listener were shorter and contained fewer events and less detail, and independent listeners understood identical phrases better when they had originally been said to the new listener.[^s2] The authors concluded that at least part of this shortening is done for the addressee, and that a simple "one-bit" judgement, whether this person has heard it before, may be enough to make audience design workable in real time.[^s2]

## Evidence

Support for the theory is real but bounded, so its status here is `mixed`. Partner-specific adaptation is well documented: in conversation corpora speakers hedge more with a new partner and return to established expressions with a familiar one, a pattern that simple priming or purely egocentric models struggle to explain.[^s3] Horton and Brennan argue, however, that what speakers know about a listener is partial, probabilistic and limited by ordinary memory, so adaptation is neither constant nor complete; memory-based accounts hold that cues about a partner often come to mind automatically rather than through deliberate perspective-taking.[^s3]

The strongest challenge comes from Boaz Keysar's egocentric account. Keysar argues that addressees tend to interpret from their own perspective, that speakers disambiguate with little regard for what listeners know, and that speakers overestimate how well they are understood, so miscommunication is systematic rather than random noise.[^s4] On this view audience design is a correction applied later, when there is time and a reason, not the default.[^s3][^s4] Most of the experimental evidence comes from laboratory referential and storytelling tasks with students, and Bell's account rests on sociolinguistic observation, so effects in high-pressure workplace exchanges are inferred rather than directly tested.[^s1][^s2]

## Putting it to work

Because adaptation is effortful and fragile, the theory argues for making it deliberate.[^s3][^s4] Before a briefing, ask the one-bit question for each person: has this listener heard the background or not? Give the newcomer the full version even when it feels repetitive to you.[^s2] Name the auditors who will read a copied email and check that they can follow it, because they get less of your attention by default.[^s1] For example, an engineer who tells a teammate only "the fix from Tuesday rolled back" can tell a newly added product manager what broke, what was tried on Tuesday and what happens next.[^s2] Finally, assume you were less clear than you think and check understanding, since speakers systematically overrate it.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Allan Bell, whose 1984 article set out the audience design framework for style, including addressees, auditors, overhearers and referee design.[^s1]
- **Explained by** common ground: tailoring depends on what speaker and listener believe they share, and on how reliably that belief is remembered.[^s3]
- **Supported by** Galati and Brennan (2010), which shows speakers attenuate retellings for familiar listeners and that this helps listeners.[^s2]
- **Challenged by** egocentric bias, Keysar's account that speakers and listeners default to their own perspective.[^s4]
- **Studied in** sociolinguistics, where it explains style shifting, and psycholinguistics, where it explains referring expressions.[^s1][^s3]
- **Related to** audience analysis (the planning technique it underpins), register and tone, communication accommodation theory, the curse of knowledge and the illusion of transparency, which all concern the gap between what a sender assumes and what a receiver has.[^s1][^s4]
