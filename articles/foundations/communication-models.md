# Communication models

A communication model is a simplified picture of a process that shows its key parts and how they connect.[^s1] Scholars in philosophy, linguistics, psychology, education and communication have each drawn their own, and introductory textbooks sort the main ones into three families: linear (or transmission) models, interactive models and transactional models.[^s1][^s2] No model reproduces a real conversation; even the most complete one is a deliberate simplification, so the practical value lies in what each model makes you notice.[^s2]

## Origin

Early communication research asked a sender-centred question. Writing in 1945, the political scientist Harold Lasswell framed the key question as "Who says what, to whom, with what effect?" and split it into control analysis (who owns and shapes a channel), content analysis (what is said), media analysis (the physical network) and effect analysis.[^s3] He also noted that people's responses feed back into the predispositions they bring to their next encounter, so his picture was less strictly one-way than later summaries suggest.[^s3]

The first formal model came from Bell Labs engineering: Shannon and Weaver's 1949 linear model, built to explain signals on telephone and broadcast lines and later borrowed for human talk.[^s1][^s4] A source encodes meaning into a message, sends it through a channel where noise can distort it, and a receiver decodes it.[^s1] Wilbur Schramm's 1954 interactive model added feedback from receiver to source and each party's field of experience, the beliefs, values and knowledge they bring.[^s1] Transactional models, from Watzlawick and colleagues (1967), Barnlund (1970) and Miller and Steinberg (1975), drop fixed senders and receivers: people send and receive at once and build meaning together inside social, relational and cultural contexts.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

Each family puts responsibility in a different place. The transmission view makes the sender responsible for a clear message, which fits text messages and email reasonably well but is too simple for face-to-face talk.[^s2] The interaction view treats a single message as only part of an exchange: some messages are never received and others are sent unintentionally, so effectiveness is judged across the whole loop of messages and feedback.[^s2] The transactional view adds that communication builds relationships, identities and shared realities, not just information.[^s2] Knowing which picture you are working from changes how you explain a misunderstanding: as a badly encoded message, a missing feedback loop, or a gap in shared context.

## How to use the models at work

- **Diagnose with the linear parts.** When a message misfires, check encoding (jargon, ambiguity), channel and noise. Textbooks separate environmental noise, such as a loud room, from semantic noise, where people do not share the same words or symbols.[^s2]
- **Build in feedback.** In meetings or presentations, watch nods, questions and silence and adjust, as the interactive model assumes; in a written channel where feedback is slow, ask explicitly for it.[^s2][^s4]
- **Mind fields of experience.** People with overlapping beliefs and knowledge understand each other more easily, so the further your audience's background is from yours, the more context you need to supply.[^s1]
- **Check the contexts.** Before a sensitive message, consider the setting's norms, your history with the person and the cultural identities in play, some of which you cannot see.[^s2]
- **Match the model to the channel.** A broadcast email to a large list behaves much like the linear model; a tense one-to-one behaves like the transactional one, so plan the first and stay responsive in the second.[^s2][^s4]

## Limits

Communication models are conceptual frameworks rather than tested predictions, so their status here is theoretical.[^s1] Gasiorek and Aune argue that linear and interactive models rest on a code model, which assumes stable pairings between signals and meanings and therefore struggles with metaphor, indirect requests such as "it's cold in here", ambiguous gestures and people with no shared language.[^s1] They call the code model incomplete rather than wrong, and say transactional models describe relational outcomes but take mutual understanding as given instead of explaining how people reach it, which is why they turn to an inferential account.[^s1] Popular textbook diagrams also mix elements: some versions of the transactional model add worldviews and surrounding noise that Barnlund's original did not include, so a summary may differ from its source.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** communication studies, where the three-model sequence is the standard introduction; the models are **studied in** psychology and other disciplines too.[^s1][^s2]
- **Influenced by** Harold Lasswell, whose who-says-what-to-whom question set the early sender-centred agenda.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with** the inferential model of communication, which Gasiorek and Aune offer to explain how understanding is actually created where code-based models fall short.[^s1]
- **Applied in** email and other text channels, which the transmission model describes better than face-to-face talk.[^s2]
- **Related to** the Shannon-Weaver model, the interaction model and the transactional model (its members); feedback and checking understanding (the loop the later models add); common ground and audience analysis (fields of experience); and channel selection.[^s1][^s2]
