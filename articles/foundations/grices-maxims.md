# Grice's cooperative principle

Grice's cooperative principle says that people in a conversation expect each contribution to serve the accepted purpose of the exchange at that point. Grice broke that expectation into four groups of maxims: **Quality** (say what you believe true and can back up), **Quantity** (be as informative as the purpose requires, and no more), **Relation** (be relevant) and **Manner** (be perspicuous: avoid obscurity and ambiguity, be brief and orderly).[^s1][^s2] The practical payoff is not the list itself but what it explains: listeners use these expectations to infer meaning a speaker never states, which Grice called *implicature*.[^s2]

## Origin

H. Paul Grice (1913-1988), an Oxford philosopher who moved to the University of California, Berkeley in 1967, set out the theory in his William James Lectures at Harvard that year. The lectures circulated in manuscript for years; the central lecture was published as "Logic and Conversation" in 1975 and collected in *Studies in the Way of Words* (1989).[^s1] Grice coined the term implicature, and his framework became a founding idea of pragmatics, the study of meaning in context.[^s2] He presented the maxims as precepts that rational people sharing a goal would follow, not as social conventions or etiquette.[^s1]

## Why it matters

At work, most misreadings are not about what was said but about what was implied. Because listeners assume you are being relevant, a reply that seems off-topic gets interpreted: if a colleague says she is low on fuel and you mention a station around the corner, she will infer you think it is open.[^s1] Because listeners assume you are as informative as needed, a weaker word implies the stronger one is false: "some of the tests passed" suggests not all did.[^s2] These inferences are cancellable, so you can block one by adding "in fact, all of them passed", but only if you notice it first.[^s2] The same mechanism explains polite hints, irony and the faint-praise reference letter: a speaker flouts a maxim so obviously that the listener has to look for another meaning.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Use the maxims as a pre-send check rather than a style rule.[^s1][^s2]

- **Quality:** separate what you know from what you believe, and say which is which. Grice himself noted the maxims often conflict; when you lack evidence for a fuller answer, say less and flag the gap rather than fill it with a guess.[^s1]
- **Quantity:** decide what this reader needs for the decision in front of them. Under-telling is the bigger risk: in Engelhardt and colleagues' experiments, listeners rated under-specified instructions as poor, while extra detail was tolerated.[^s3]
- **Relation:** answer the question asked before adding context. A status update that opens with background invites the reader to infer you are avoiding the answer.[^s2]
- **Manner:** remove ambiguous terms and order points as the reader will use them.[^s1]
- **Scan for unintended implicatures:** words such as "some", "most", "should be fine" or "I think" all imply a stronger claim is not available. If it is, use it.[^s2]

For example, a manager asks whether a vendor migration is on track. "Most of the cutover tasks are done" implies some are late and invites alarm. "All cutover tasks are done; the data check finishes Thursday, which is still within plan" answers the question, states what is known and cancels the worrying inference.[^s2][^s3]

## Common misreadings

The most common error is treating the principle as a rule to be nice, agreeable or maximally explicit. Bethan Davies calls this "cooperation drift": Grice's cooperation is the minimal, rational kind that lets a listener work out meaning, and a curt or evasive reply can still follow it.[^s4] Davies also notes that the theory says little about politeness or efficiency, so it does not tell you how warm or how short to be.[^s4] A second misreading is that Grice prescribed the maxims as etiquette; he described the expectations listeners bring, which is why flouting them communicates.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The maxims are a theoretical model, and the empirical record on them is mixed. In a 2006 set of three experiments, speakers over-described objects in roughly a third of utterances where a shorter phrase would do, listeners did not rate the over-descriptions as worse, yet eye-tracking showed the extra words delayed listeners by about 300 ms; the authors concluded language use is only moderately Gricean.[^s3] Relevance theory (Sperber and Wilson) argues the four maxims reduce to a single principle of relevance, and neo-Gricean accounts by Horn and Levinson restructure them; critics of all versions say they overgenerate and do not predict which of several implicatures a listener will draw.[^s2] Cultural universality is disputed too. Keenan (1976) reported that Malagasy speakers routinely give less information than their partners need; Ellen Prince (1982) replied that the speakers were exploiting the Quantity maxim, which still exists, rather than lacking it.[^s5] The working lesson: what counts as "enough" and "relevant" varies by culture and setting, even if the inference mechanism is shared.[^s5]

## Connections

Grice introduced the principle in "Logic and Conversation", and it is a core topic of pragmatics.[^s1][^s2] It is challenged by Engelhardt, Bailey and Ferreira's tests of the Quantity maxim, by relevance theory's competing account and by Keenan's cross-cultural data.[^s2][^s3][^s5] Practical writing norms such as plain language and bottom line up front can be read as applications of Quantity, Relation and Manner; the principle depends on audience analysis, because "as informative as required" is defined by the listener's purpose; and it sits alongside politeness theory and high- and low-context cultures, which explain why people often depart from the maxims on purpose.[^s1][^s4][^s5]
