# Social psychology

Social psychology studies how people affect one another: how the real or implied presence of others, and membership of groups, shapes what we think, feel and do.[^s1] Its topics run from the intrapersonal (attitudes, emotions, the self) to the interpersonal (helping, prejudice, attraction and group processes).[^s1] Britannica describes it as the scientific study of individual behaviour in its social and cultural setting, with long-running work on social perception, nonverbal communication, language and the design of mass communication.[^s2] For anyone trying to communicate better at work, it is the field that supplies much of the experimental evidence behind advice about credibility, persuasion and how we misread one another.[^s2][^s6]

## Origin

Textbooks often call Norman Triplett's 1898 study of children competing on a reeling task the first social-psychology experiment. Wolfgang Stroebe's 2012 review in *Perspectives on Psychological Science* showed that this story is wrong in several ways.[^s3] Earlier studies of social influence already existed, including Ringelmann's 1880s work on group effort. The task is usually misdescribed, and a 2005 reanalysis found Triplett's effects were not statistically significant by modern standards.[^s3] Stroebe dates the field's modern identity to 1924, when Floyd Allport defined it as the experimental study of social behaviour.[^s3] Britannica notes that early social psychology was largely speculative and became empirical after about 1925, led by US research.[^s2]

For communication, the decisive period came during and after the Second World War. Carl Hovland ran the US Army's experiments on training films, published in 1949 in the series *Studies in Social Psychology in World War II*.[^s6] He then founded the Yale Communication and Attitude Change Program, which tested how source credibility, order of presentation, one- versus two-sided messages, explicit conclusions and fear appeals change opinions.[^s6] Later scholars ranked it as the largest single force behind psychology's research on persuasion.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Three ideas from the field change how you handle everyday workplace messages.

- **Situations drive behaviour more than we assume.** Social psychologists lean toward situationism: much of what people do is shaped by the circumstances around them, not only by personality.[^s1] The fundamental attribution error is our habit of explaining others' behaviour by their traits while underweighting their situation.[^s1] Suppose a colleague sends a two-word reply to your carefully drafted proposal. The attribution habit says "dismissive"; the situational question is "what else landed on their desk today?" Ask or wait before you reply in kind.[^s1]
- **We excuse ourselves and judge others.** The actor-observer bias explains our own lapses by circumstance and other people's by character, because we know our own context and not theirs.[^s1] The self-serving bias credits our successes to skill and our failures to circumstance.[^s1] Both distort project post-mortems and feedback conversations unless someone deliberately asks about situational causes.[^s1]
- **Culture moderates these habits.** People from individualist cultures commit the fundamental attribution error more often. People from collectivist cultures tend to give more context-aware explanations, which matters when interpreting colleagues across a global team.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The field's findings are not equally solid. In 2015 the Open Science Collaboration repeated 100 studies from three psychology journals. Only 36% of replications produced significant results, against 97% of the originals, and replication effects averaged about half the original size.[^s4] Many Labs 2 (2018) ran 28 classic and newer findings across 125 samples in 36 countries. About half replicated, with the median effect size falling from 0.60 to 0.15.[^s5] Variation depended more on the effect than on the sample or culture, so a finding that works tends to work broadly. A finding that fails tends to fail everywhere.[^s5] The practical rule is to check whether a social-psychology result behind a piece of communication advice has survived large replications before you build a habit on it.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Floyd Allport,** whose 1924 definition of the field as the experimental study of social behaviour marks its modern identity.[^s3]
- **Influenced by Carl Hovland,** whose wartime and Yale programmes made persuasion and source credibility core topics.[^s6]
- **Challenged by the Open Science Collaboration (2015)** and **by Many Labs 2 (2018),** large replication projects that found many published effects smaller or absent.[^s4][^s5]
- It sits under psychology and overlaps interpersonal and organizational communication. Persuasion, the elaboration likelihood model, nonverbal communication and egocentric biases in this graph are all studied in it.[^s1][^s2][^s6]
