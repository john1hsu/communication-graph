# Rapport

Rapport is the sense that a conversation is "clicking": both people are tuned in to each other, feel friendly toward each other, and move through the exchange smoothly. In the most-cited account it exists only inside an interaction. It is not a personality trait, although some people are better at creating it.[^s1] Researchers in negotiation describe it as a state of mutual positivity and interest that grows as two people's expressive behaviour falls into rhythm.[^s2] At work, rapport is what makes a colleague willing to tell you the real problem, a client willing to say what worries them, and a counterpart willing to look for a deal that works for both sides.

## Origin

Psychologists Linda Tickle-Degnen and Robert Rosenthal gave the concept its standard structure in 1990. They split rapport into three parts: **mutual attentiveness** (both people focused on and involved with each other), **positivity** (mutual warmth and friendliness) and **coordination** (balance and harmony, being "in sync").[^s1] The parts can vary on their own; you can be attentive to someone you feel cool toward. Their weight also shifts over time. In first meetings, positivity and attentiveness count most because people are still sizing each other up and following politeness norms. In longer relationships, coordination matters more.[^s1] They linked each part to observable cues. Orientation and posture signal attention. Smiling, nodding, forward lean and facing the other person signal positivity. Postural mirroring and interactional synchrony signal coordination.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Rapport has measurable effects on cooperation. In Drolet and Morris's experiments, negotiators who could see each other settled a simulated strike sooner than pairs who stood side by side without visual contact. Strangers who chatted face to face before a prisoner's-dilemma game reached mutual cooperation far more often than pairs who talked by phone (64% vs. 20%). Measured rapport statistically accounted for that difference, while individual good mood did not.[^s2] Rapport also explains why email negotiations break down. Moore and colleagues found that email negotiators who had neither a shared group identity nor any personal exchange were more likely to reach impasse. Shared membership and mutual self-disclosure each improved the process and the rapport between the parties.[^s3] In a follow-up, a short non-business phone call before an email negotiation led pairs to plan for cooperation and to reach better economic and social outcomes.[^s4]

The concept also matters well beyond negotiation. A 2021 systematic review of 35 studies on interviews where the goal is to gather information found that 32 reported full or partial support for rapport increasing what people disclose.[^s5]

## What works here

Rapport is built mostly through what you say and how you listen, not through body-language tricks. In the 2021 review, the most common rapport behaviours were active listening (66% of studies), showing personal interest and reciprocity (51%), self-disclosure (40%) and empathic responses (37%). Smiling, eye contact and nodding each appeared in only about a tenth of studies.[^s5] In practice:

- **Give full attention.** Face the person, put the screen away and follow up on what they actually said. This is the attentiveness part of rapport.[^s1]
- **Exchange something personal.** One genuine detail each, such as where you are based or what else is on your plate this week, before the agenda.[^s3]
- **Talk live before you work in writing.** If a tense or high-stakes exchange will happen by email, have a short call or meeting first.[^s4][^s2]
- **Read the room.** Whether small talk helps depends on the setting and the person. An offer of coffee or a relaxed seat invites conversation. Being shown straight to the desk signals they want to start. Look for shared ground later if the opening is brisk.[^s6]

*Example.* Before: a project lead's first contact with a new vendor contact is a long email listing missed deadlines. The reply is defensive and the thread hardens. After: the lead books 15 minutes, asks how the vendor's team is set up, mentions their own team's crunch, and only then raises the deadlines. The follow-up email lands as a joint problem rather than an accusation. Why: a brief live, personal exchange builds the attentiveness and positivity that email strips out.[^s4][^s3]

## Pitfalls

Rapport-building that feels scripted can backfire. Personal comments that the setting does not invite, such as remarking on family photos in a formal meeting, can land badly, and norms around small talk vary by industry and culture.[^s6] Copying someone's posture is a sign of coordination that develops naturally, not a lever. The original account treats mirroring as something that emerges once people are in sync.[^s1] Rapport is also not agreement. Its value is that it keeps hard conversations cooperative, not that it avoids them.[^s2]

## Evidence

The core findings are consistent, but the evidence base has limits, so `evidenceStatus` is *mixed*. Lab experiments show rapport mediating cooperation,[^s2] and the information-gathering literature is largely positive.[^s5] However, the 2021 review found that rapport is defined and measured very inconsistently: almost no two research teams used the same measure. It also found that whether rapport acts directly or indirectly is often unclear, that no meta-analytic effect size exists yet, and that samples are mostly Western.[^s5] The phone-call ("schmoozing") benefit appeared mainly in mixed-gender pairs.[^s4] Treat rapport as a reliable aid to cooperation, not a guarantee.

## Connections

Rapport sits under **interpersonal communication**.[^s1] It is **demonstrated by** Drolet and Morris's face-to-face experiments,[^s2] Moore and colleagues' email-negotiation study[^s3] and the "schmooze or lose" study,[^s4] and it is **supported by** Gabbert and colleagues' 2021 systematic review.[^s5] It **complements active listening**, the behaviour researchers use most often to build it.[^s5] It is **applied in face-to-face conversation**, where it forms most easily,[^s2] and in **email**, where it is hardest to build and most worth seeding with a live conversation first.[^s4] It is related to small talk and self-disclosure, which are routes to it,[^s3][^s6] to nonverbal communication, which carries its visible cues,[^s1] to psychological safety, and to channel selection.
