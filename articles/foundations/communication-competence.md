# Communication competence

Communication competence is the degree to which what you say and do is seen as both **appropriate** and **effective** in a given context.[^s1] Appropriate means it fits the rules, expectations and relationship of the situation; effective means it gets you, at least partly, what you were trying to achieve.[^s1][^s3] Brian Spitzberg, the scholar most associated with the idea, stresses that competence is a judgment of quality made by people in a context, not a fixed ability: the same blunt status update can read as competent in an incident bridge call and as rude in a client review.[^s1] That framing is useful at work because it turns "be a better communicator" into two questions you can actually check.

## Origin

The modern construct took shape in US communication studies around 1980, when James McCroskey and others debated whether competence meant knowing what to do or being able to do it.[^s2] Spitzberg and William Cupach's **component model** (1984) settled on three interdependent parts: **motivation** (whether you approach or avoid the interaction), **knowledge** (knowing what behaviour would fit and work) and **skill** (actually performing it).[^s2][^s3] Other definitions of the period added face concerns and goal management, for example choosing behaviours that reach your goals while preserving the other person's face, and later work added criteria such as adaptability, involvement, conversation management and empathy alongside appropriateness and effectiveness.[^s3] Shockley-Zalabak (1988) split motivation into sensitivity and commitment for organisational settings.[^s3] In 2013 Spitzberg restated the model for health professionals, adding context and outcomes as predictors.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Crossing the two criteria gives four outcomes: **minimizing** (neither appropriate nor effective), **sufficing** (polite but you did not get what you needed), **maximizing** (you got your way but broke norms or bruised the relationship) and **optimizing** (both).[^s1] Many workplace failures are sufficing, such as the hedged request nobody acts on, or maximizing, such as the escalation that works once and costs trust for months.[^s1] Supervisors' competence also matters to the people they lead: a 2008 survey of 220 US employees found that their rating of the supervisor's communicator competence was the strongest predictor of their communication satisfaction (68% of variance) and also predicted job satisfaction (about 18%), more than task or relational leadership style.[^s4] That study is cross-sectional and relies on one rater per supervisor, so it shows association, not cause.[^s4]

## What good looks like

Spitzberg groups the observable skills into four clusters:[^s1]

- **Attentiveness:** showing interest in and concern for the other person, for example asking about and following up on their points.[^s1]
- **Composure:** confidence and assertiveness without anxiety cues or aggression.[^s1]
- **Coordination:** managing the flow of talk, including openings, turn-taking, topic changes and closings.[^s1]
- **Expressiveness:** vivid, animated, clear delivery that fits the setting.[^s1]

Context sets what "fits": culture, relationship, timing, situation and function all change which behaviours are judged appropriate.[^s1] Effective does not always mean winning; in a no-win conversation, such as owning a serious mistake, it can mean choosing the least harmful path.[^s1]

**Example.** *Situation:* an analyst needs a delayed data extract from another team before a board deadline. *Before:* she posts in the team channel, "This is blocking us AGAIN, need it today," and gets the file, but the other lead complains to her manager (maximizing). *After:* she messages the lead directly, names the deadline and the one table she needs, asks what is blocking it and offers to trim the scope. *Why:* the request is just as clear about what she needs, but it respects the other lead's standing and gives them a way to help, so it is effective and appropriate.[^s1][^s3]

## How to practise it

1. **Name both goals before a key conversation.** Write one line on what effective looks like for you and one on what the other side will see as appropriate.[^s1]
2. **Diagnose the weak component.** Decide whether you are avoiding the conversation (motivation), unsure what fits (knowledge) or knowing but fumbling it (skill), because each needs a different fix.[^s2][^s3]
3. **Get other people's ratings.** Self-ratings and others' ratings typically correlate only about 0.25 to 0.50, and people tend to overrate how appropriate they seem; you are the better judge of your effectiveness, others of your appropriateness.[^s1]
4. **Practise specific behaviours with feedback, then consolidate.** A 2011 meta-analysis of 13 controlled studies of clinicians found that skills training moderately improved observed behaviours such as open questions and empathy (effect size 0.54); follow-up workshops or supervision added more, and courses under 24 hours did little.[^s5]
5. **Re-check in each new context.** A new client, culture or channel resets what counts as appropriate, so ask early how people there prefer to communicate.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The status is `mixed`. The appropriateness-and-effectiveness framework is widely used, but competence is a perception, so scores shift with who is rating and in what context, and self-reports in particular are weakly related to others' judgments.[^s1][^s3] Researchers have not agreed on one list of competencies to measure, which makes studies hard to compare.[^s5] Training evidence comes mostly from healthcare: gains in observed behaviour are moderate and heterogeneous, the few long-term follow-ups did not show lasting effects, and effects on patients were small, so expect to keep practising rather than to be fixed by one course.[^s5] Workplace evidence such as the supervisor study is correlational.[^s4]

## Connections

The component model was introduced by Brian Spitzberg (`cm:brian-spitzberg`) with William Cupach, in a field shaped by James McCroskey's early debate over knowledge versus performance (`cm:james-mccroskey`), and it is studied within `cm:communication-studies`.[^s2][^s3] It is a core idea of [[interpersonal-communication]], and `cm:intercultural-competence` applies the same appropriate-and-effective test across cultures.[^s1] [[communication-apprehension]] is the avoidance side of the motivation component.[^s1] [[clarity]], [[register-and-tone]] and [[audience-analysis]] are ways of meeting the appropriateness and effectiveness criteria, and [[active-listening]] builds the attentiveness cluster.[^s1] Madlock's supervisor study (`cm:madlock-2008`) supports its link to employee satisfaction, and Barth and Lannen's meta-analysis (`cm:barth-lannen-2011`) supports the claim that the skill component can be trained.[^s4][^s5] [[receiving-feedback]], [[feedback]] and [[self-recording-playback]] supply the outside view that self-ratings lack.[^s1]
