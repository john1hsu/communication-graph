# Transactional model of communication

The transactional model treats communication as something people make together rather than something one person delivers to another. Its participants are called communicators, not senders and receivers, because each one is sending and receiving at the same moment: while you speak, you are also taking in the other person's face, posture and silence, and adjusting as you go.[^s2] Meaning is not packed into a message and unpacked at the other end; Barnlund argued that people invent and assign meaning to the cues around them.[^s1] The model also places every exchange inside social, relational and cultural contexts that shape it before, during and after the conversation.[^s2]

## Origin

Dean C. Barnlund set out the model in "A Transactional Model of Communication", a chapter in Kenneth Sereno and David Mortensen's 1970 reader *Foundations of Communication Theory*.[^s1] He opened by warning that a model is only an analogy and can be no better than the data and assumptions behind it.[^s1] His first postulate was that communication describes the evolution of meaning: in his words, "the production of meaning, rather than the production of messages" defines it.[^s1] He added that this process is dynamic, continuous, circular, unrepeatable, irreversible and complex, and the book's editors stressed his view that social context largely determines what a communication achieves.[^s1] His diagram showed each person's decoding and encoding as a linked spiral, fed by public cues in the setting, private cues inside the person, and behavioural cues, verbal and nonverbal, that people produce.[^s1] His running example is a patient, Mr. A, in a clinic waiting room whose doctor greets him; both men must make sense of an ambiguous greeting by weighing every cue at once.[^s1]

Introductory textbooks present the model as the third step after the one-way transmission (Shannon-Weaver) model and the turn-taking interaction model.[^s4][^s2] Gasiorek and Aune group it with other transactional accounts, such as Watzlawick and colleagues (1967) and Miller and Steinberg (1975), that cast communication as a collaborative process in which everyone co-creates meaning.[^s3]

## Why it matters

The transmission picture lets a speaker assume that a clearly sent message was received as meant. The transactional model removes that comfort: meaning is assigned by the listener, so the speaker's intention is only one input.[^s1] It also reframes communication as more than information transfer. Textbooks stress that people use it to build relationships, shape self-concept and create shared social realities, so a status meeting is also negotiating trust and roles.[^s2] Barnlund's point that communication is irreversible has a blunt workplace meaning: a sharp remark in a meeting cannot be withdrawn, only followed by more communication.[^s1]

## How to use it at work

- **Read while you talk.** Because you receive while you send, watch reactions mid-sentence and adjust; a technologist introducing a procedure can change course as soon as a patient looks confused.[^s2]
- **Check the three contexts.** Before a hard message, ask what the norms of the setting are (social), what history you share with this person (relational), and which identities, visible or not, may shape how it lands (cultural).[^s2]
- **Treat meaning as negotiated, not delivered.** Ask the other person what they took from a message rather than assuming your intent arrived intact.[^s1][^s3]
- **Account for every cue.** The room, the timing and your tone are read alongside your words, so the same sentence can mean different things in a hallway and in a performance review.[^s1]
- **Plan for irreversibility.** In high-stakes exchanges, pause before reacting, since a repair takes more communication than the original slip.[^s1]

## Evidence

The model is a conceptual framework, not a tested prediction, so its status is `theoretical`.[^s3] Barnlund himself described his pilot models as partial, saying they might not fully meet any of his own criteria.[^s1] Gasiorek and Aune credit transactional models with a fundamentally different and more understanding-focused view than code-based linear and interactive ones, but list clear limits: they focus mainly on social and relational outcomes, say little about how meaning is actually co-built, are heuristic and abstract rather than functional, and take mutual understanding as given instead of explaining how people reach it.[^s3] Popular textbook versions also add elements Barnlund did not, such as explicit worldviews and noise around each communicator, so a summary may differ from the 1970 chapter.[^s4] For practical use, pair the model with more specific accounts of how people establish shared understanding.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Dean C. Barnlund, in the 1970 chapter "A Transactional Model of Communication".[^s1]
- **Contrasts with** the interaction model of communication, in which people alternate between sending and receiving rather than doing both at once.[^s2]
- **Complements** common ground and grounding theory, which supplies the mechanism of mutual understanding that transactional models assume.[^s3]
- **Studied in** interpersonal communication, the setting Barnlund modelled, and communication studies, where it is taught as a core model.[^s1][^s3]
- **Broader:** communication models; **requires** the Shannon-Weaver model, the linear baseline it answers.[^s4]
- **Related to** checking understanding and perception checking (testing the meaning you or others assigned), nonverbal communication (behavioural cues), feedback, communication competence and high- and low-context cultures (cultural context).[^s2][^s1]
