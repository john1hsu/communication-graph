# Cognitive psychology

Cognitive psychology is the part of psychology that studies how people think: perception, knowledge, judgement, language, problem solving and memory, much of which runs below conscious awareness.[^s1] It asks how people sort information into categories and how thinking interacts with emotion and language.[^s1] For anyone communicating at work, it is the field that explains what happens inside a reader's or listener's head after your message arrives. It shows why a dense email gets skimmed, why a ten-point agenda item is forgotten, and why two people can hear the same sentence and file it differently.[^s1][^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Through the first half of the twentieth century, American experimental psychology was organised around behaviourism, which accepted only observable behaviour as data.[^s3] George A. Miller dates the counter-revolution to the 1950s. He picks out one meeting as its moment of conception: a symposium on information theory at MIT on 11 September 1956.[^s3] There, Allen Newell and Herbert Simon presented a logic-proving program, Noam Chomsky presented generative grammar, and Miller described how people get around the bottleneck of limited short-term memory.[^s3] In 1960 Miller and Jerome Bruner founded the Harvard Center for Cognitive Studies.[^s3][^s4]

Miller's 1956 paper on "the magical number seven" showed that attention and memory have limited capacity. It also introduced "chunks": people recode information into larger meaningful units rather than storing raw bits.[^s4] Steven Pinker credits Miller with founding both psycholinguistics and cognitive psychology.[^s4] Ulric Neisser's 1967 book *Cognitive Psychology* gathered research on perception, pattern recognition, attention, problem solving and memory into one information-processing framework.[^s2] Colleagues credit it with naming the field, and Neisser became known as its father.[^s2] Neisser later criticised the field he had shaped. In *Cognition and Reality* (1976) and afterwards, he argued that laboratory tasks had drifted away from how people perceive and remember in everyday life, and he pushed for ecologically valid research.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Three findings from the field translate directly into workplace habits.

- **Working memory is small, so chunk for your reader.** Miller treated seven as a rough guide, not a precise limit.[^s4][^s5] Cowan's 2001 review put the central capacity at about four chunks when people cannot rehearse or group items.[^s5] A spoken update that lists nine unrelated risks will overflow that store. Grouping them under three labelled themes ("vendor", "staffing", "budget") gives listeners fewer, larger units to hold.[^s4][^s5]
- **People read through schemas.** Schemas are clusters of related concepts that let the brain process information quickly, and they trigger fast assumptions about people and events.[^s1] Event schemas, or scripts, run automatically and are hard to change.[^s1] When a team expects a "status update" and you deliver a decision request in that format, the request may be filed as routine. Name what you need in the subject line or opening sentence so the right script fires.[^s1]
- **Categories differ between people.** Natural concepts come from experience, so two colleagues can share a word but hold different prototypes for it.[^s1] "Urgent" or "done" can mean different things to an engineer and a sales lead. Define terms that carry a decision or a deadline.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Cognitive findings have held up better than many in psychology, but not uniformly. In the Open Science Collaboration's 2015 replication of 100 studies, 50% of cognitive effects (21 of 42) replicated at the conventional significance level, against 25% of social effects (14 of 55).[^s6] The authors linked part of that gap to weaker original effects in the social studies, and effect sizes shrank on replication across all disciplines.[^s6] Even the most famous result has been revised: the estimate of short-term capacity moved from about seven items to about three to five chunks once rehearsal and grouping were controlled.[^s4][^s5] Neisser's critique also still applies. A capacity limit measured with word lists in a lab is a guide to how an audience handles a slide or a memo, not a precise rule for it.[^s2][^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Ulric Neisser,** whose 1967 textbook unified the field and is credited with naming it.[^s2]
- **Influenced by George A. Miller,** whose work on capacity limits and chunking, and whose role in the 1956 MIT symposium, helped launch the cognitive revolution.[^s3][^s4]
- **Supported by Cowan (2001),** which consolidated evidence for a central working-memory limit of about four chunks.[^s5]
- **Challenged by the Open Science Collaboration (2015),** which found that half of the cognitive effects it retested did not replicate at the original significance level.[^s6]
- It sits under psychology alongside social psychology. Cognitive load theory, the cognitive theory of multimedia learning, cognitive biases, the curse of knowledge and information overload in this graph all draw on its models of attention, memory and judgement.[^s1][^s4][^s5]
