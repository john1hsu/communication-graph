# Organizational communication

Organizational communication is the discipline that studies how communication works inside and around organizations. One textbook definition treats it as the process by which an organizational stakeholder, or group of stakeholders, tries to stimulate meaning in the mind of another through intentional verbal, nonverbal or mediated messages.[^s1] Following Stanley Deetz, the term can name three different things: an academic discipline with its own courses and journals, a way of describing and explaining organizations through their communication, and the specific events that happen at work, from an employee conflict to an executive presentation.[^s1] For a practitioner, the field is where much of the evidence behind everyday advice originates, including which channel to choose, how information overload builds up, and why the way a supervisor talks matters.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The field grew out of business speech teaching. Early twentieth-century public-speaking books written for businessmen were followed by Chester Barnard's 1938 argument that an executive's first job is to build and maintain a communication system.[^s1] W. Charles Redding, usually called the father of organizational communication, published on speech and human relations in 1937. Redding and George Sanborn's 1964 *Business and Industrial Communication: A Source Book* is generally treated as the field's real starting point.[^s1] A 1967 conference at NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center helped fix the name "organizational communication", and the following year it became a formal division of the scholarly body that later became the International Communication Association.[^s1] Redding and Philip Tompkins traced the field's ideas to three roots: classical rhetoric, human-relations theory and management-organization theory.[^s2] Redding judged the move to the new name significant because it widened the subject beyond business and industry and beyond speaking-skills training.[^s2]

The field then shifted several times. In the early 1980s an interpretive turn, marked by Linda Putnam and Michael Pacanowsky's 1983 collection, studied organizational culture and meaning rather than only measured message flows. Critical studies of power followed in the 1990s.[^s1][^s2] A more recent strand, the communicative constitution of organizations (CCO), argues that an organization is not a container in which communication happens. Instead, it is an ongoing and fragile accomplishment produced through communication, drawing on the Montreal School, Karl Weick's work on organizing and sensemaking, and Robert McPhee's "four flows" model.[^s3]

## Why it matters

The field gives a working professional three useful habits of thought:

- **Look at the system, not only the message.** Problems such as overloaded inboxes, filtered upward reporting or unclear decision rights are patterns in how information moves. They are rarely one person's bad email, and the field studies them at that level.[^s1][^s2]
- **Treat meaning as co-constructed.** CCO and interpretive research hold that what a policy, status update or reorganization means is negotiated in conversation and text. It is never simply transmitted, so announcements need follow-up conversations, not only distribution.[^s3]
- **Take supervisor talk seriously.** Motivating language theory, a line of organizational-communication research, sorts leader speech into direction-giving, meaning-making and empathetic language.[^s5] A 1995 scale study linked it to employees' satisfaction with how their supervisor communicates.[^s5] A 2010 study of 151 health-care workers associated it with higher rated performance and, at the dyad level, job satisfaction.[^s4]

Consider an operations manager who announces a new shift system by memo and then sees confusion and pushback. An organizational-communication lens points to the gaps: no meaning-making about why the change is happening, no channel for questions to flow back up, and no conversation in which teams could make sense of it together.[^s3][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

Organizational communication is a broad field with several methods, so its claims vary in strength. A review of 289 articles from the 1980s found most were social-scientific, about a quarter qualitative and a small share critical.[^s1] Much of its applied evidence comes from correlational surveys. The motivating-language findings, for example, rest on single-site samples and self-reported or supervisor-rated outcomes. The 2010 authors note that they examined only a subset of possible outcomes.[^s4] The 1995 scale study found no link between motivating language and supervisors' ratings of employees' communication competence.[^s5] Scholars inside the field also describe it as theoretically fragmented. The CCO literature acknowledges abstract language, blurry boundaries between perspectives and limited engagement with mainstream organization theory.[^s3] Use the field's frameworks as diagnostic lenses and its effect sizes as directional, not as precise predictions for your team.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

- **Introduced by W. Charles Redding,** with the 1964 Redding and Sanborn source book usually treated as the field's starting point; Chester Barnard's view of the executive as communication-system builder was an early influence.[^s1]
- **Influenced by rhetoric,** along with human-relations and management theory, according to Redding and Tompkins's account of its roots.[^s2]
- **Supported by Mayfield and Mayfield (2010),** one of the studies linking leader communication to employee performance and satisfaction, and related to motivating language theory more generally.[^s4][^s5]
- **Related to the communicative constitution of organizations,** the field's main contemporary theoretical strand.[^s3]
- **Theories and problems studied in the field** include media richness theory and channel selection, information overload, upward communication such as managing up and status reporting, and delivering bad news; intercultural communication is a sibling field that often overlaps with it in multinational teams.[^s1][^s2]
