# Social sciences

The social sciences are the academic disciplines that study human behaviour in its social and cultural aspects; Britannica's core list is cultural anthropology, sociology, psychology, political science and economics.[^s1] Funders draw the boundary more widely: the UK's Economic and Social Research Council (ESRC) supports eighteen disciplines, among them linguistics, education, human geography, social statistics and management and business studies.[^s3] From the 1950s the same group was often called the behavioural sciences, partly to connect it with physical anthropology and physiological psychology.[^s1] History sits on the border: many treat historiography as a social science, while most historians place their subject in the humanities.[^s1]

## Origin

Questions about human nature, the state and morality go back to ancient Greece, but the social sciences became distinct, recognised academic disciplines only around the start of the 20th century.[^s1] Britannica traces the idea itself to the 17th and 18th centuries, when thinkers sought rationally deduced principles for organising society.[^s2] Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau analysed the structure of the political state, the physiocrats and Adam Smith applied the idea of structure to economic life, and writers such as Condorcet, Rousseau and Smith developed theories of social development before biological evolution was proposed.[^s2] For much of the 19th century social reform and social science were treated as nearly the same thing, which Britannica argues slowed real knowledge of behaviour, because reform is by nature biased toward the reformer's idea of the good.[^s2] Census, tax and trade records and travellers' accounts supplied plenty of data well before then, but until the early 19th century there was no framework for reading them objectively.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Almost every evidence-based claim in this graph, from how people react to feedback to how meetings fail, was tested by a social scientist: sociolinguists and discourse analysts study how people make meaning through language, and management researchers study organisational psychology, employment relations and productivity.[^s3] Knowing how these fields collect evidence helps a practitioner judge a claim before acting on it. Qualitative research, using semi-structured or unstructured interviews, observation, open surveys and diaries, explains what situations mean to the people in them; the ESRC notes that a single case study needs quantitative methods before it can be generalised.[^s4] Quantitative research, using questionnaires, cross-sectional and longitudinal surveys and administrative records, can describe whole organisations or populations in ways no single observer could, but it depends on well-worded, non-leading questions.[^s6]

A workplace example: a manager reads that "people stop listening after ten minutes" and plans to cut every all-hands to that length. Asking the social-science questions first (Who was studied? Was it interviews and observation, a survey of a sample, or an experiment? Were the questions leading?) turns a slogan into a claim that can be checked, because each method supports a different kind of conclusion.[^s4][^s6] The next question, whether anyone has replicated it, matters just as much, as the evidence below shows.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

Social-science findings are not equally solid. In 2018 a 24-author team led by Colin Camerer repeated 21 experimental social-science studies published in *Nature* and *Science* between 2010 and 2015, with pre-registered plans and samples about five times larger than the originals.[^s5] Thirteen of the 21 (62%) found a significant effect in the original direction, and the replicated effects averaged about half the size of the originals.[^s5] Peer researchers' beliefs about which results would hold up were strongly related to what actually replicated, which suggests experts can often spot fragile findings.[^s5] The practical lesson is to prefer effects that have survived replication or meta-analysis, and to expect real-world effects to be smaller than headline numbers.[^s5] The fields' early fusion with reform is a reminder that values can shape what researchers look for, so practitioners should separate a study's findings from its authors' recommendations.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Contrasts with the humanities.** Britannica notes that history is claimed by both camps, and that most historians count it as a humanity rather than a social science; communication studies and rhetoric draw on both traditions.[^s1]
- **Challenged by Camerer et al. (2018),** whose replication of high-profile experiments found that about two-thirds held up and that effects were roughly halved, a caution that applies to any single study behind communication advice.[^s5]
- **Linguistics** is related: the ESRC lists linguistics, including sociolinguistics and discourse analysis, as one of its social-science disciplines.[^s3]
- Psychology, social psychology, industrial-organizational psychology, organisational communication and conflict resolution are related fields within or beside this family; the ESRC's psychology and management and business studies disciplines cover much of their workplace research.[^s3][^s1]
