# Industrial-organizational psychology

Industrial-organizational (I-O) psychology studies how human behaviour shapes work and how work shapes human behaviour.[^s1] Its professional body, the Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology (SIOP), describes it as the science of well-being and performance in work settings, practised in a scientist-practitioner model where the same people do research and apply it.[^s3] The American Psychological Association lists the problems it tackles: hiring and placement, training and development, performance measurement, motivation and incentives, job and work design, and organizational change.[^s4] Many of those problems are communication problems in disguise: how a manager gives feedback, how an interviewer asks questions, how a team coordinates. That is why so many articles in this graph trace their evidence back to this field.[^s4][^s6]

## Origin

SIOP treats Hugo Münsterberg as the field's founding father and his 1913 book *Psychology and Industrial Efficiency* as its seminal text.[^s2] The OpenStax textbook adds that the book covered employee selection, training and advertising.[^s1] Münsterberg, James Cattell and Walter Dill Scott had all trained under Wilhelm Wundt. Scott wrote some of the first books applying psychology to business, including work on advertising and on influencing people in business, before Carnegie Tech hired him in 1916 to research employee selection.[^s1] In the First World War, Robert Yerkes's group built the Army Alpha and Beta ability tests, while Scott and Walter Bingham worked on officer selection, job descriptions and performance ratings.[^s1]

The "organizational" half came later. Elton Mayo and colleagues studied Western Electric's Hawthorne Works near Chicago from 1929 to 1932. They began by varying lighting and concluded that social factors such as supervisory style mattered more, which textbooks treat as the birth of organizational psychology.[^s1] APA Division 14, Industrial and Business Psychology, was founded in 1945, and it was renamed the Division of Industrial and Organizational Psychology in 1973.[^s2] After the Second World War, research turned to test fairness, job satisfaction and motivation. The US Civil Rights Act of 1964 raised the stakes for fair selection.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The field is useful to anyone who communicates at work because it tests advice instead of repeating it.

- **It splits work into three lenses.** Industrial psychology covers job requirements, hiring, training and evaluation. Organizational psychology covers relationships, motivation, leadership and culture. Human factors covers how people use tools and displays, including how readable they are.[^s1] Ask which lens a problem sits in before choosing a fix: a confusing dashboard is a human-factors problem, not a motivation problem.[^s1]
- **It treats teams as a process you can improve.** Kozlowski and Ilgen's 2006 review drew on more than 50 years of team research. They concluded that there is a solid science of team effectiveness, built around cognitive, motivational and behavioural team processes, and that leaders can shape those processes with specific interventions.[^s6] For a team lead, the practical move is to treat coordination habits like briefings and check-ins as things to design, not as personality.[^s6]
- **It asks for evidence on people decisions.** Selection, coaching and performance criteria are core I-O activities.[^s4] Before adopting an interview format or a review template, ask whether there is I-O research behind it.[^s3][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The field's founding stories deserve the same scrutiny it applies to others. The popular Hawthorne story says productivity rose with every lighting change, even dimming, because workers knew they were being watched.[^s1] Levitt and List found the original illumination data, long believed lost. They reported that the remarkable patterns in the usual retellings were not in the data, although subtler signs of an observation effect were.[^s5] The lesson for practitioners is to check the primary study behind any famous workplace finding before quoting it in a presentation or a policy.[^s5]

As a Field, I-O psychology is judged by how useful its research is rather than by one result. Its own professional bodies frame it as applied science, so specific findings (on feedback, interviews or engagement) carry their own evidence ratings in their own articles.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Hugo Münsterberg** and **introduced in** *Psychology and Industrial Efficiency* (1913), which SIOP names as its founder and seminal text.[^s2]
- **Influenced by Wilhelm Wundt,** under whom Münsterberg, Cattell and Scott trained, and **by the Hawthorne studies,** which textbooks credit with launching its organizational side.[^s1]
- It sits under psychology beside social psychology, and overlaps organizational communication. Feedback, receiving feedback, job interviews, performance reviews and employee engagement in this graph are **studied in** it, matching APA's list of its core problems: selection, performance measurement, coaching and motivation.[^s4] Team research such as Kozlowski and Ilgen's review is the evidence base for group communication.[^s6]
