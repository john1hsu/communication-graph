# Communication studies

Communication studies is the academic discipline that examines how people use messages to create meaning in different contexts, covering the forms, modes, media and effects of communication. Its main U.S. scholarly association describes it as drawing on humanistic, social-scientific and aesthetic inquiry, and as treating communication as relational, collaborative, strategic, symbolic and adaptive.[^s1] The field is cross-disciplinary by nature. Its scholars come from philosophy and rhetoric as well as psychology and sociology.[^s3] Most of the research behind workplace communication advice, from listening and feedback to channel choice, is produced in one of its subfields.[^s1]

## Origin

The discipline began as the study of rhetoric, the art of persuasive speaking, which ancient thinkers treated as part of every citizen's education. Rhetoric then became a liberal art in American colleges, as in John Quincy Adams's 1810 lectures on rhetoric and oratory at Harvard.[^s1] In 1914 seventeen speech teachers in Chicago left the National Council of Teachers of English to found their own association, in order to establish the study of speech as a separate discipline.[^s1] That body began as the National Association of Academic Teachers of Public Speaking. It became the Speech Communication Association in 1970 and the National Communication Association in 1997. The renaming came partly because the field had grown to nearly 50 subdisciplines that "speech" no longer described.[^s3]

After the Second World War, scholars increasingly used social-science methods to study audiences and message effects, prompted partly by the role propaganda had played in fascism.[^s1] U.S. communication research also grew out of journalism schools in this period. Wilbur Schramm did much to establish communication as a field in American universities and is sometimes called its founder.[^s5] Historian Jefferson Pooley argues that Schramm also gave the young field an origin story built around four "founders". In his account, that story was a constructed narrative that helped a discipline with a legitimacy deficit justify itself, not a neutral history.[^s4] Other figures also have claims to the field's beginnings.[^s5]

## Why it matters

The field's subdivisions map closely onto workplace problems. The National Communication Association lists specializations including applied, interpersonal, organizational, small-group, intercultural, health, legal and visual communication, along with public relations, mediation and dispute resolution, and electronic and digital media.[^s1] Knowing which subfield studies a problem helps you find better evidence than generic advice. Meeting dysfunction, for example, falls under small-group research, and a clash in a multinational team falls under intercultural research.[^s1][^s3]

Robert Craig's 1999 article *Communication Theory as a Field* gives practitioners a second tool. Craig argued that the field had not cohered. One analysis of seven textbooks he cites counted 249 "theories", and only 7% of them appeared in more than three books.[^s2] Instead of a single unified theory, he proposed treating communication as a practical discipline organized around seven traditions. Each tradition defines communication, and what goes wrong with it, differently:[^s2][^s6]

- **Rhetorical:** a practical art of discourse; the problem is a situation that needs collective judgment.[^s2]
- **Semiotic:** mediation through signs; the problem is a gap between people's viewpoints.[^s2]
- **Phenomenological:** dialogue and the experience of the other; the problem is failing to sustain authentic relationships.[^s2]
- **Cybernetic:** information processing; the problems are noise, overload and malfunction.[^s2]
- **Sociopsychological:** expression, interaction and influence; the problem is finding the causes that produce outcomes.[^s2]
- **Sociocultural:** the production and reproduction of social order; the problem is shared patterns breaking down across groups or over time.[^s2]
- **Critical:** reflection on discourse; the problem is power and ideology distorting what can be said.[^s2]

In practice, the traditions work as a diagnostic checklist. Picture a project lead whose reorganization announcement drew confusion and quiet resistance. A cybernetic reading asks whether the message got lost in an overloaded channel. A semiotic reading asks whether "restructure" meant something different to engineers than to managers. A sociocultural reading asks whether the change broke team norms. A critical reading asks whether people felt unable to object. Each reading suggests a different fix, so running through several before acting avoids treating every problem as a messaging problem.[^s2][^s6] Craig's own case for the traditions is that each builds on everyday beliefs about communication and challenges others, which makes them relevant to practitioners' own reflection.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

Communication studies is a discipline, not a finding, so its claims are as strong as the subfield and method behind them. Some work is experimental and quantitative, and some is interpretive, rhetorical or critical.[^s1][^s6] Craig's own diagnosis is that much of the field's theory is fragmented and that scholars in different traditions often ignore each other.[^s2] His map has critics too. Manuel Martín Algarra, for example, argues that it turns communication theory into theory about theories and moves it away from real communication problems, while granting its value for seeing the field as a whole.[^s7] The field's founding narratives are also contested, as Pooley and McAnany show.[^s4][^s5] Treat the field as a source of tested subfield research and useful lenses, and check the evidence for any single claim made in its name.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

- **Influenced by rhetoric:** the discipline began as the study and teaching of rhetoric and public speaking, the line that runs back to [[aristotles-rhetoric]].[^s1]
- **Influenced by social psychology:** Craig identifies a sociopsychological tradition that studies communication as interaction and influence, the strand that grew as post-war scholars turned to social-science methods to study audiences and message effects.[^s2][^s1]
- **Introduced by Wilbur Schramm** (`cm:wilbur-schramm`), with low-to-medium confidence: he did much to establish the field institutionally in U.S. universities, but historians dispute the founder story.[^s5][^s4]
- **Related to Communication Theory as a Field** (`cm:communication-theory-as-a-field`): Craig's 1999 article maps the field's seven theoretical traditions; Martín Algarra's 2009 critique notes Craig later added pragmatism as an eighth.[^s2][^s7]
- **Subfields** in this graph include [[interpersonal-communication]], [[organizational-communication]], [[intercultural-communication]] and [[digital-communication]]. The [[shannon-weaver-model]] is the classic model in the cybernetic tradition, and [[pragmatics]] is a neighbouring linguistic field.[^s1][^s2]
