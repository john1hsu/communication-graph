# Rhetorical studies

Rhetoric, studied as a discipline, is the field that examines, and teaches, how people use language to change what others think or do. Britannica's entry by Berkeley rhetorician Thomas Sloane describes it as the principles of training communicators who seek to persuade or inform, and notes that modern rhetoric covers all discourse, with attention to audience, intention and structure.[^s2] Purdue's Online Writing Lab puts it more broadly still: any communication meant to shift how someone else sees things, a sense far wider than the everyday slur of "empty rhetoric".[^s5] The field works in two directions: analysing texts other people made, and generating your own.[^s2]

## Origin

Britannica traces rhetoric to fifth-century BCE Syracuse, where a new democratic legal system meant ordinary citizens had to argue their own property claims in court; in Athens the Sophists then made it a central subject of education.[^s2] Plato attacked it in the *Gorgias* as persuasion without knowledge and denied that it was a real art.[^s1] Aristotle answered that critique. In his *Rhetoric* he defined the art as the ability to see what is possibly persuasive in a given case, called it the counterpart of dialectic, and identified three means of persuasion: the speaker's character (*ethos*), the hearers' emotions (*pathos*) and the argument itself (*logos*).[^s1] He also sorted speeches into three kinds: deliberative (about future action and what is advantageous), judicial (about past acts and what is just) and epideictic (praise or blame).[^s1]

Roman writers turned the art into a curriculum. Cicero set out its five "offices" of invention, arrangement, style, delivery and memory, and Quintilian's *Institutio oratoria* made rhetoric the core of education.[^s2] The discipline lost standing from the sixteenth century, when Ramus split it apart, and through the age of print, until a revival in the 1930s.[^s2] In that revival, speech departments in American universities led the return to rhetorical traditions and writing teachers followed; Kenneth Burke built a theory for interpreting all kinds of symbolic action, and I. A. Richards applied rhetoric to literary analysis.[^s2] Over the past century, Burke and others reframed rhetoric's aim from persuasion alone toward identification, the ways people come to understand one another.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Rhetoric supplies the master questions behind most workplace communication advice. Lloyd Bitzer's 1968 essay "The Rhetorical Situation" argued that speech is called into existence by a situation with three parts.[^s3] The **exigence** is an imperfection that words can help change. The **audience** is only the people who can act on the message, not everyone who hears it. The **constraints** are the beliefs, facts, interests and traditions that shape what that audience will decide.[^s3] A message succeeds, he argued, when it is a fitting response to its situation.[^s3] Purdue's writing lab reduces the same idea to a checklist of text, author, audience, purposes and setting to settle before drafting.[^s6]

Consider a product lead who needs a release delayed after testing finds a data-loss bug. Without a rhetorical lens she might send the whole engineering list a detailed bug history. Read rhetorically, the exigence is the risk to customers' data; the rhetorical audience is the one director who can move the date; the constraints include a promised launch, the director's budget cycle and her own credibility after a previous slip.[^s3][^s6] Aristotle's categories sharpen the draft. It is deliberative, so it should argue about future advantage and harm, not past blame. Her credibility, the director's concern for customers and the test evidence all have to carry weight together.[^s1]

Richard Vatz's 1973 reply adds a responsibility test. He argued that no situation announces its own meaning: speakers choose which facts to make salient, so rhetoric is a cause, not an effect, of meaning.[^s4] In the example, calling the bug a "crisis" or a "known issue" is a choice the product lead must be able to defend.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

Rhetoric is a humanistic discipline. Its frameworks are tools for analysis and invention, judged by how well they explain and guide discourse, not experimentally tested effects; claims about which appeals actually change attitudes belong to persuasion research in social psychology.[^s2][^s1] Its key models are disputed: Bitzer treats the situation as the source of discourse, while Vatz holds that speakers create situations through what they choose to emphasise.[^s3][^s4] Popular summaries also flatten the classical theory. Rapp's account notes that Aristotle treated the argument as the only properly probative means of persuasion, not just one equal leg of an ethos-pathos-logos triangle.[^s1] The ethical question is old too. Aristotle saw the art as neutral and open to abuse by demagogues, while arguing that truth and justice are naturally easier to argue for; interpreters still disagree about how normative his rhetoric is.[^s1] That is why training in rhetoric should include where influence ends and manipulation begins.

## Connections

- **Influenced by Aristotle's Rhetoric** ([[aristotles-rhetoric]]): the treatise supplied the field's definition, its three means of persuasion and its three kinds of speech.[^s1]
- **Influenced by Quintilian** ([[quintilian]]): his *Institutio oratoria* made rhetoric the centre of Roman education.[^s2]
- **Influenced by Kenneth Burke** (`cm:kenneth-burke`): his theory of symbolic action underpins the twentieth-century "new rhetoric" and its turn toward identification.[^s2][^s5]
- **Challenged by Plato** (`cm:plato`): the *Gorgias* denied rhetoric was an art, the critique Aristotle's work answers.[^s1]
- **Complements dialectic** (`cm:dialectic`): Aristotle paired rhetoric's public speech with dialectic's question-and-answer debate, both arguing from accepted opinions.[^s1]
- **Related:** the rhetorical situation (`cm:rhetorical-situation`) of Bitzer and Vatz; [[audience-analysis]], the practical habit the rhetorical audience calls for; [[credibility]] and [[rhetorical-appeals]], modern forms of ethos and the three proofs; [[persuasion]]; and [[communication-studies]], the discipline that grew out of rhetoric and speech teaching.[^s3][^s2][^s1]
