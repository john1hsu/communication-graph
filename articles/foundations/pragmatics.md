# Pragmatics

Pragmatics is the branch of linguistics and philosophy of language that studies utterances: particular things said by particular people at a particular time and place, and what those people manage to do with them.[^s1] Where grammar asks whether a sentence is well formed and semantics asks what its words mean, pragmatics asks what the speaker meant by saying it here, to this listener.[^s1] "Can you send the deck by noon?" is grammatically a question about ability; pragmatically it is a request, and every competent colleague hears it that way.[^s2]

## Origin

The philosopher Charles Morris named the field in 1938, when he split the study of signs (semiotics) into syntax, semantics and pragmatics, the last being the relation of signs to the people who use them; Rudolf Carnap's 1942 version assigned to pragmatics any inquiry that refers to the language user.[^s1] The modern field took shape in mid-century Oxford philosophy. J. L. Austin's lectures, published after his death as *How to Do Things with Words* (1962), argued that saying something is often doing something: promising, warning, ordering.[^s1][^s2] John Searle (1969) sorted these speech acts into classes such as directives (requests, suggestions), commissives (promises, offers), expressives (apologies, complaints) and declarations.[^s2] H. P. Grice's 1967 lectures added the theory of conversational implicature, explaining how listeners infer unstated meaning by assuming the speaker is being cooperative; later, Sperber and Wilson's relevance theory reworked Grice's account around the search for relevance.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Most workplace miscommunication is not about vocabulary. It happens when the literal content and the intended force come apart: a "quick suggestion" from a director that was really an instruction, a "sounds interesting" that meant no, or a request phrased so bluntly it read as a reprimand.[^s1][^s2] Pragmatics gives names to these gaps. Philosophers distinguish *near-side* pragmatics, the context needed to work out what was said at all (who "you" is, which "Friday" was meant), from *far-side* pragmatics, the speech act performed and the implications drawn beyond the words.[^s1] Each side maps to a common workplace failure: ambiguous references in a handover note, and missed hints in a feedback conversation.

Linguist Jenny Thomas (1983) called the inability to grasp what is meant by what is said *pragmatic failure*, and split it into two kinds, as summarised in a 2017 review: *pragmalinguistic* failure, using the wrong linguistic form for the intended force, and *sociopragmatic* failure, misjudging the social conditions such as power or distance.[^s4] Her classic example was learners who answered a teacher's "Would you like to read?" with "No, I wouldn't", hearing a question about preference rather than a request.[^s4] A sociopragmatic case the same review cites is students emailing professors in a register that ignored the status gap.[^s4] The review also argues that Thomas's split is too narrow and that the line between the two kinds blurs in practice.[^s4]

## What the field studies

Core topics are implicature, speech acts, reference and indexicals (words like "this", "here" and "tomorrow" whose meaning depends on context), presupposition (what an utterance takes for granted), and the long-running debate between literalists and contextualists over how much context shapes what is said.[^s1] Applied researchers study how specific speech acts such as requests, refusals, complaints and apologies are performed and judged across languages and settings.[^s2]

Workplace data show how this plays out. Hofweber and Jaworska (2022) coded 223 requests in 398 internal emails at a multinational insurer and found 62% were direct, mostly "please" plus an imperative, with nearly all indirect requests in the "could you…?" form.[^s3] Senders' first language mattered more than whether they wrote English as a first or second language: Hebrew-speaking staff were almost always direct, while French- and Dutch-speaking staff were direct far less often.[^s3] Hierarchy shaped directness for native English speakers (senior staff were more direct), though the junior native-speaker sample was very small.[^s3] The lesson for readers is to treat directness as partly a lingua-cultural habit rather than automatic evidence of rudeness.[^s3]

## Using the field at work

- Before sending a request, check that its force is unmistakable: name the action, owner and deadline rather than relying on a hint.[^s1][^s3]
- When a message from a colleague feels rude or vague, separate what was literally said from what you inferred, and consider whether their first language or status norms explain the form.[^s3][^s4]
- Replace context-dependent words ("this", "next week", "the issue") with explicit references in anything read later or by others.[^s1]
- Adjust register to the power and distance of the relationship, the sociopragmatic dimension, especially when writing upward or across cultures.[^s4]

These are applications of descriptive theory, not interventions tested in trials; the field explains why misreadings occur rather than measuring which fixes work.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

Pragmatics is part of semiotics in Morris's three-way division and contrasts with semantics, which studies meaning independent of the speaker and occasion; Morris introduced the term.[^s1] It was shaped by speech act theory from Austin and Searle.[^s2] Grice's cooperative principle and common ground are central pragmatic ideas, and face, facework and politeness theory extend them to how speakers protect relationships.[^s1][^s4] Its findings on directness are applied in email and connect to intercultural communication, direct-versus-indirect feedback and high- and low-context cultures, where the same words carry different force.[^s3][^s4] Clarity, in this graph's sense, is largely a pragmatic achievement: the reader recovers the meaning the writer intended.[^s1]
