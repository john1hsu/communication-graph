# Psychology

Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behaviour.[^s1] Because nobody can observe a mind directly, psychologists measure behaviour, biological signals and self-reports and infer the mental processes behind them; they test hypotheses by observation and experiment and publish so that others can try to replicate the result.[^s1] Britannica describes it as a discipline that studies psychological and biological processes and behaviour in humans and other animals, made up of a large profession of practitioners and a smaller, growing research enterprise.[^s3] For communicators, psychology matters less as a subject than as the place where many claims about listening, persuasion, feedback, nerves and misunderstanding are tested, and where some of them fail.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Questions about temperament, mind and body go back to Aristotle, Plato, Hippocrates and Descartes, but psychology only split from philosophy as an empirical discipline in the late 1800s.[^s1][^s3] Wilhelm Wundt is usually called the first psychologist: in 1879 he opened a laboratory at Leipzig where he and his students ran reaction-time experiments and studied conscious experience through trained introspection.[^s2] Britannica pairs him with William James in the United States, whose *The Principles of Psychology* (1890) framed the field as a science of mental life.[^s3] James led functionalism, which asked how mental activity helps people adapt to their surroundings rather than breaking the mind into parts.[^s2]

The field then moved through several schools that still shape communication advice. Behaviourism (Watson, later Skinner) set the inner mind aside and studied observable behaviour and its consequences.[^s2][^s3] Humanistic psychology reacted against it; Carl Rogers's client-centred therapy, built on empathy, genuineness and unconditional positive regard, is the root of much modern listening training.[^s2] From the 1950s the cognitive revolution, helped by Noam Chomsky's critique of behaviourism and Ulric Neisser's 1967 *Cognitive Psychology*, brought thinking, memory and language back to the centre.[^s2] Later, cross-cultural and feminist psychologists challenged how narrow the field's samples and researchers had been.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Most communication frameworks borrow a psychological claim: that people misread intent, that emotion can be regulated, that framing changes choices, that feedback improves performance. Knowing the field, and how unevenly its findings hold up, helps you use those claims well.[^s3][^s4]

- **Know which subfield a claim comes from.** Britannica lists clinical, personality, social, developmental, experimental (perception, memory, thinking, learning), industrial-organizational and newer integrative areas such as behavioural economics.[^s3] A finding about memory in a laboratory and a finding about teams at work answer different questions; ask which one your advice rests on.[^s3]
- **Treat a single study as a lead, not a rule.** The Open Science Collaboration (2015) repeated 100 studies from three psychology journals. Only 36% of the replications were statistically significant, against 97% of the originals, and replication effects were about half as large.[^s4] Before you build a team habit on "research shows", check for a meta-analysis or a large replication.[^s4]
- **Ask who was studied.** Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan (2010) reviewed comparisons across populations and found that participants from Western, educated, industrialised, rich, democratic (WEIRD) societies were often outliers on reasoning, fairness, cooperation and self-concept.[^s5] Advice tested on US undergraduates may not transfer to a colleague in another culture.[^s5]
- **Think like the field.** Psychology courses explicitly train critical thinking: scepticism, spotting your own biases, asking good questions and observing carefully.[^s1] Those habits are also the habits of a good listener and a careful reader of evidence.[^s1]

A practical example: a manager reads a blog post saying that one small study found a short pre-meeting routine makes people more persuasive, and plans to roll it out to the sales team. Asking the three questions above (which subfield, has it replicated, who was studied) takes a few minutes and avoids training people on an effect that, on the 2015 replication figures, could easily turn out half as large or absent.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced by Wilhelm Wundt,** whose 1879 Leipzig laboratory marks psychology's start as an experimental science,[^s2] and **by William James,** named by Britannica as the field's other founder.[^s3]
- **Challenged by the Open Science Collaboration (2015),** whose large replication project found many published effects weaker or absent,[^s4] and **by Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan (2010),** who showed that generalising from WEIRD samples is unjustified for many basic processes.[^s5]
- It sits within the social sciences while drawing on natural science, because behaviour has both biological and social roots.[^s1] Its subfields include social psychology, cognitive psychology and industrial-organizational psychology; it overlaps communication studies, and this graph's nodes on cognitive bias, emotion regulation and Carl Rogers all draw on it.[^s2][^s3]
