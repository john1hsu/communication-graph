# Interpersonal communication

Interpersonal communication is talk between people whose lives affect each other: a manager and a report, two peers sharing a project, a consultant and a client contact.[^s2] Introductory communication textbooks treat it as one of five forms of communication, alongside intrapersonal, group, public and mass communication, and note that people spend more time in it than in any other form.[^s2] A narrower scholarly definition, from Arthur Bochner (1989), requires at least two people who deliberately orient to each other, each taking account of how the other sees them.[^s1] The term names both the everyday activity and the academic field that studies it.

## Origin

The field's roots lie in rhetoric and public speaking, which is why early research focused heavily on persuasion between individuals.[^s1][^s2] Social psychology later widened communication research to cover social and psychological context, and speech communication scholars adopted its hypothesis-testing methods, partly to establish the discipline as a rigorous social science.[^s1][^s2] Interpersonal communication became a distinct research area in the 1970s, drawing on Watzlawick, Beavin and Jackson's work on interaction (1967), Gregory Bateson, and Erving Goffman's studies of face-to-face encounters.[^s1] Landmark ideas from that period include Berger and Calabrese's uncertainty reduction theory (1975), Miller and Steinberg's continuum from impersonal to interpersonal relationships (1975), and Mark Knapp's stage model of how relationships grow and come apart (1978).[^s1] Later scholars influenced by social constructionism argued that relationships are built moment by moment in talk rather than simply expressed through it.[^s1] Knapp and Daly's four-volume benchmark collection (2010) organises the field into history and communicator traits, processes and messages, forming and maintaining relationships, and persuasion and competence.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Most workplace coordination happens one person to another, so most miscommunication and conflict also happen there.[^s2] Interpersonal talk serves instrumental goals (getting the task done) and relational goals (showing what a working relationship is and should be) at the same time, which is why a technically correct request can still damage trust.[^s2] The labour market rewards this capacity: David Deming's 2017 study of US data found that jobs demanding high social interaction grew by nearly 12 percentage points as a share of employment between 1980 and 2012, while math-intensive jobs with little social interaction shrank, and the wage return to social skills was markedly higher in the 2000s than in the 1980s and 1990s.[^s3] His explanation is that social skills cut the cost of coordinating, letting colleagues trade tasks and specialise.[^s3] That finding is correlational and uses broad survey measures of social skill, so it shows that interpersonal capability is valued, not which specific behaviours produce it.[^s3]

## What the field studies

Core research areas include how relationships start, develop and end; self-disclosure and uncertainty; interpersonal influence and compliance-gaining; nonverbal behaviour such as space, touch, timing and voice; and traits that shape communication, including cognitive complexity, self-monitoring and communication apprehension.[^s1] Researchers split between a psychological, experimental tradition and an interpretive tradition that studies talk as social ritual, and the field's findings have been applied to groups, families and organizations.[^s1]

## Using the field at work

The field's practical payoff is communication competence: knowing which patterns are effective and appropriate, and being able to adapt them to the person and setting, since standards vary across social and cultural contexts.[^s2] Textbook guidance for building it is to observe and evaluate how skilled colleagues handle conversations, work on personal blocks such as anxiety, stay mindful of context and the other person's perspective, and self-assess against concrete competencies such as stating ideas clearly, listening attentively and managing conflict.[^s2] A practical habit that follows from the instrumental/relational distinction is to ask, before a hard one-to-one conversation, both "what do I need done?" and "what will this say about how I see them?"[^s2]

## Connections

The field sits under communication studies, next to sibling fields such as organizational and intercultural communication, which study many of the same exchanges in their organizational or cultural setting.[^s2] It was influenced by social psychology, whose methods it adopted, and by rhetoric, from which speech communication grew.[^s1][^s2] Erving Goffman's work on face-to-face interaction was one of its early intellectual sources.[^s1] Uncertainty reduction theory and communication apprehension are among the theories and traits it studies, and communication competence is the outcome its applied work aims at.[^s1][^s2] Practical topics in this graph such as feedback, rapport, small talk, nonverbal communication and one-on-one meetings are narrower areas of interpersonal communication.[^s1][^s4]
