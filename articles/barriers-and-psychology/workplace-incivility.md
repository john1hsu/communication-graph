# Workplace incivility

Workplace incivility is everyday rudeness at work: condescending remarks, interrupting, ignoring someone's greeting or request, leaving people out of discussions, or claiming another person's idea. What sets it apart from aggression or bullying is its low intensity and ambiguous intent; the person on the receiving end often cannot tell whether the slight was deliberate, and the instigator may not have noticed it at all.[^s1][^s5] Because each act looks minor, organisations tend to shrug it off, yet researchers now treat it as a genuine workplace stressor with consequences far larger than the acts themselves.[^s5]

## Origin

Management scholars Lynne Andersson and Christine Pearson introduced the concept in a 1999 *Academy of Management Review* article. They argued that small discourtesies can trigger an **incivility spiral**: a target responds in kind, each exchange crosses a slightly higher threshold, and at a tipping point the episode can escalate into openly aggressive behaviour, with secondary spirals spreading to bystanders.[^s1] In 2001 Lilia Cortina and colleagues published the first large measurement study and a seven-item Workplace Incivility Scale, which turned the idea into something researchers could count.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Incivility is common. In Cortina's 2001 survey of about 1,100 employees in a federal court system, 71% reported some uncivil treatment in the previous five years, and the authors considered that an underestimate because rudeness from clients and the public was excluded. Targets reported lower job satisfaction and more lateness, sick days and reduced effort. People who stayed silent avoided retaliation but reported more psychological distress, while lower-status employees who objected to higher-status instigators often faced professional or social retaliation.[^s2]

It also degrades thinking and cooperation. In three experiments, Porath and Erez found that people exposed to rudeness, whether from an authority figure, a stranger, or merely imagining an incident, solved fewer problems, generated fewer creative ideas and were far less likely to help someone who dropped their things (in one study about a third helped, versus almost nine in ten controls). Impaired memory recall, not bad mood, carried much of the effect.[^s3] In Porath's 2016 survey of roughly 800 managers and employees, most said their performance and commitment fell after being treated poorly, many deliberately cut their time or quality of work, and about one in eight left the job because of it.[^s6]

## How to spot it

Look for patterns rather than single incidents:

- **Dismissing:** cutting people off, checking a phone while someone speaks, ignoring questions or greetings, or replying with a curt "already covered".[^s2][^s6]
- **Demeaning:** sarcasm, talking down, public criticism of small mistakes, or jokes at someone's expense.[^s2]
- **Excluding:** leaving people off invitations or threads they need, or overlooking their input in meetings.[^s5]
- **Credit-taking:** presenting a colleague's idea or work as your own.[^s6]
- **Retaliation in kind:** a curt reply to a curt reply, the early stage of the spiral.[^s1]

Power shapes it. Cortina's data showed that more powerful people were responsible for a disproportionate share of incidents, which is one reason targets rarely speak up.[^s2]

## How to fix it

For individuals, start with your own behaviour: audit how you treat people in meetings and messages, especially those junior to you, and replace dismissal with attention, credit and a clear "let's come back to that". Do not brush off incivility you experience; small slights accumulate, so note patterns and raise them privately and specifically with the person, or with a manager when power makes that unsafe.[^s5][^s6] When someone is curt with you, pause before replying so you do not start or feed a spiral.[^s1]

For leaders and organisations, Porath recommends modelling respect yourself (her respondents valued respect from leaders above recognition or development), screening for civility in structured interviews and reference checks, training people in feedback and difficult conversations, and making clear that everyone may hold colleagues and managers to the agreed norms. Team-level interventions such as CREW apply these ideas through facilitated sessions.[^s6]

## Evidence

The core finding that experienced incivility harms well-being, attitudes and behaviour has accumulated across two decades. A 2022 meta-analysis by Han and colleagues pooled 253 independent samples from 219 primary studies covering antecedents (target demographics, personality and work environment), affective, health, social-exchange and behavioural outcomes, and compared incivility's effects with those of bullying, abusive supervision and sexual harassment.[^s4] Experimental work shows short-term causal effects on performance and helping.[^s3] Limits remain: much of the field relies on self-reported, cross-sectional surveys, which is why the meta-analysis tested time-lagged versus cross-sectional designs as a moderator;[^s4] widely quoted prevalence figures come from different samples and time frames and should not be compared directly;[^s2][^s6] and Cortina's team has flagged parts of the literature that call for caution and correction.[^s5] Status here is therefore **mixed**: real, consistent harms, with causal and size estimates still being refined.

## Connections

Andersson and Pearson's **incivility spiral** is explained by the **norm of reciprocity** (`cm:norm-of-reciprocity`): people return rudeness in kind, and escalation can turn it into open **interpersonal conflict** (`cm:interpersonal-conflict`); Christine Pearson (`cm:christine-pearson`) co-introduced the concept.[^s1] Its harms are **demonstrated by** Cortina et al. 2001 (`cm:cortina-et-al-2001`)[^s2] and Porath and Erez 2007 (`cm:porath-erez-2007`),[^s3] and **supported by** the Han et al. 2022 meta-analysis (`cm:han-et-al-2022`).[^s4] As a stressor it **causes workplace stress** (`cm:workplace-stress`), and it **contrasts with workplace bullying** (`cm:workplace-bullying`), which is more intense, more clearly intended and repeated.[^s5] Porath reports that civil rather than uncivil delivery of the same suggestion raised psychological safety markedly, so incivility **undermines psychological safety** (`cm:psychological-safety`).[^s6] **Cyber incivility** is its digital form, **CREW** a team intervention against it, and **employee silence**, **defensiveness** and **emotional contagion** are neighbouring dynamics.[^s2][^s6]
