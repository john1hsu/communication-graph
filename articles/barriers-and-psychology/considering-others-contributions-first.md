steps:
  - Before anyone states or estimates their own share of a piece of team work, write down the name of every person who contributed, including people outside the core team.
  - Next to each name, note one or two concrete things that person did, such as drafts written, analysis run, approvals chased or customers handled.
  - Only after the list is done, write your own contributions in the same concrete terms and estimate your share.
  - If people give percentages, add them up; a total well above 100% is a signal that someone's work is being overlooked, not that someone is lying.
  - In large teams, split the review by sub-team or workstream and make assignments explicit, so each person has fewer colleagues' contributions to recall.
  - When the list shows an uneven split, talk about how work will be divided next time rather than letting the person who carried more quietly resent it.
example:
  situation: A project lead is drafting the end-of-quarter update to a director about a launch that five people worked on.
  before: "I drove the launch end to end: I built the rollout plan, fixed the pricing page and got legal sign-off. Shipped on time."
  after: "The launch shipped on time. Ana built the analytics dashboard, Raj rewrote the pricing page, Mei ran the legal review, and Tom handled the support handoff. I owned the rollout plan and coordinated the go/no-go calls."
  why: Listing colleagues first surfaces work the lead had half forgotten, gives the director an accurate picture of who to recognise, and still states the lead's real role plainly.

# Considering others' contributions first

Considering others' contributions first is a simple ordering rule for any conversation about credit: before you judge, report or negotiate your own share of shared work, you go through each collaborator and what they did.[^s1] It targets egocentric over-claiming, the tendency for team members' self-estimated shares to add up to well over 100% because each person's own work is the easiest to remember.[^s1][^s2] Researchers call the manipulation "unpacking" others' contributions or an other-focused assessment.[^s1]

## Origin

The technique comes from experimental research on egocentric responsibility judgments led by Nicholas Epley and colleagues. In 2006 Eugene Caruso, Epley and Max Bazerman published four studies in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* in which some participants estimated only their own share of a group effort, while others first listed their collaborators and estimated each person's share.[^s1] The groups ranged from co-authors of published organisational-behaviour papers to lab groups writing a short essay together.[^s1] A 2016 follow-up by Juliana Schroeder, Caruso and Epley linked over-claiming to group size and again found that explicitly considering others' contributions reduced it.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Credit is one of the most charged topics at work, and most disputes about it are not deliberate grabs. Schroeder and colleagues found that MBA study groups' self-estimates regularly summed to more than 100%, with teams of eight or more claiming over 140% in total, and that people were surprised by how much they over-claimed.[^s3] The researchers trace this to egocentrism: in bigger groups there is more of other people's work to overlook.[^s2][^s3] Left unchecked, the bias shows up in status updates that over-weight the author, retrospectives where everyone feels under-thanked, and self-assessments that managers have to discount.[^s2][^s3]

## How to apply it

Use it whenever you are about to describe your role in team work: a status update to a senior stakeholder, a performance self-review, an author-order discussion or a project wrap-up.[^s1][^s3] List the people, then their concrete contributions, then your own.[^s1] Schroeder recommends having people assess others' contributions before their own, and also suggests smaller sub-teams and explicit assignments so that contributions are easier to track.[^s3] In a written update, this ordering also makes recognition visible to the reader, which a self-focused summary hides.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- **Expecting it to make everyone happier.** Caruso and colleagues found that considering others reduced over-claiming in every study but lowered enjoyment and interest in future collaboration among the people who had contributed the most, especially in cooperative groups rewarded as a whole.[^s1] Seeing an uneven split clearly can sting, so pair the exercise with a forward-looking talk about dividing work.[^s1]
- **Treating a total above 100% as dishonesty.** The research points to memory and attention, not lying, so use the gap to surface overlooked work rather than to accuse.[^s2][^s3]
- **Doing it only in your head.** The studies asked people to name collaborators and estimate each share explicitly; a vague nod to "the team" does not do the same work.[^s1]

## Evidence

The core benefit is consistent across studies: other-focused assessments cut self-claims among academic co-authors, students and lab groups, and the 2016 work replicated the pattern in field data and in two experiments that varied group size directly.[^s1][^s2] For example, in the 2006 co-author study, adjusted claims fell from about 140% under self-focus to about 123% under other-focus.[^s1] The status here is *mixed* rather than *robust* because the effects have costs: lower satisfaction for high contributors in cooperative groups, while competitive groups showed no such cost.[^s1] Most studies come from one research network, the samples were mainly academics and students, and moderators such as power or liking for teammates have not been tested.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a structured form of perspective-taking aimed at other people's work rather than their thoughts.[^s1]
- **Addresses** egocentric bias, specifically its over-claiming form in shared work.[^s1][^s2]
- **Demonstrated by** Caruso, Epley and Bazerman's 2006 studies, which showed both the reduction in over-claiming and its costs for high contributors.[^s1]
- **Supported by** Schroeder, Caruso and Epley's 2016 group-size studies, where considering others' contributions again reduced over-claiming.[^s2]
- **Applied in** team projects, from co-authored papers to group assignments.[^s1]
- **Related:** perspective-getting (asking others what they did rather than guessing), and the feedback conversations and one-on-one meetings where credit and workload are usually discussed.[^s1][^s3]
