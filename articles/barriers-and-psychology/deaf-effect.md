# Deaf effect

The deaf effect is what happens when bad news is delivered but not received. A decision maker does not hear a warning, ignores it, or overrules it, and keeps the project, plan or deal on its current course.[^s3] The term comes from research on information-systems projects that run far over time and budget. There, a sponsor or project owner may get a clear risk warning from an auditor, tester or team member and still press ahead.[^s1][^s2] Where the MUM effect is about messengers who stay quiet, the deaf effect is about listeners who do not take in what they are told.[^s6]

## Origin

Mark Keil and Daniel Robey discussed the deaf effect, citing earlier work, in a 2001 *Communications of the ACM* article on whistle-blowing in troubled software projects. Auditors in their study said senior executives received their reports but played down how serious the problems were.[^s6] Michael Cuellar, Keil and Richard Johnson turned it into an experimental research topic. Their 2006 paper drew on Miceli and Near's theory of whistle-blowing effectiveness and on decision-making research, and Cuellar's 2009 Georgia State dissertation added further experiments and a case study.[^s1][^s3] Arno Nuijten's 2012 Erasmus University dissertation and later work with Keil and Harry Commandeur focused on how the messenger is seen.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The deaf effect is one proposed explanation for escalation of commitment: continuing to pour money and people into a failing course of action.[^s1][^s3] It also closes a feedback loop. In Keil and Robey's cases, auditors who escalated over a manager's head did not always succeed, and in one case the auditor was told the company would no longer need them.[^s6] People who see a warning ignored learn that warning is pointless, so the deaf effect feeds the silence and the MUM effect that leaders then complain about.[^s6][^s5] The cost is usually paid later, when options are fewer.[^s1]

## Evidence

The research base is small, mostly from one network of information-systems researchers, and mostly role-play experiments with students. Hence `mixed`: the patterns are consistent, but no large independent replication was found this run.[^s4][^s2]

- **Relevance is the gate.** In Cuellar, Keil and Johnson's role-play experiment, decision makers who judged a bad-news message relevant were willing to de-escalate. The messenger's credibility and gender were key to whether the message was judged relevant.[^s1] A 105-student experiment reported at AMCIS 2007 found reporter credibility, the reporter's role, and the decision maker's risk perception drove perceived relevance.[^s4]
- **When deafness is likelier.** Cuellar's dissertation (three experiments and a case study) found decision makers more often treated bad news as irrelevant when the reporter lacked credibility and the risk of failure seemed low. Illusion of control, the reporter's formal role and a highly political environment all increased the effect.[^s3]
- **Partner or opponent.** In two experiments, Nuijten, Keil and Commandeur found warnings from a messenger seen as a collaborative partner got more weight, raised perceived risk and lowered willingness to continue. A strong sense of control over the project strengthened this effect, and framing outcomes as losses weakened it.[^s2]
- **In the field.** Interviews with 11 senior internal auditors in the Netherlands, in a 2016 working paper, sorted deaf responses into not hearing (cancelled meetings, unanswered calls), ignoring (disputing credibility or relevance) and overruling (delay, procedural detours, no formal response). It is retrospective and cannot show cause and effect.[^s5]

## How to spot it

In yourself, the signs are reacting to the messenger rather than the message. You ask "who are they to say this?" before "is it true?" You treat your own sense of control as evidence that the risk is low.[^s3][^s2] In an organisation, look for warnings that get a meeting but no decision, findings sent back for "more analysis" more than once, and auditors or quality staff who are no longer invited.[^s5] When several people give a sponsor conflicting accounts, the sponsor may pick the most comfortable one.[^s6]

## How to fix it

**If you receive warnings:**

1. Judge relevance separately from the source. Write down what would have to be true for the warning to be right, before you weigh who sent it.[^s1][^s4]
2. Treat high confidence as a reason to look harder. Illusion of control makes deafness more likely.[^s3][^s2]
3. Close the loop on every formal warning with a decision and a date: act, test, or decline with reasons. Silence and endless procedure are deafness too.[^s5]
4. Keep reviewers independent of the people whose projects they assess, and make their reports hard to ignore.[^s6]

**If you deliver warnings:**

1. Make clear you share the decision maker's goal. Warnings from people seen as partners carry more weight.[^s2]
2. State what will be lost if nothing changes. Loss framing weakened the partner-versus-opponent gap in the experiments.[^s2]
3. Agree your role at the start. Auditors who drifted from partner into operations and then had to report bad news were seen as betraying the team.[^s5]
4. If you are not heard, use an agreed route upward, but expect leapfrogging to strain the relationship.[^s6][^s5]

## Connections

- **Causes** escalation of commitment, the continued investment in a failing course that the research set out to explain.[^s1][^s3]
- **Demonstrated by** Cuellar, Keil and Johnson's 2006 role-play experiment[^s1] and Nuijten, Keil and Commandeur's 2016 experiments on partner versus opponent messengers.[^s2]
- **Undermines** escalation communication: an auditor going over a manager's head did not always get a hearing.[^s6]
- **Broader:** upward communication, the flow of information from staff to those who decide.[^s6]
- **Related:** the MUM effect and employee silence are the sender-side problems the deaf effect teaches.[^s6][^s5] Credibility shapes whether warnings are judged relevant.[^s1] Status reporting, delivering bad news, psychological safety, defensiveness and active listening are the practices and conditions around a warning.[^s2][^s3]
