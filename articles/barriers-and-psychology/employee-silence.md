# Employee silence

Employee silence is the deliberate withholding of ideas, concerns, problem information or disagreement from people at work who could do something about it, most often one's own manager or more senior leaders.[^s1] It is not the same as having nothing to say: the silent employee has noticed a mistake, a risk or a better way and keeps it to themselves.[^s1][^s4] Typical cases include not telling a boss that a colleague is struggling, not challenging a decision you think is wrong, and not mentioning a process that wastes everyone's time.[^s1]

## Origin

NYU Stern researchers Elizabeth Wolfe Morrison and Frances Milliken put the topic on the management research agenda around 2000, describing silence as a collective barrier to organisational change. Their 2003 interview study with Patricia Hewlin, of 40 employees in consulting, finance, pharmaceuticals, media and advertising, made it concrete.[^s1] Amy Edmondson and James Detert then studied the situational and taken-for-granted side of the problem. Their 2011 *Academy of Management Journal* paper named "implicit voice theories": unspoken rules about when speaking up in a hierarchy is risky.[^s2][^s6] Later work by Morrison and colleagues added a feeling of powerlessness as a key driver.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Good news travels upward easily; problems do not. Leaders who hear mainly the former make decisions on a distorted picture, and errors go undetected until they are expensive.[^s1] Silence is often shared: in the 2003 study, three quarters of those who had kept quiet said colleagues knew about the issue and were also holding back.[^s1] The person pays too. A meta-analysis links silence to lower well-being, weaker job attitudes and lower performance,[^s3] and a separate meta-analysis and panel study found that silence is tied to burnout more strongly than voice is.[^s4] Edmondson and Detert add that people feel frustrated and hurt by their own silence, which suggests fear rather than disengagement is driving it.[^s6]

## Evidence

- **Prevalence and reasons.** In Milliken, Morrison and Hewlin's interviews, 34 of 40 people (85%) recalled at least one important issue they had not raised with their boss. The commonest reasons were fear of being labelled a troublemaker or complainer, fear of damaging relationships, a belief that nothing would change, and fear of retaliation.[^s1] The sample was small and recruited from part-time MBA classes, so the percentages are illustrative rather than population estimates.[^s1]
- **Implicit rules.** Detert and Edmondson's four studies, starting with 190 interviews at a multinational and ending with survey measures tested on more than 300 adults, found five implicit voice theories. These beliefs predicted silence over and above personality and workplace climate.[^s2]
- **Power and openness.** Across a lab experiment and two field surveys, Morrison, See and Pan found that feeling powerless increased silence, and that the effect weakened when employees saw their supervisor as open to input.[^s5]
- **Meta-analytic picture.** A 2022 meta-analysis of 168 samples and about 63,000 employees found silence related to leader behaviour, personal dispositions and job beliefs. Inhibition-related factors such as psychological safety explained more of silence than of voice, and different forms of silence predicted outcomes even after voice was accounted for.[^s3] Sherf, Parke and Isaakyan's meta-analysis put the average correlation between voice and silence at only about -0.15, so they are separate behaviours rather than two ends of one scale.[^s4]

Evidence status is `mixed`: the pattern is consistent, but most studies are correlational and self-reported, and effects depend on the leader, the issue and the setting.[^s3][^s6]

## How to spot it

In yourself: you rehearse the concern privately, tell a peer, then say nothing in the meeting; or you decide "it isn't my place" without having tested that belief.[^s2][^s1] In a team: meetings with no pushback, problems that surface first through customers or auditors, ideas that circulate in side chats but never reach a decision-maker, and newcomers who learn quickly what not to say.[^s1][^s6] As a leader: if you rarely hear bad news, do not read that as good news.[^s6]

## How to fix it

**If you are holding something back:**

1. Name the reason. Is it a real, specific risk, or an inherited rule about hierarchy? Implicit voice theories often go untested.[^s2]
2. Pick the person and setting. Willingness to speak depends on the specific boss and venue; a one-to-one is often safer than a large meeting.[^s6]
3. Raise it as a problem you want solved, with the impact and a suggestion, and use structured escalation or graded assertiveness if it is a safety or quality issue.[^s1][^s6]

**If you lead people:**

1. Ask for problems explicitly and often, including informally, rather than relying on an open-door policy or suggestion box.[^s6]
2. Reward the messenger. Thank people for raising issues, act on them or explain why not, and never let speaking up earn a "troublemaker" label.[^s1][^s6]
3. Reduce powerlessness and signal openness, since both blunt the urge to stay quiet.[^s5]
4. Provide a route outside the normal chain of command, such as an ombudsperson, for issues people will not raise with their own manager.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Demonstrated by** Milliken, Morrison and Hewlin's 2003 interview study of what employees withhold and why.[^s1]
- **Explained by** implicit voice theories, the taken-for-granted rules of self-censorship,[^s2] and by low psychological safety, which the 2022 meta-analysis found to be one of the strongest antecedents of silence.[^s3]
- **Supported by** Hao and colleagues' 2022 meta-analysis of antecedents and outcomes.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with** employee voice: the two are related but distinct behaviours with different causes and effects.[^s4]
- **Undermines** group decision-making, because leaders decide on information from which problems have been filtered out.[^s1]
- **Related:** the MUM effect is the bad-news-specific form; upward dissent, issue selling, escalation and graded assertiveness are ways to break silence; power distance makes it more likely; the deaf effect is the leader-side failure that teaches people to stay quiet.[^s1][^s5]
