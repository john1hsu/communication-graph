steps:
  - List what you do to stay safe in the situations you dread - staying quiet, scripting sentences, keeping answers short, avoiding eye contact, asking questions so you never talk about yourself - and ask of each one whether you would feel more anxious without it.
  - Pick one upcoming, real conversation and write down your specific prediction (for example, "if I speak without notes, I will go blank and people will think I am incompetent") and how strongly you believe it, out of 100.
  - Decide in advance what evidence would confirm or disconfirm the prediction, such as what people actually say or do, not how anxious you feel.
  - Have the conversation without the chosen safety behaviour, and put your attention on the other people and the topic rather than on monitoring yourself.
  - Afterwards, record what actually happened, compare it with your prediction and re-rate how much you believe it; if possible, check with a trusted colleague or a recording rather than your memory of how you felt.
  - Repeat with the next safety behaviour, dropping each as fully and as early as you can bear rather than keeping a reduced version indefinitely.
example:
  situation: A data analyst dreads the weekly team meeting and usually says one pre-scripted sentence, then stays quiet with camera angled away.

# Dropping safety behaviours

Safety behaviours are the small, often invisible habits people use to get through a feared social situation without being judged: staying quiet, rehearsing what to say, keeping eye contact brief, steering the conversation onto others, or dressing so as not to stand out.[^s1] What makes something a safety behaviour is its purpose - it is done to stop a feared outcome - so the same action can be ordinary for one person and protective for another.[^s1] Dropping safety behaviours is the deliberate practice of doing the conversation without them, usually framed as a small experiment that tests the feared prediction.[^s1]

## Origin

The idea comes from the cognitive model of social anxiety published by David M. Clark and Adrian Wells in 1995, which argued that people with social anxiety focus attention on themselves, judge how they look from how anxious they feel, and lean on safety behaviours that keep the fear going.[^s5] Clark's cognitive therapy for social anxiety disorder turns this into an early in-session experiment: the person holds one conversation while self-focused and using their usual safety behaviours, then a second while absorbed in the conversation and without them, and compares how each felt and looked.[^s5] Public clinical services now teach the same steps as self-help; the Western Australian Centre for Clinical Interventions, for example, sets out identifying safety behaviours, dropping them, and running written behavioural experiments.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Safety behaviours backfire in two ways. They stop people from ever testing their fears, so the relief gets credited to the behaviour and the dependence grows; and they can create the very outcome they are meant to prevent, such as a manager growing frustrated with someone who never contributes.[^s1] They also pull attention inward, away from the conversation.[^s1] In a 2019 experiment, students who were asked to use avoidance-type safety behaviours (talking less, holding back) in a get-to-know-you conversation felt more anxious, and their partners liked them less, enjoyed the conversation less and were less keen to talk again.[^s3] A 2023 study of adults with social anxiety disorder found that conversation partners rated them as less likeable, and that the gap was carried by inhibiting behaviours such as staying quiet and holding back.[^s4] At work, where speaking up, networking and interviews all depend on being seen as engaged, those habits quietly cost people credibility and connection.

## How to apply it

Start by noticing: write down what you do to reduce anxiety, avoid attention or seem better to others, because many safety behaviours are habitual and subtle.[^s1] Then turn one upcoming conversation into a test: write the specific prediction, rate your belief in it, plan the conversation without the safety behaviour, decide in advance what evidence would count, and record the outcome afterwards.[^s1] During the conversation, aim attention outward - at the person and the topic - because the self-focused, evaluate-yourself mode is itself part of the problem.[^s5] Where you can, check the result against something other than your feelings: in Clark's therapy, people watch a recording of both conversations after predicting how they will look, and 98% in one clinical series rated themselves more favourably after watching.[^s5] Clinical guidance prefers dropping safety behaviours outright; if that is too hard, phase them out, starting with the easiest.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is swapping one safety behaviour for a subtler one, such as giving up notes but silently rehearsing every sentence instead; reviews note that covert safety behaviours are easy to miss and rarely measured.[^s2] A second is keeping a "reduced dose" indefinitely: a 2016 review found the evidence for planned, gradual fading inconclusive and recommended removing safety behaviours as quickly as people are willing.[^s2] A third is treating all self-presentation as harmful. In the 2019 experiment, impression-management behaviours such as checking how you are coming across raised the person's own anxiety but did not hurt partners' ratings the way avoidance did, so withdrawing from the conversation is the priority to drop.[^s3] Finally, people often judge the experiment by how anxious they felt, which is exactly the bias the technique is meant to correct.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is promising but mixed rather than settled. Several social-anxiety studies found that eliminating safety behaviours improved outcomes, but one found that safety behaviours, while linked to poorer social performance, also helped people approach situations at all.[^s2] Many studies were brief, single-session laboratory tasks with small or non-clinical samples and short follow-up.[^s2] The 2019 experiment used undergraduates and manipulated adding safety behaviours rather than dropping them,[^s3] and the 2023 study was cross-sectional and small, with groups that differed in age and education.[^s4] The technique is a self-help and coaching tool for everyday nerves; persistent, impairing social anxiety calls for treatment from a qualified clinician, where it is used inside a fuller therapy.[^s5]

## Connections

Dropping safety behaviours **addresses** [[social-anxiety]] and the self-protective **safety-behaviours** it produces, which the anxiety literature identifies as a key maintaining factor.[^s1][^s2] It is **explained by** the **clark-wells-cognitive-model**, which places self-focused attention and safety behaviours at the centre of why social fear persists.[^s5] The effect of avoidance-type behaviours on how others see you is **demonstrated by** Gray, Beierl and Clark's 2019 experiment, and **supported by** Dabas and colleagues' 2023 study of likeability.[^s3][^s4] It **complements** [[self-recording-playback]], because watching a recording is how Clark's therapy checks the prediction against reality rather than against feelings.[^s5] Its broader home is **exposure-therapy**, since safety behaviours are judged largely by how they interfere with learning during exposure; it sits alongside [[systematic-desensitization]] and [[anxiety-reappraisal]] for [[speaking-anxiety]].[^s2]
