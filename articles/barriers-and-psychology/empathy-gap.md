# Hot-cold empathy gap

The hot-cold empathy gap is the difficulty of appreciating how much an emotional or physical state changes what people feel, want and do when you are not in that state yourself.[^s2] A "cold" (calm, rested, included) person underestimates the pull of a "hot" state such as fear, anger, fatigue or social rejection, and a person in a hot state overestimates how stable their current feelings are.[^s2] Because we predict other people's reactions by first imagining our own, our current state leaks into those predictions.[^s1] At work it shows up when a relaxed manager sends a curt message about a layoff, a well-rested reviewer reads a tired colleague's error as carelessness, or an angry sender fires off an email they would never write calm.

## Origin

Behavioural economist George Loewenstein developed the idea in the 1990s; a 2013 review by Leaf Van Boven, Loewenstein, David Dunning and Loran Nordgren traces the concept to his 1996 work on visceral factors and to later papers with Ted O'Donoghue, Matthew Rabin and David Schkade.[^s2] Van Boven and Loewenstein showed in 2003 that the gap is social as well as personal: people asked how lost hikers would feel projected their own momentary thirst onto them.[^s1] The 2013 review formalised a "dual judgment" model in which we first predict our own reaction and then adjust, too little, for how others differ.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Communicators routinely talk across a state difference: the person delivering bad news is calm, the person receiving it is not. Nordgren's dissertation found that people who were not fatigued or in pain explained someone else's poor performance by low motivation or discipline, while people in that state blamed the circumstances, and simply telling the cold group to empathise did not close the gap.[^s4] In negotiation, buyer's agents who did not own the item underestimated the owner's selling price, made offers that were too low, and had only about a quarter accepted; giving agents an item of their own raised their offers and their earnings.[^s2] Even shared history is no guarantee: a 2024 set of three studies in the *Journal of Organizational Behavior* found that colleagues who had once endured the same kind of distress offered compassion that sufferers rated as less helpful, partly because responders focused on themselves and validated the sufferer's distress less.[^s5]

## Evidence

In Van Boven and Loewenstein's exercise study (N = 47), people surveyed just after a workout felt thirstier and predicted that stranded hikers would be more bothered by thirst; their own self-predictions fully mediated the effect.[^s1] Nordgren, Banas and MacDonald (2011) found that people briefly excluded in an online ball-toss game rated social pain as worse than included people did, observers on a different team underestimated an excluded partner's pain, and excluded teachers recommended more support for bullied students.[^s3] The evidence is consistent in direction but has limits: many studies use small student samples and correlational path models, and hot-to-cold gaps have fewer demonstrations than cold-to-hot ones.[^s1][^s2] A 2015 doctoral study of 106 children found no empathy gap for social pain after the same exclusion manipulation, though its author notes limited power.[^s6] No large multi-lab replication was found, so the status here is `mixed`.

## How to spot it

Watch for confident statements about how someone "should" feel, especially when you are calm and they are not.[^s2] Other signs are explaining a stressed colleague's lapse by character rather than circumstance, and skipping acknowledgement because a change "isn't a big deal".[^s4] Leaning on "I've been through this, so I know" is also a warning sign, because remembered distress tends to fade and pull attention toward your own story.[^s5] Excluded people in one study later recalled their mood as better than it had been, so memory of your own past pain is an unreliable guide.[^s3]

## How to fix it

- Ask before you assume: invite the other person to describe how the change, workload or decision is landing, then reflect it back.[^s5]
- Separate your state from the decision: if you are angry or anxious, draft the message but delay sending until you are calm, since hot states make current feelings seem permanent.[^s2]
- Get some relevant experience where you can, such as shadowing a frontline shift before rewriting its procedures; small doses of experience reduced the gap in the negotiation studies.[^s2]
- When you have "been there", keep the focus on the other person and validate their account rather than retelling yours.[^s5]
- Use awareness as a prompt to correct, not as the cure: assume their experience is probably worse than it looks, then check, since a bare instruction to empathise did not close the gap in the lab.[^s3][^s4]

Example: a team lead announcing a reorganisation by email writes "This is a small structural tweak; nothing changes day to day." After asking two team members how the rumours were landing, she rewrites it to name what is changing, acknowledge that reporting-line changes are unsettling, and offer one-to-one time that week, the kind of correction the empathy-gap researchers suggest for an employer who underestimates staff stress.[^s2]

## Connections

The gap is a form of egocentric bias: we anchor on our own current state and adjust too little, the same mechanism behind the curse of knowledge and the illusion of transparency.[^s2] It was introduced by George Loewenstein and demonstrated in the hiker and social-pain studies.[^s1][^s2][^s3] It undermines workplace compassion when responders project their own remembered experience.[^s5] Perspective-getting, which means asking rather than imagining, and a good support response are the natural counter-moves, and delivering bad news is the context where the gap does most damage.[^s2][^s5] A null result with children challenges how general the social-pain version is.[^s6]
