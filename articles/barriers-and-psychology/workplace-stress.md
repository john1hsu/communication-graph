# Workplace stress

Workplace stress is the harmful reaction people have when the pressures and demands placed on them at work outrun their ability to cope; the UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) describes it as an adverse reaction to excessive pressure or other demands.[^s4] Pressure itself is not the problem. Stress begins when demands no longer match a person's skills, time and support, which is why HSE pairs the definition with remedies such as planning, training and support.[^s4] For communicators it matters twice over: many of the demands that tip people into stress travel through messages, meetings and announcements, and a stressed colleague reads, listens and responds differently from a calm one.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Research on job stress grew out of occupational health psychology, which treats strain as the result of how work is designed rather than only as a personal weakness.[^s3] The most widely used modern account is the job demands-resources (JD-R) model associated with Arnold Bakker and Evangelia Demerouti: excessive demands wear people down through a health-impairment process, while resources such as support, control and clear roles feed a separate motivational process.[^s3] Regulators turned this kind of thinking into practice. HSE's Management Standards name six areas of work design that drive stress: demands, control, support, relationships, role and change.[^s5] At least four of them - support, relationships, role clarity and how change is shared - are largely matters of communication.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Communication habits can be stressors in their own right. In a controlled experiment, Gloria Mark and colleagues had 48 people answer a batch of work emails while a "supervisor" interrupted them by phone or instant message about every two minutes.[^s1] Interrupted participants finished faster with no loss of quality, but reported clearly more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort than when they worked uninterrupted; the topic of the interruption made little difference.[^s1] In short, people absorb interruptions by speeding up, and they pay for it in strain.[^s1]

Availability expectations matter as well as actual workload. Across three studies, including one of 138 employee-partner pairs, William Becker and colleagues found that an organization's expectation that staff monitor email after hours raised anxiety even beyond the time people actually spent on email.[^s2] That anxiety was linked to poorer health and lower relationship satisfaction, and the effects crossed over to employees' partners.[^s2] A 2024 meta-analysis of prospective studies in European healthcare found that role stress - conflicting or unclear expectations - predicted later strain (r = .21), as did negative relationships with colleagues (r = .14), while support and control predicted less strain and higher motivation.[^s3]

## How to spot it

- **Speed without depth.** Replies get shorter and faster while effort and frustration rise, the pattern interrupted workers showed in the lab.[^s1]
- **Always-on anxiety.** People apologise for slow evening replies, or check messages on holiday, because they are unsure what is expected.[^s2]
- **"Whose job is this?" moments.** Repeated confusion about ownership, or two people given the same task, signals role stress.[^s3][^s5]
- **Change by rumour.** Staff learn about reorganisations informally and spend meetings speculating rather than working.[^s5]
- **Withdrawal.** Motivation dips and people stop volunteering ideas, consistent with the link between hindrance demands and lower motivation.[^s3]

## How to fix it

- **State reply-time norms.** Say which channels need same-day answers and which can wait, and if a role truly needs after-hours cover, write it into the role or rotate it rather than leaving it implied.[^s2]
- **Batch instead of interrupting.** Collect non-urgent questions into one message or a standing check-in so colleagues can finish focused work.[^s1]
- **Make roles explicit.** Agree who owns which decisions and deliverables, and remove conflicting assignments.[^s3][^s5]
- **Explain change early.** Share what is changing, why and when, and give people a chance to ask questions and be consulted.[^s5]
- **Open the conversation.** Encourage someone who seems to be struggling to talk to their line manager or another trusted contact, and use structured conversations to agree an action plan.[^s4]

**Example.** *Before:* a team lead fires off questions in chat throughout the day and sends late-night emails with no stated expectation. Team members answer within minutes, including at 11 p.m. *After:* the lead batches questions into a morning message, adds "no reply needed until tomorrow" to evening emails, and names one on-call person per week. *Why:* this removes both the stream of interruptions and the vague availability expectation, the two communication stressors the studies above isolated.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

The core claim is well supported: across many prospective studies, higher job demands predict later strain and job resources predict both lower strain and higher motivation.[^s3] The effects are modest, however (correlations mostly between .10 and .25), and the 2024 meta-analysis was limited to European healthcare workers.[^s3] The communication-specific findings rest on narrower evidence: the interruption experiment used 48 mostly student participants in a single lab session, and its authors could not say whether people adapt over time.[^s1] The email-expectation studies used self-report and online samples in the United States.[^s2] The status here is therefore `mixed`: a reliable general relationship, with effect sizes that vary by setting and with communication levers that are promising but less often tested in field interventions.[^s1][^s2][^s3]

## Connections

- **Explained by** the job demands-resources model, in which demands drive a health-impairment process and resources drive motivation.[^s3]
- **Demonstrated by** Mark, Gudith and Klocke (2008), where interruptions raised stress, frustration and effort.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Becker and colleagues (2019), where after-hours email expectations raised anxiety for employees and their partners.[^s2]
- **Supported by** the Marzocchi and colleagues (2024) meta-analysis, and it **undermines** employee engagement, since hindrance and threat demands predicted lower later motivation.[^s3]
- **Related:** information overload, one of its main communication causes; setting reply-time expectations and scheduled inbox checks, which reduce always-on pressure; role ambiguity and the RACI matrix, which clarifies ownership; and mindfulness, an individual-level remedy.[^s2][^s3][^s5]
