steps:
  - Set a fixed slot of five to fifteen minutes a day - before your first meeting works well - and use a short guided audio or app rather than relying on willpower.
  - Practise a three-minute breathing space twice a day - notice what you are thinking and feeling, narrow attention to the breath, then widen it to the whole body - so the skill exists before you need it.
  - Pick one routine work task, such as opening your inbox or walking to a meeting room, and do it with full attention as an informal daily practice.
  - In a tense conversation, use the trigger as a cue - feel your feet or one breath - and name the thought to yourself as a thought ("I'm having the thought she's blaming me") before you reply.
  - From that pause, choose your next move deliberately - ask a question, restate the issue or propose a time to continue - rather than defending or going quiet.
  - After a hard conversation, spend two minutes noting what you felt, where in the body, and what you did with it, to see whether your reactions are getting more spacious over weeks.
example:
  situation: In a project review, a peer from another team says in front of the group that the analyst's forecast "clearly wasn't checked" before it went to the client.

  after: "Let's look at it - which number worries you? If it's the Q3 line, I can walk through how I got it, and we can agree on a check before the next client send."

# Mindfulness training

Mindfulness training is brief, repeated practice in paying attention to what is happening right now - your breath, body sensations, thoughts and feelings - in a non-judging, accepting way.[^s1] At work its value for communication is the gap it builds between a trigger and your reply: instead of acting out a first reaction, you notice it, treat it as a passing thought or feeling, and choose what to say.[^s3][^s4] It is a practice that develops the broader skill of emotion regulation rather than a script for any single conversation.[^s1]

## Origin

Workplace programmes adapt exercises from two clinical courses, Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), which normally run for eight weeks with a trained teacher.[^s1] Organisational researchers then tested much shorter, self-guided versions: in 2013 Ute Hülsheger and colleagues at Maastricht University gave service employees a ten-working-day self-training programme built around a body scan, a three-minute breathing space twice a day, mindful routine activities and loving-kindness meditation.[^s1] In 2020 Adam Kay and Daniel Skarlicki at the University of British Columbia extended the question from wellbeing to conflict, testing whether a short online mindfulness programme changed how people handle disagreements at work.[^s3][^s4]

## Why it matters

Many communication failures start as an emotion that gets ahead of the person feeling it: a defensive reply, a sarcastic email, or a decision to say nothing. Hülsheger's team found that more mindful employees relied less on surface acting - faking the expected emotion over what they really felt - and that lower surface acting explained their lower emotional exhaustion, both in a five-day diary study of 219 employees and in a randomised field experiment.[^s1] In Kay and Skarlicki's work, people higher in mindfulness collaborated more and avoided conflict less, and cognitive reappraisal - seeing the conflict differently - accounted for the rise in collaboration.[^s3] A brief training reached the same pattern in an experiment at a large health authority with about 600 staff, compared with both a breathing-exercise group and a no-change control.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Treat it like rehearsal: the skill has to be practised on calm days to be available on hard ones.[^s1] Short, regular doses are the realistic target - the UBC programme combined about five minutes of online training with 10-15 minutes of daily practice, and Hülsheger's programme excluded participants who averaged under six minutes a day.[^s1][^s4] Anchor practice to existing routines, keep one formal exercise (the breathing space) and one informal one (a task done with full attention), and use the moment of feeling triggered in a meeting as the cue to pause and label the thought before replying.[^s1][^s3] The aim is not to feel nothing but to see thoughts as thoughts, so that you can respond to the content of a conflict rather than to the sting of it.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Expecting a one-off session to work in a crisis.** The studied benefits come from daily practice over days or weeks; people who barely practised were the ones the 2013 study had to exclude.[^s1]
- **Using calm as cover for avoidance.** The point is to engage better - Kay and Skarlicki measured less avoidance, not more acceptance of problems - so the pause should end in a question or next step.[^s3]
- **Mistaking it for faking calm.** Pasting on a neutral face over unchanged anger is surface acting, the very strategy mindfulness reduced in the 2013 studies.[^s1]
- **Overselling it to a team.** Mandating it as a cure for burnout or poor performance outruns the evidence, which is inconclusive on both.[^s2][^s5]

## Evidence

The strongest evidence is for wellbeing. A 2019 meta-analysis of workplace mindfulness randomised controlled trials (23 studies) found moderate benefits for stress (g = 0.56), anxiety (g = 0.62), psychological distress (g = 0.69) and wellbeing (g = 0.46), but could not reach a conclusion on burnout, depression (because of publication bias) or work performance (too little data).[^s2] Evidence for communication outcomes is thinner: the conflict findings come from one research programme, and the 2013 field experiment analysed only 64 people against a waitlist control, which its authors note could add expectation effects.[^s1][^s3] More broadly, a 2018 critique by 15 researchers led by Nicholas Van Dam argued that mindfulness is poorly defined, that many trials lack active control groups, that few monitored adverse effects, and that public enthusiasm has outpaced the data.[^s5] The status here is therefore `mixed`: a well-supported stress and emotion-regulation tool with promising but not yet replicated effects on conflict behaviour.[^s2][^s3][^s5]

## Connections

- **Develops** emotion regulation and is **demonstrated by** Hülsheger and colleagues (2013), where mindfulness reduced surface acting and, through it, emotional exhaustion.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Kay and Skarlicki (2020) for conflict management, and **complements** cognitive reappraisal, which carried mindfulness's effect on collaboration.[^s3]
- **Supported by** the Bartlett and colleagues (2019) meta-analysis and **addresses** workplace stress.[^s2]
- **Applied in** difficult conversations and workplace conflict, where brief training raised collaboration and lowered avoidance.[^s4]
- **Challenged by** Van Dam and colleagues' "Mind the hype" critique of definitions, controls and overselling.[^s5]
- **Related:** self-distancing, which also gives an observer's vantage point on your reactions; labeling emotions; cooling-off periods; and defensiveness, the reaction a pause most often catches.[^s1][^s3]
