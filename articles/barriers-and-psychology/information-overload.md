# Information overload

Information overload is what happens when more information arrives, faster or in more tangled form, than a person can make sense of in the time they have. Researchers define it in several ways: as the point where extra information starts to make decisions worse, as supply outrunning processing capacity, as processing demands exceeding the time available, or simply as the strain people report.[^s1] For communicators the lesson is the same under every definition: a message competes with everything else in the reader's day, and past a certain point more words, more recipients and more channels reduce what actually gets understood.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The idea draws on research about the limits of human working memory, such as George Miller's 1956 work, and on organizational theorists of the 1960s and 1970s who described how organizations and people cope when inputs exceed capacity.[^s1] The best-known formal version is an inverted-U curve credited to Schroder, Driver and Streufert (1967): decision performance rises as useful information is added, peaks, then falls; Chewning and Harrell (1990) later placed overload at the point where the amount of information actually integrated into a decision starts to drop.[^s1] Martin Eppler and Jeanne Mengis consolidated three decades of work from organization science, accounting, marketing and information systems into a framework of causes, symptoms and countermeasures, published in *The Information Society* in 2004.[^s1] Peter Roetzel's 2019 bibliometric review updated that picture for the following decade of business research.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Overload damages both the work and the people doing it. Eppler and Mengis list effects on decisions, including lower accuracy, slower choices, superficial analysis, selective omission and misplaced confidence, alongside personal effects such as stress, lower satisfaction and a narrowed view of the problem.[^s1] They group the causes into five families: the information itself (quantity, ambiguity, complexity, poor quality), the person, the task (interruptions, time pressure), the organization (coordination demands, cross-functional teams) and the technology, with email and push notifications named directly.[^s1] For communicators, the information and the interruptions are largely under the sender's control.[^s1]

Email is where most knowledge workers feel it. In a study of professionals at high-tech firms, Barley, Meyerson and Grodal found that time spent on email was linked to a greater sense of overload, and email came to stand as a symbol for all the overload in people's working lives.[^s3] Participants with the most meetings returned to the biggest email backlogs, and some believed messages needed an answer within an hour, which drove constant checking in the evening and early morning.[^s4]

## Evidence

The core claim is well supported: people and groups have finite processing capacity, and decision quality suffers when inputs exceed it.[^s1][^s2] Beyond that, the evidence is uneven. Eppler and Mengis note inconsistent empirical results, partly from methodological problems; disputes over whether the inverted-U holds; competing objective and subjective definitions that make studies hard to compare; and a reliance on laboratory experiments without real organizational context.[^s1] Roetzel's later review still frames the field as a set of separate research streams with open questions rather than a settled model.[^s2]

The workplace evidence also complicates the simple story. Barley and colleagues found that processing more email was linked to a greater feeling of being able to cope, and that hours worked did not explain email-related overload; they argue email partly works as a symbol that diverts attention from other sources of overload.[^s3] On remedies, Kushlev and Dunn ran a two-week field experiment with 124 adults: in the week people checked email only three times a day, daily stress was lower (d = 0.37) than in the week of unlimited checking, even though they handled the same amount of mail.[^s5] The authors call the study exploratory, with a mostly student convenience sample and no true control condition, so the status here is `mixed`: the problem is real, but its threshold, mechanism and best fixes vary by person, task and organization.[^s5]

## How to spot it

Signs in yourself and in the people you write to:[^s1][^s4]

- Messages get skimmed, half-answered or answered late, and decisions are reopened because someone missed a key line.[^s1]
- People ignore low-priority items, filter aggressively, or stop replying to whole channels, which are classic coping reactions to overload.[^s1]
- Inbox and chat backlogs pile up after days of back-to-back meetings.[^s4]
- Team members check messages in the evening or first thing in the morning because they believe a fast reply is expected.[^s4]

## How to fix it

1. Send less, to fewer people: trim distribution lists and cut background the reader does not need to act.[^s1]
2. Compress before you send: lead with a summary or the request, and turn dense data into a short visual where it helps.[^s1]
3. Agree team norms on channels and response times, including which messages can wait until tomorrow, rather than leaving each person to guess.[^s1][^s4]
4. Protect focus by batching: check and process email at set times instead of continuously; in one field experiment this lowered daily stress without reducing the mail handled.[^s5]
5. Treat persistent overload as an organizational signal, not a personal failing: look at meeting load, unclear priorities and coordination demands as well as the inbox.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a central problem in organizational communication, where coordination needs and cross-functional work increase information load.[^s1]
- **Explained by** limited cognitive processing capacity: overload begins where demands exceed what a person can process in the time available.[^s1]
- **Undermines** decision quality, through lower accuracy, slower choices and superficial analysis.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Barley, Meyerson and Grodal's 2011 field study linking time on email to felt overload.[^s3]
- **Supported by** Kushlev and Dunn's 2015 experiment, in which checking email less often reduced daily stress.[^s5]
- **Causes** workplace stress, especially when email piles up behind meetings and fast replies feel mandatory.[^s3][^s4]
- **Related:** email (a leading channel for overload), asynchronous communication and setting reply-time expectations (norms that ease it), bottom line up front and plain language (ways to compress a message), channel selection, meeting agendas and Zoom fatigue.[^s1][^s4]
