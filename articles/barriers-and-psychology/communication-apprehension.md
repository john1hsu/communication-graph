# Communication apprehension

Communication apprehension (CA) is the fear or anxiety a person associates with real or anticipated communication with others, a definition that goes back to James C. McCroskey's 1977 work.[^s1] It is broader than stage fright: McCroskey's standard measure, the PRCA-24, scores apprehension separately in four settings - group discussion, meetings, one-to-one conversation and public speaking - and adds them into a total from 24 to 120.[^s4] At work it shows up less as visible panic than as silence: people with high CA tend to avoid speaking-heavy situations or take passive roles, which in turn slows the growth of their communication skills.[^s1]

## Origin

McCroskey, a communication researcher, treated CA as close to a personality trait rather than a passing state, and built self-report scales to measure it; the PRCA-24 became the most widely used of them.[^s1][^s4] Its published norms come from more than 40,000 college students, with a national sample of non-student adults giving nearly identical results (total mean 65.6, SD 15.3; above 80 counts as high).[^s4] Later work distinguished trait-like CA from situational CA triggered by a particular setting, and public-speaking textbooks now teach four sources: trait, context (formality, novelty, uncertainty), audience (size, status, familiarity) and the unique mix of a given occasion.[^s2][^s3] Late in his career McCroskey argued that CA is largely inherited and that the earlier social-learning account was probably a misstep, a view his colleagues present as resting on some accounts rather than settled fact.[^s6]

## Why it matters

A 2025 paper summarising a survey of more than 3,000 working adults reported that about 20% described high CA and a further 35-40% moderate levels, with apprehension highest among people in their twenties.[^s1] Those numbers mean a typical meeting contains several people for whom the open floor is costly. The cost is not just personal comfort: in a 2024 study of 206 university instructors moved online in spring 2020, apprehension about computer-mediated communication predicted lower job satisfaction, motivation and communication satisfaction even after preparation was controlled for.[^s2] The same authors describe that apprehension as a barrier to communication competence, and cite earlier work linking it to lower participation in virtual teams.[^s2]

## Evidence

The construct and its measure are well established: the PRCA-24 is described as highly reliable, though its four subscores are less reliable than the total.[^s4] Most consequences found so far are correlational, so a quiet colleague's silence cannot be blamed on CA from a survey alone.[^s2] On treatment, a 2025 meta-analysis of 42 public-speaking anxiety interventions, many measured with McCroskey's PRCA, found a large pooled effect (g = 1.17) but very high heterogeneity and moderate average study quality.[^s5] Systematic desensitization reduced both state and trait apprehension in the studies it reviewed, skills training showed a moderate effect, and purely cognitive approaches were mixed.[^s5] A 2025 randomized study of 260 adults found that one online coaching session cut PRCA-24 scores from about 81 to 62, but it had no untreated control and only a two-week follow-up, and its first author runs the training academy involved.[^s1]

## How to spot it

Look for a pattern across settings rather than a single nervous moment.[^s3] Signs include someone who contributes well in writing or one-to-one but goes silent in larger meetings, who turns down presenting or chairing roles, who over-scripts, or who leaves the talking to others when senior people are present - subordinate status and audience size both raise apprehension.[^s3][^s1] In yourself, notice when you choose tasks, channels or even jobs to avoid speaking; that avoidance is the main way CA limits careers.[^s1]

## How to fix it

Start by locating it: the PRCA-24's four subscores show whether the problem is meetings, groups, conversation or presenting, so practice can target the right one.[^s4] Graduated practice in that setting - speaking once early in a small meeting, then in larger ones - follows the logic of desensitization and skills training, the approaches with the clearest support.[^s5] Practise the point aloud beforehand, and reduce novelty and uncertainty by learning the agenda and the room in advance, since those features raise apprehension.[^s3] Because formality, uncertainty, audience size and status all raise apprehension, managers and facilitators can lower the cost for everyone: share questions before the meeting, use rounds, small groups or written input, so that contribution does not depend on comfort with the open floor.[^s3] Where apprehension is severe or spills into daily life, structured programmes or professional help work better than willpower.[^s5]

## Connections

McCroskey introduced the concept and its measurement.[^s1] It causes unequal participation, because apprehensive people avoid or take passive roles in speaking situations,[^s1] and undermines communication competence, as shown for online teaching.[^s2] Public speaking anxiety is its best-known context-specific form, and the evidence base on treating it, such as McWilliam, Beattie and Callow's 2025 meta-analysis, supports targeted practice.[^s5] Anxiety reappraisal and presentation rehearsal are practical remedies, while psychological safety and well-run meetings reduce the situational triggers.[^s3]
