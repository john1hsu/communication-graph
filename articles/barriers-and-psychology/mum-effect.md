# MUM effect

The MUM effect is the reluctance a messenger feels about delivering unwelcome information. The name abbreviates "keeping Mum about Undesirable Messages", and the hesitation shows up as delay, softening, distortion or plain silence.[^s1] At work it matters most when news has to travel upward: the analyst who knows the forecast is wrong, the engineer who knows the release will slip, the account manager who knows the client's pilot is failing. Each tends to wait, hedge or hope the problem fixes itself.[^s2][^s6]

## Origin

Social psychologists Rosen and Tesser described the effect in 1970; Dibble's 2017 paper traces the term and its definition to that work.[^s1] Later researchers split the explanations two ways. One asks whether messengers are managing their public image (not wanting to be linked with bad news) or feeling private discomfort. The other asks whether the concern is for themselves or for the recipient.[^s1] Around 2000, information-systems researchers led by Mark Keil and H. Jeff Smith applied the idea to IT projects. They modelled reluctance to report negative project status by borrowing from whistle-blowing research.[^s2][^s4]

## Why it matters

Large projects rarely fail suddenly. A 2014 review by Keil and colleagues of 14 studies over 15 years found that warning signs usually appear well before a failure. Those signs are muffled by optimistic spin, overconfidence and silence. The authors' first "inconvenient truth" is that executives cannot count on staff to report status accurately or raise problems unprompted.[^s6] Smith, Keil and Depledge argued that this reluctance helps turn troubled projects into runaways. The longer the news waits, the fewer options remain.[^s2] The same review warns that the blockage runs both ways. Executives often discount bad news when they do get it, which teaches people to stay quiet next time.[^s6]

## Evidence

The core finding, that people are slower and less complete with bad news than good, is well supported. The details of why are more mixed.[^s1][^s3]

- **Experiments on project reporting.** Smith, Keil and Depledge (2001) varied how much harm would follow from not reporting and how much wrongdoing was visible. Their model explained a significant share of reluctance, and it worked differently for reporting inside versus outside the organisation.[^s2] Park, Im and Keil (2008) found the whistle-blowing model held in IT projects. Who was at fault (for example, an outside vendor) affected willingness to report both directly and indirectly, while time urgency acted only indirectly.[^s4]
- **Replication.** A 2016 close replication confirmed the central result. People who believe news should be reported feel responsible for reporting it, but only to internal recipients. Several effects did not replicate, including the influence of project risk and wrongdoing, and greater responsibility was linked to *more* reluctance to go through the chain of command.[^s3] Hence `mixed` rather than `robust`.
- **Motive.** In a 2017 experiment with about 300 students, bad news produced far more reluctance than good news. The strongest predictor was fear of distressing the recipient. Fear of blame had a small effect, and fear of looking bad had none. Communication channel did not change reluctance.[^s1] The scenario was imagined and the sample was students, so how far this carries to the workplace is unclear.[^s1]
- **Channel and distortion.** Sussman and Sproull (1999) had 117 students give critical résumé feedback in person, by phone or by email. Senders distorted negative information less by email than face to face or by phone. Positive information was hardly distorted in any channel.[^s5]

## How to spot it

In yourself, the signs are a growing gap between what you know and what you have said. You "wait one more week for data". You lead an update with wins and bury the risk in paragraph four. Or you swap "we will miss the date" for "the date is at risk".[^s6][^s5] In a team, look for status reports that stay green until they suddenly turn red. Look also for issues that outsiders or auditors raise first, and for people who share bad news in hallways but not in the meeting.[^s6] Keil and colleagues add that putting a senior executive in charge of a project can increase misreporting, because the stakes of telling the boss go up.[^s6]

## How to fix it

**If you are the messenger:**

1. Report the news, not your discomfort about it. Lead with what has happened, the impact and what you need, before the history.[^s6]
2. Frame the urgency. Park and colleagues suggest that stressing the short window to fix a defect, before it reaches users, raises willingness to report. Use that argument on yourself and your team.[^s4]
3. Pick a channel that keeps the facts intact. A written note sent before a live conversation can reduce the softening that happens face to face. Then talk it through.[^s5]
4. If your hesitation is mostly concern for the recipient, remember that the distress of late bad news is usually larger.[^s1][^s2]

**If you are the receiver:**

1. Assume you are not hearing everything. Adopt "trust, but verify" and ask directly what could make the date slip.[^s6]
2. React to early bad news in a way that makes the next report more likely. Ask, thank and act, and don't hunt for blame.[^s6][^s2]
3. Give people an internal route that is not only through the direct chain of command, because replication evidence suggests responsibility alone does not overcome reluctance to report up that chain.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Demonstrated by** Smith, Keil and Depledge's 2001 experiment on reluctance to report negative project status.[^s2] Dibble's 2017 study linked the effect to concern for the recipient,[^s1] and Sussman and Sproull's 1999 study showed less distortion of bad news over email.[^s5]
- **Supported by** the 2016 replication by Petter and colleagues, which reproduced the core finding but not every effect.[^s3]
- **Undermines** status reporting, because accurate status depends on people volunteering bad news early.[^s6]
- **Broader:** employee silence, the wider pattern of withholding concerns from those above you.[^s6]
- **Related:** escalation communication and delivering bad news, the practices that counter it.[^s4][^s1] Managing up and psychological safety shape whether people speak.[^s6] The deaf effect is the receiver-side failure to hear bad news.[^s6] Channel selection and bottom line up front put the news where it cannot be missed.[^s5]
