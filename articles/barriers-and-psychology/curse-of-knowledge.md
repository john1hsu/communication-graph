# Curse of knowledge

The curse of knowledge is the difficulty of setting aside what you know when you try to judge what someone else knows. Once you understand a project, a term or a conclusion, you tend to assume your audience understands it too, so you skip steps, lean on internal shorthand and describe ideas at a level of abstraction that only makes sense with your background.[^s1][^s4] It is a barrier rather than a lack of effort: better-informed people often cannot ignore their private information even when doing so would pay off.[^s1]

## Origin

Economists Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein and Martin Weber named the effect in a 1989 *Journal of Political Economy* paper. Standard models of asymmetric information assumed that a better-informed party could accurately predict a less-informed party's judgments; their experiments showed a systematic violation of that assumption, so extra information could leave someone worse off.[^s1] Developmental psychologists later used the same label for a bias in reasoning about other minds: Susan Birch and Paul Bloom reported in 2007 that adults, like young children, let their knowledge of an outcome distort their judgment of what another person believes.[^s2] In management writing, Chip and Dan Heath popularised the term in a 2006 *Harvard Business Review* article on why strategy statements fail to reach the front line.[^s5]

## Why it matters

At work the person explaining is usually the person who knows most: the analyst presenting findings, the engineer writing a handover, the leader announcing a strategy. The Heaths argue that executives compress years of context into phrases such as "unlock shareholder value", which carry rich meaning for them but sound empty to employees who lack that context, so a sound strategy fails to change behaviour.[^s5] The same pattern appears in public-facing writing: a Digital.gov guide notes that readers can say a text is clear yet still misread it or take the wrong action, citing government letters whose timing and terms meant different things to different readers.[^s6]

## Evidence

The core effect has been found in several paradigms. In Camerer and colleagues' experiments, trading in a market cut the bias by roughly half but did not remove it.[^s1] Birch and Bloom found that adults' outcome knowledge biased their false-belief judgments, and that the bias grew when the outcome seemed plausible.[^s2] In four experiments published in 2023, Jonathan Tullis and Brennen Feder found that studying trivia answers made people worse at estimating how many novices would know them.[^s4] The size of the effect is less settled. Rachel Ryskin and Sarah Brown-Schmidt ran seven large-sample experiments on the adult false-belief finding and estimated the true effect at less than half of the originally reported size, warning that its real-world importance may need re-evaluation.[^s3] The status here is therefore `mixed`: the direction is consistent, but magnitudes vary by task and some early estimates were inflated.[^s3]

## How to spot it

Look for signs that a message only works for someone who already shares your context.[^s5][^s6]

- Readers or listeners reply with questions you think the message already answered.
- The message uses acronyms, project code names or abstractions ("drive alignment", "optimise the funnel") without a concrete example.
- You judged it clear by rereading it yourself, which is exactly the judgment the bias distorts.[^s4]

## How to fix it

Trying harder to imagine the novice view is a weak fix: in Tullis and Feder's studies, experimentally reducing reliance on one's own knowledge did not improve estimates, because people lack diagnostic cues about what others know.[^s4] The practical remedy is to get those cues from real readers and to make abstractions concrete.[^s5][^s6]

1. Translate abstract goals into concrete examples, numbers or stories a newcomer could picture.[^s5]
2. Spell out acronyms and the first step a reader should take.[^s6]
3. Run a quick paraphrase test: ask a few people from the target audience (Digital.gov suggests six to nine for formal testing) to read a section and explain it in their own words, record what they say without correcting them, and revise wherever they misread it.[^s6]
4. Ask open-ended follow-ups such as what they would do next, rather than yes/no questions like "Is this clear?".[^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader:** the curse of knowledge is a form of egocentric bias, in which one's own perspective leaks into judgments of others; Ryskin and Brown-Schmidt frame the adult errors as egocentric.[^s3]
- **Explained by theory of mind:** Birch and Bloom treat it as a limit on reasoning about other people's mental states.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by** Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber's 1989 experiments, which named it.[^s1]
- **Challenged by** Ryskin and Brown-Schmidt's 2014 high-powered re-estimate, which found the adult false-belief effect much smaller than first reported.[^s3]
- **Causes abstract language and undermines clarity:** experts default to compressed, general phrasing that loses the audience.[^s5]
- **Related:** the illusion of transparency and the tappers-and-listeners study (separate nodes) are neighbouring ideas about overestimating how well others understand us; audience analysis and plain language are the routine habits that counteract it, and paraphrase testing is one concrete way to check for it.[^s6]
