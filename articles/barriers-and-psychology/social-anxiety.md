# Social anxiety

Social anxiety is fear or anxiety about situations in which other people might watch, judge or evaluate you.[^s1] At work it attaches to familiar moments: presenting, job interviews, meeting new colleagues, answering a question on the spot, or asking a manager for help.[^s1] Most people feel some of it before a high-stakes conversation; it becomes a communication barrier when the fear decides what you say, how you say it, or whether you speak at all. Where the fear is intense, lasts six months or more and interferes with work or relationships, clinicians call it social anxiety disorder, which is a mental-health condition rather than a personality quirk.[^s1]

## Origin

The U.S. National Institute of Mental Health separates the disorder from ordinary shyness: shyness can look similar, but the disorder involves strong, persistent fear that disrupts daily life, and it usually begins in childhood or adolescence.[^s1] The most influential account of why the fear persists is the cognitive model published by Clark and Wells in 1995.[^s5] It proposes that socially anxious people turn their attention inward, use their own feelings of anxiety to judge how they look to others, and rely on "safety behaviours" - such as saying little or rehearsing sentences before speaking - to prevent the disaster they expect.[^s5] Those habits keep the fear alive, because the person never learns that the disaster would not have happened anyway.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Social anxiety is common. In the U.S. National Comorbidity Survey Replication (2001-2003), about 7.1% of adults met criteria for the disorder in the past year and 12.1% at some point in their lives; among those with a past-year diagnosis, nearly 30% had serious impairment.[^s2] Many more people have milder, sub-clinical fear of evaluation. For communication, the cost is double. First, people avoid the very conversations that build careers - speaking up in meetings, networking, interviewing.[^s1] Second, the self-protective habits backfire: in a 2023 study, conversation partners rated people with social anxiety disorder as less likeable and less authentic, and the gap in likeability was explained by "inhibiting" safety behaviours such as staying quiet and holding back.[^s4] In other words, the strategy meant to avoid a bad impression tends to create one.

## Evidence

The core findings are well replicated. A 2014 network meta-analysis of 101 randomised trials (13,164 participants) found that individual cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) had a large effect against waitlist and was one of only two treatment classes to beat an appropriate placebo; the authors recommended it as the first-line treatment, with SSRI medication as the best-supported alternative for people who decline therapy.[^s3] The safety-behaviour mechanism also has converging support: Dabas and colleagues review several studies in which partners found safety-behaviour users less likeable and in which dropping those behaviours improved ratings.[^s4] Limits matter, though. The 2023 study was small, used one judge per participant and unmatched groups.[^s4] Evidence for the video-feedback component of cognitive therapy comes from a single-group pre/post analysis of 47 patients, not a randomised comparison.[^s5] And a 2024 randomised trial of work-focused CBT for 250 unemployed adults with social anxiety found only a small advantage at post-treatment (d = -0.25) that faded as the usual-services group also improved, with no difference on employment outcomes.[^s6] Workplace adjustments help people participate, but they are not treatment.

## How to spot it

Look at behaviour across situations rather than a single nervous moment.[^s1] Common signs include blushing, trembling, a very soft voice or rigid posture; difficulty making eye contact or talking with unfamiliar people; worrying for days or weeks before an event; and picking apart one's own performance afterwards.[^s1] In meetings it often appears as safety behaviours: saying the minimum, scripting and rehearsing remarks, avoiding eye contact, keeping a low profile on camera.[^s5][^s4] In yourself, the strongest clue is a gap between how anxious you felt and how others reacted - patients who watched video of themselves almost always found they came across better than they predicted.[^s5]

## How to fix it

- **Shift attention outward.** In cognitive therapy, clients hold the same conversation twice - once focused inward with their safety behaviours, once focused on the other person without them - and usually feel less anxious and come across better the second time.[^s5] Before a meeting, decide what you want to learn from the other person rather than how you want to appear.
- **Drop one safety behaviour at a time.** Since inhibiting habits drive lower likeability, try asking one question early, offering one opinion unscripted, or keeping eye contact through one answer.[^s4]
- **Check the evidence, not the feeling.** Record a practice presentation or video call and watch it as if it were a colleague's; video feedback corrected people's overly negative self-image in the clinical study.[^s5]
- **Approach rather than avoid.** Graded exposure to feared situations is a core CBT method.[^s1]
- **Get proper help when it is more than nerves.** If fear lasts for months and disrupts work, CBT delivered by a qualified clinician is the best-supported first step.[^s3][^s1]
- **As a manager or facilitator**, lower the cost of evaluation for everyone: share questions in advance, use rounds or written input, and do not cold-call people to perform on the spot - the situations NIMH lists as triggers are exactly these.[^s1]

## Connections

The Clark and Wells cognitive model explains why the fear persists, and that model holds that social anxiety causes safety behaviours which keep it going.[^s5] Those inhibiting behaviours undermine rapport, as Dabas and colleagues demonstrated with confederate likeability ratings.[^s4] The Mayo-Wilson network meta-analysis supports CBT as the primary treatment.[^s3] Communication apprehension and public speaking anxiety are narrower, communication-specific forms; the spotlight effect and the illusion of transparency describe the overestimation of how much others notice; exposure therapy, systematic desensitization and self-recording playback are related remedies.[^s5][^s1]
