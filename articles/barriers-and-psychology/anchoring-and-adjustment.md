# Anchoring and adjustment

Anchoring and adjustment describes how people reach an estimate by starting from a value that is already in front of them and moving away from it, usually not far enough, so the final answer stays biased toward the start.[^s1][^s2] In workplace communication the starting value can be a number someone says first ("this should take two weeks"), last year's budget line, the opening comment in a meeting, or - less obviously - your own understanding of a message you wrote.[^s1][^s3] Whoever sets the first reference point shapes the conversation that follows, and listeners rarely notice how much.

## Origin

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman named the heuristic in their 1974 *Science* paper on judgment under uncertainty, alongside representativeness and availability.[^s1] They described it as a shortcut people use mainly for numerical predictions when a relevant value is available, and argued that such heuristics are usually efficient but produce systematic, predictable errors.[^s1] Nicholas Epley and Thomas Gilovich later separated two cases: anchors handed to us by someone else, and anchors we generate ourselves, such as a known fact or our own perspective. Their 2006 experiments found that with self-generated anchors people adjust step by step and stop at the first value that seems plausible.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Anchors steer workplace numbers. A manager's offhand guess at a deadline, a vendor's opening price or a prior year's headcount can become the reference that every later estimate is measured against, even when better information exists.[^s1][^s5] Mussweiler, Strack and Pfeiffer found the pull held in a real-world study with experts, not only with students guessing trivia.[^s5]

Anchoring also distorts how we judge our own messages. Epley and colleagues showed that when people try to imagine how someone else will read a situation, they start from their own view and adjust away from it incompletely.[^s3] Participants who knew the context behind an ambiguous voicemail expected an uninformed listener to read it much as they did, which is the mechanism behind sending a "clear" email that the recipient finds cryptic or sarcastic.[^s3] Under time pressure the judgments became more egocentric, and an accuracy incentive moved them closer to the truth.[^s3]

## How to counter it in practice

1. **Form your own estimate before you hear anyone else's.** Write down your number, timeline or recommendation before a planning meeting, so the first figure spoken is not the only reference.[^s1][^s5]
2. **Argue against the anchor.** Before you accept or counter a number, list reasons it could be wrong; considering the opposite reduced the anchor's influence for both students and experts.[^s5]
3. **Slow down when you judge other people's understanding.** Adjustment takes time, so a rushed read of how a stakeholder will interpret your message stays closest to your own view.[^s3]
4. **State a range, then pick from it.** In Epley's experiments, people's point estimates sat near the edge of the plausible range closest to their own view, so sketching the range first shows how far your single guess may be leaning.[^s3]
5. **In meetings, vary who speaks first** and collect estimates independently, so one early voice does not set the reference point for the room.[^s1][^s5]

For example, before: a team lead asks, "Marketing thinks the launch needs about four weeks - does that work for engineering?" After: "Before we compare notes, can each team send me its own estimate of the launch effort? Then we'll discuss the gaps." The second version collects independent judgments before any number is spoken, so the first figure cannot frame the rest of the discussion.[^s1][^s5]

## Evidence

Anchoring is one of the best-replicated effects in social psychology. The 2014 Many Labs project ran four classic anchoring questions in 36 samples with more than 6,000 participants; every item replicated in every sample, and the replicated effects were larger than the original estimate, though they varied across samples, perhaps with how much participants already knew.[^s4] Epley and Gilovich found that people can adjust further when they are both able and motivated to search for a better answer.[^s2]

The **mechanism** is less settled than the effect. Mussweiler and Strack's selective accessibility account explains anchoring as information consistent with the anchor coming to mind more easily, rather than as insufficient adjustment.[^s5] A 2025 file-drawer report by Röseler and colleagues ran four preregistered studies (N = 898) and found no evidence that less time to adjust produces stronger anchoring, a core prediction of the insufficient-adjustment model; anchoring itself was robust in the classic designs but vanished or reversed in faster response formats.[^s6] So treat "anchors pull estimates" as robust, and "people adjust from the anchor too little" as one contested explanation.[^s2][^s6]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in their 1974 *Science* paper.[^s1]
- **Supported by** the 2014 Many Labs replication, in which all four anchoring items replicated across 36 samples.[^s4]
- **Demonstrated by** Epley and Gilovich's 2006 experiments on self-generated anchors and by Epley, Keysar, Van Boven and Gilovich's 2004 perspective-taking studies.[^s2][^s3]
- **Causes** the curse of knowledge in communication: informed speakers anchor on what they know and overestimate how clear their message is to listeners who lack that context.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with** the selective accessibility model, which explains the same effect through what comes to mind rather than through adjustment.[^s5]
- **Challenged by** Röseler and colleagues' 2025 preregistered studies, which found no support for the time-based adjustment prediction.[^s6]
- **Related:** the first offer and BATNA in negotiation, which apply anchoring deliberately or defend against it, and the illusion of transparency and spotlight effect, two egocentric biases explained by anchoring on one's own perspective.[^s3]
