# Primacy and recency effects

Primacy and recency effects are order effects: when people take in a sequence of points, arguments, candidates or events, the items at the start or the end of the sequence tend to shape their memory and judgment more than the same items in the middle.[^s4] A primacy effect means the first information dominates; a recency effect means the last information dominates.[^s2] Neither end wins every time. Research on persuasion and belief updating shows that the direction depends on how carefully the audience processes the material and on whether the pieces of evidence agree with each other.[^s2][^s3] For communicators this makes order a barrier to fair, accurate understanding and, used honestly, a design choice.

## Origin

The idea that first impressions set the frame comes from Solomon Asch's 1946 studies of impression formation. One group heard a person described as intelligent, industrious, impulsive, critical, stubborn and envious; another heard the same six traits in reverse. The first group mostly pictured a capable person with some flaws, while the second pictured a troubled person whose abilities were held back, and ambiguous words such as "impulsive" were read positively or negatively depending on what came before.[^s1] Asch described a "factor of primacy" and argued it was structural: early terms set a direction, and later terms were interpreted to fit it.[^s1] Memory research on list recall, where start and end items are remembered best, later informed accounts of order effects in evaluations.[^s4]

## Why it matters

At work, information almost always arrives in sequence: slides in a deck, bullet points in an email, options in a decision meeting, candidates on an interview day, and months of an employee's performance before an annual review. Order effects mean the same content can lead to different conclusions depending only on its position.[^s1][^s4]

- **Evaluations can be unfair.** In Eurovision Song Contest and figure-skating data, scores tended to rise for performers who appeared later, whether judges rated at the end or after each performance.[^s4] In performance appraisal, an I-O psychology textbook lists recency as a common rating distortion: a manager remembers last month more vividly than the first half of the year, even though both should count equally, and a first-impression error does the reverse.[^s6]
- **Persuasive messages can backfire by position.** When two sides of an issue are presented in turn, whether the first or the second has more influence depends on how engaged the audience is with the topic.[^s2]

## Evidence

The core finding that order changes judgments is well established across several paradigms, but its direction and size depend on conditions, so the status here is `mixed`.[^s2][^s3][^s4]

- **Elaboration decides which end wins.** Curtis Haugtvedt and Duane Wegener reviewed earlier research and ran two experiments varying the personal relevance of a topic. When people thought hard about the messages, the first message had more influence (primacy); when they processed lightly, the second had more influence (recency).[^s2]
- **Conflicting evidence favours recency.** Building on Hogarth and Einhorn's belief-adjustment model, Robert Tubbs and colleagues found significant recency effects when people updated beliefs in response to evidence that pointed in different directions, larger when the pieces differed more in value, and persisting after training intended to reduce them.[^s3]
- **Sequential judging favours later candidates.** Wändi Bruine de Bruin's 2005 analysis of real competitions found that later positions received higher ratings under both end-of-sequence and step-by-step scoring, and argued that randomising order spreads the advantage fairly but does not remove it.[^s4]
- **A caution about Asch.** A 2014 replication of the studies in Asch's paper found no evidence for a separate claim often attributed to it, that warmth traits dominate impressions; the influence of warmth depended heavily on context.[^s5] Classic demonstrations are often cited for more than they show.[^s1][^s5]

Limits: most evidence comes from laboratory tasks, consumer messages and competitions rather than workplace conversations, and no single rule says "go first" or "go last".[^s2][^s3][^s4]

## How to work with it

Use order to aid understanding, never to hide information.[^s1][^s2][^s4][^s6]

1. **Put the main point where it will be weighed.** Lead with your conclusion or request so it frames what follows, and restate it at the end so it is also the last thing read; this uses both ends rather than leaving the key point in the middle.[^s1][^s2]
2. **Do not bury inconvenient facts in the middle.** If a risk or limitation matters to the decision, give it its own visible position, because mid-sequence content is the most likely to be underweighted.[^s4]
3. **When you run an evaluation, control order.** Randomise or rotate the order of candidates, vendors or proposals, and score each option against the same criteria as soon as it is presented.[^s4]
4. **For reviews, collect evidence across the whole period.** Keep notes throughout the year and use rater training and shared performance standards so that the last few weeks or the first meeting do not dominate the rating.[^s6]
5. **Present competing options in a balanced way.** In a decision meeting, give each option equal time and a written summary, so the last presenter does not win by default when attention is low.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

- **Broader:** primacy and recency effects are a family of cognitive biases in how sequences of information are remembered and weighed.[^s4]
- **Demonstrated by** Asch's 1946 impression-formation experiments, which showed that the same traits in reverse order produced different impressions, and by Bruine de Bruin's 2005 analysis of order effects in real jury-scored competitions.[^s1][^s4]
- **Explained by the elaboration likelihood model:** Haugtvedt and Wegener used the amount of message elaboration, a central idea of that model, to predict when primacy or recency will occur.[^s2]
- **Explained by the belief-adjustment model:** Hogarth and Einhorn's model predicts recency when evidence is mixed, which Tubbs and colleagues tested, finding the predicted recency for inconsistent evidence along with some results the model did not anticipate.[^s3]
- **Applied in performance reviews,** where recency and first-impression errors are recognised rating distortions.[^s6]
- **Related:** anchoring is a close cousin in which an early number frames later estimates; bottom line up front, signposting and meeting recaps put the key message at the start or end.[^s1][^s2]
