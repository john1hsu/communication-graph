# Cognitive bias

A cognitive bias is a systematic, predictable departure from good judgment that arises from the shortcuts people use to estimate, interpret and choose quickly. Tversky and Kahneman described these shortcuts as usually efficient and useful, yet capable of producing severe and repeatable errors.[^s1] "Bias" here means a consistent lean in one direction, not random noise and not prejudice in the moral sense. For communicators, biases matter because nearly every exchange involves quick guesses: what the reader already knows, what a colleague meant by a terse message, which facts are worth mentioning and how sure we are of our own reading.[^s1][^s7]

## Origin

The modern concept comes from the heuristics-and-biases research programme of psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. Their 1974 *Science* article set out three heuristics: judging likelihood by resemblance (representativeness), by how easily examples come to mind (availability), and by adjusting from a starting value (anchoring and adjustment), with each linked to typical errors such as ignoring base rates or staying too close to an anchor.[^s1] They also observed that experienced, statistically trained researchers made the same mistakes when reasoning intuitively, which helped establish that biases are features of ordinary cognition rather than signs of ignorance.[^s1] Later decision researchers framed biases as a product of fast, automatic "System 1" thinking, with the slower "System 2" able to correct them but often too tired, rushed or distracted to do so.[^s7]

## Why it matters

Biases rarely announce themselves, and people reliably see them more in others than in themselves. Pronin, Lin and Ross found that people rated themselves as less susceptible to a range of biases than the average person or their peers, and kept that view even after the bias had been explained to them.[^s2] The authors linked this "bias blind spot" to conflict and misunderstanding: each side treats its own view as the objective one and the other side's as distorted.[^s2] In a workplace, that pattern turns ordinary disagreements about a forecast, a hiring decision or a customer complaint into arguments about who is being unreasonable.[^s2]

Biases also narrow how teams think. Soll, Milkman and Payne describe a tendency to consider one future, one objective and one option at a time, made worse by fatigue, stress and multitasking.[^s7] A manager who writes a status update while juggling three meetings is more likely to anchor on last quarter's numbers, lead with whichever risk came to mind most recently, and assume the reader shares their context.[^s1][^s7]

## How it shows up in communication

- **Writing and presenting.** Anchoring and availability pull a writer's estimates toward the first figure on the table and the examples that come to mind most easily, so a forecast or risk list can reflect the author's starting point more than the evidence.[^s1]
- **Interpreting others.** A short reply read as hostile, or a colleague's error put down to carelessness rather than circumstance, are quick judgments that feel like perception rather than inference.[^s2]
- **Weighing evidence in meetings.** People tend to give a fair hearing to evidence that fits their current view and to discount evidence that does not, which is why "let's be objective" rarely changes a discussion.[^s3]

## What helps

Telling people to be fair or unbiased does little. Lord, Lepper and Preston found that prompting people to actively consider the opposite of their current view reduced biased evaluation of evidence and biased hypothesis testing, while simple instructions to be unbiased did not.[^s3] Practical versions include asking "what would I expect to see if I were wrong?" before replying to a contentious message, assigning a devil's advocate, or running a premortem in which the team imagines a plan has already failed and explains why.[^s3][^s7] Soll and colleagues add making three estimates (low, likely, high) rather than one, listing objectives before asking for advice, and generating two to five options instead of a yes-or-no choice.[^s7]

Training can help too. In two experiments with about 240 adults each, Morewedge and colleagues found that a single session of an interactive training game cut bias errors by roughly a third to almost a half immediately, with reductions still around a quarter to a third two to three months later; a 30-minute video helped less.[^s4] The tasks were laboratory measures of six biases with convenience samples from one city, so the transfer to real workplace decisions is untested.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The core idea that intuitive judgment deviates from formal standards in predictable ways is well supported, but individual biases differ in robustness and the framing has critics.[^s1][^s6] Many Labs 2, a 2018 preregistered replication of 28 classic and newer psychology findings across 125 samples in 36 countries, found that only about half replicated and that the median effect was far smaller than in the original papers.[^s6] Each named bias therefore needs its own evidence check rather than borrowing credibility from the category.[^s6] Gerd Gigerenzer argued that some "illusions", such as overconfidence and base-rate neglect, shrink sharply when problems are posed as natural frequencies rather than single-case probabilities, and that the programme sometimes judged people against too narrow a norm.[^s5] The practical lesson for communicators is double: watch for bias in your own judgments, and present numbers in formats that make good reasoning easier.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, whose 1974 article launched the heuristics-and-biases programme.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Pronin, Lin and Ross (2002), whose bias blind spot shows why people resist hearing that their own judgment is biased; **studied in** social psychology.[^s2]
- **Explained by** dual-process theory: fast System 1 shortcuts that slower System 2 thinking fails to check.[^s7]
- **Challenged by** Gigerenzer (1991), who argued that frequency formats make several illusions disappear.[^s5]
- **Broader** than specific communication barriers such as egocentric bias, anchoring and adjustment, primacy and recency effects and the mythical fixed pie, which this graph treats in their own articles; **related** to the remedies of considering the opposite, devil's advocacy, premortems and perspective-getting.[^s3][^s7]
