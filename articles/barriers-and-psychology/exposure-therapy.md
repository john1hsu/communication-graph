steps:
  - Name the communication goal you avoid (for example, presenting at the team meeting) and write the specific thing you fear will happen, such as "I'll say something wrong and colleagues will think I'm incompetent."
  - Build a ladder of 6-10 real situations that lead toward that goal, rate each for anxiety from 1 to 10, and start with one you can do this week (replying to a group email with your own suggestion).
  - Before each step, write your prediction and how you would know whether it came true, so the step is a test rather than an endurance trial.
  - Do the step fully, without escape routes or safety behaviours such as reading from a script, staying silent after one sentence or asking a colleague to cover the hard question.
  - Afterwards, compare what happened with what you predicted, note what you learned, and repeat the step soon - in different meetings, rooms and audiences - before moving up.
  - Expect setbacks; if a step proves too big, add a smaller bridging step or repeat an earlier one, and get help from a qualified clinician if the fear is severe or spreads into everyday life.
example:
  situation: An analyst who has avoided speaking in cross-team reviews for a year is told she will present her findings to the leadership group next quarter.
  before: "I'll keep quiet in the reviews until then, write out the whole talk word for word, and ask my manager to take the Q&A so I don't freeze."
  after: "This week I'll add one suggestion in the team chat thread, then say one sentence in Thursday's review. Next week I'll give the two-minute status item without notes, then run a practice presentation for three colleagues and take their questions myself. Before each one I'll write what I expect to go wrong and check it afterwards."
  why: The first plan avoids every feared situation and leans on safety behaviours (a full script, a stand-in for Q&A), so her fear is never tested and the leadership talk arrives as a cliff. The second turns the goal into graded exposures, each framed as a test of a specific prediction, which is how exposure produces lasting change.
selfCheck:
  - What exactly do I predict will happen when I speak up, and have I written it down so I can check it afterwards?
  - Which safety behaviour am I still using - a script, a stand-in, staying silent after one point - that stops me from learning the fear was wrong?
  - Have I practised this step in more than one meeting, room or audience, or only in the easiest setting?
  - Am I judging the step by how anxious I felt, or by what actually happened?
  - Is my fear severe enough that I should work through this with a professional rather than alone?

# Exposure therapy

Exposure therapy is the practice of deliberately confronting what you fear, in a safe and planned way, instead of avoiding it.[^s1] Avoidance brings short-term relief but tends to make fear worse over time, so exposure reverses the pattern: you repeatedly enter the feared situation and stay until the planned test is complete.[^s1] Clinicians deliver it in real life (in vivo), in imagination, in virtual reality, or by provoking harmless body sensations such as a racing heart, and pace it either gradually up a ranked list of fears or, less often, by starting with the hardest item.[^s1] For workplace communication, the feared situations are usually social: speaking up in a meeting, presenting, taking questions, or making small talk with senior colleagues.[^s5]

## Origin

Exposure has been a core part of cognitive behavioural therapy for anxiety since that therapy began.[^s2] Joseph Wolpe first proposed it in the late 1950s in the form of systematic desensitization, which paired graded exposure with relaxation and was rooted in early models of extinction learning.[^s2] Later habituation-based accounts, such as Foa and Kozak's in 1986, held that fear had to fall during a session for treatment to work.[^s2] In a 2014 review, Michelle Craske and colleagues set out an inhibitory learning account: the original fear memory stays intact, and exposure builds a competing memory that the feared outcome does not follow.[^s2] Public clinical services now teach the method as self-help; Western Australia's Centre for Clinical Interventions frames it as a stepladder of behavioural experiments.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Fear of speaking and of being judged is one of the most common reasons capable people stay quiet at work, and avoidance keeps that fear alive.[^s1][^s4] Exposure-based treatment is among the best-supported psychological interventions for these fears. A 2014 network meta-analysis of 101 trials (13,164 participants) found individual cognitive behavioural therapy, which centres on exposure, was the most effective psychological treatment for social anxiety disorder, and that a combined exposure and social-skills class also beat waitlist.[^s3] A 2019 meta-analysis of 30 randomised trials of interventions for fear of public speaking, most of them cognitive or behavioural, found a moderate-to-large effect after treatment (Hedges' g about 0.74) that held or grew at follow-up.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Turn the communication goal you avoid into a ladder of real situations, each rated for anxiety, and start low enough that you will actually do the first step.[^s5] The CCI's workplace example climbs from agreeing with someone in a group email, to making your own suggestion, to saying one or two things in a team meeting, to a practice presentation and finally the real one.[^s5] Treat each step as a test: state the prediction first, design the step to check it, and end when the test is complete rather than when you feel calm.[^s2] Drop safety behaviours and safety signals where you can, because they block the new learning.[^s2] Vary the conditions - different meetings, rooms, audiences, times - since fear learned to be safe in one context tends to return in another.[^s2] Naming the feeling ("I'm anxious right now") without trying to suppress it may also help.[^s2] Repeat steps close together and add a bridging step after a setback.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is waiting until the fear has gone before acting; the inhibitory learning research suggests that how much fear falls within a session does not predict long-term results, so steps should be judged on whether the prediction was tested.[^s2] A second is climbing a ladder that is too steep, or so shallow that it never reaches the real goal.[^s5] A third is keeping crutches - a word-for-word script, a colleague who takes the hard questions - that let you credit survival to the crutch.[^s2] A fourth is practising only in one friendly setting and then being surprised when nerves return in the boardroom; renewal of fear in a new context is a well-described effect.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence for exposure-based treatment of clinical anxiety is strong and has replicated across many trials and meta-analyses, but it has limits.[^s2][^s3] Craske and colleagues note that a substantial share of people do not benefit or see fear return after treatment.[^s2] In the 2014 network meta-analysis, several intervention classes, exposure among them, rested on few moderate-sized trials, most trials stopped at the end of treatment, and psychological trials carried a high risk of bias from lack of masking.[^s3] The public-speaking meta-analysis found unclear randomisation in most trials, completers-only data, possible publication bias, and smaller effects on behavioural and physiological measures than on self-report.[^s4] Most evidence comes from clinical or student samples rather than workplaces. Self-guided stepladders suit everyday nerves; persistent, impairing anxiety should be treated with a qualified clinician.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

Exposure therapy **addresses** [[speaking-anxiety]], as the 2019 public-speaking meta-analysis shows, and [[social-anxiety]], where the 2014 network meta-analysis found exposure-centred CBT the strongest psychological treatment.[^s4][^s3] Both meta-analyses are the studies it is **supported by**.[^s3][^s4] Its modern rationale is **explained by** **inhibitory-learning**, the account that exposure builds a new safety memory through violated expectations rather than by wearing fear down.[^s2] It is **applied in** [[workplace-meetings]] and [[presentation]] situations, which make up the CCI's workplace stepladder.[^s5] [[systematic-desensitization]] is its oldest variant, pairing graded exposure with relaxation, and [[dropping-safety-behaviours]] is one of its key ingredients.[^s1][^s2] It sits alongside [[anxiety-reappraisal]] and [[labeling-emotions]] as ways to handle nerves without avoidance.[^s2]
