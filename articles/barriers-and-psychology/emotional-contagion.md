# Emotional contagion

Emotional contagion is the transfer of mood from one person to others in an interaction. The definition Sigal Barsade adopts (from Schoenewolf, 1990) describes it as a process in which a person or group influences the emotions or behaviour of another person or group, consciously or not.[^s1] At work it means the tone you bring into a stand-up, a client call or a tense one-on-one does not stay with you: the people in the room tend to pick it up, and it then shapes how they cooperate, argue and judge the work.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The modern account comes from Elaine Hatfield, John Cacioppo and Richard Rapson, whose 1994 book proposed "primitive emotional contagion": people automatically mimic others' faces, voices and postures, and feedback from that mimicry makes them feel the matching emotion.[^s4] Barsade adds a second, slower route, social comparison, in which people look at others to judge how they ought to feel about a situation.[^s1] Her 2002 laboratory study in *Administrative Science Quarterly* brought the idea into organisational behaviour by testing whether contagion changes how work groups actually behave.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Contagion turns a communicator's mood into part of the message. In Barsade's study, groups that caught a pleasant mood were more cooperative, had less conflict as rated by observers, and rated their own and each other's performance more highly, even though the content of what was said was held constant.[^s1] Leaders are an especially strong source because they are watched closely: in a 2005 experiment by Sy, Côté and Saavedra, members of groups whose leader was in a positive mood reported more positive moods, and those groups showed a more positive collective affective tone and better coordination.[^s2] The same process can spread irritation, anxiety or exhaustion, and Barsade notes that people often do not realise their mood has shifted, which makes negative spirals in service and care work hard to spot.[^s1]

## How to work with it

The practical lessons follow from the mechanisms rather than from a tested protocol, so treat them as reasoned practice. First, check your own state before a high-stakes conversation, because your face, voice and posture carry it even when your words are neutral; in Barsade's study the confederate changed only his nonverbal displays.[^s1] Second, choose the mood that fits the task instead of defaulting to cheerfulness: in the Sy study, groups led by a person in a positive mood coordinated better but put in less effort than groups whose leader was in a negative mood.[^s2] Third, expect weaker transfer when there is rivalry or distrust; mimicry of smiles drops in competitive settings and toward disliked or outgroup members, so enthusiasm may not land with a sceptical counterpart.[^s4] Fourth, remember that text also carries mood. The emotional tone of a written update can shift readers' own expression slightly, so a stream of anxious chat messages is not mood-neutral.[^s3]

A workplace illustration: a project lead joins a delayed-launch review visibly tense, speaking quickly and frowning at the dashboard. The team mirrors the tension, explanations turn defensive and the meeting ends without a plan. In the next review, the lead takes a minute beforehand to settle, opens by calmly naming the problem and the goal for the hour, and keeps a steady pace. The facts are the same; the room has a better chance of staying in problem-solving mode.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

The core group finding is consistent. Barsade's 2002 experiment ran 29 groups of business students through a leaderless budget-allocation exercise with a trained confederate who displayed one of four moods; mood spread at both the individual and group level by self-report and by outside coders, though energy level did not matter and unpleasant moods did not spread more readily than pleasant ones.[^s1] Sy and colleagues replicated the leader-to-member pattern experimentally in 2005.[^s2] Online, a 2014 Facebook experiment with 689,003 users found that removing positive or negative posts from News Feeds shifted users' own word choice in the same direction, but the effects were tiny (Cohen's d of 0.001 to 0.02), and the journal published an expression of concern about informed consent.[^s3]

The mechanism is less settled than popular accounts suggest. Hess and Fischer's 2013 review argues that emotional mimicry is not an automatic motor copy: the evidence mostly supports matching positive versus negative valence rather than specific emotions, and people mimic friends and allies far more than rivals or those they disagree with.[^s4] Facial feedback, the second link in Hatfield's chain, has partial support: the 2022 Many Smiles Collaboration, a multi-lab test, found that mimicking smiling photos or voluntarily smiling raised self-reported happiness, while the pen-in-mouth manipulation did not.[^s5][^s6] We rate the evidence `mixed`: mood convergence in groups is real, but its size depends on channel and relationship, and the automatic-mimicry explanation is contested.

## Connections

Emotional contagion is a phenomenon of interpersonal communication, introduced in its "primitive" form by Hatfield and colleagues and demonstrated in work groups by Barsade (2002).[^s4][^s1] Sy, Côté and Saavedra (2005) and Kramer, Guillory and Hancock (2014) support it in leader-led teams and in text, respectively.[^s2][^s3] It is partly explained by the facial feedback hypothesis, tested at scale by the Many Smiles Collaboration, and challenged on mechanism by Hess and Fischer (2013).[^s5][^s4] It plays out most visibly in workplace meetings, where a facilitator's tone sets the room.[^s1] It relates to mirroring and nonverbal communication, which carry it; to labeling emotions and de-escalation, which help when a negative mood is spreading; and to psychological safety, a team climate that collective mood can support or erode.[^s1][^s4]
