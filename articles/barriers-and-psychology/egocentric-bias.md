# Egocentric bias

Egocentric bias is the habit of judging other people's minds from the starting point of our own. We know what we meant, what we did and what we were thinking, and that private knowledge quietly becomes the yardstick for what others understood, noticed or contributed.[^s1][^s3] For communicators it is the umbrella over several better-known barriers: the sender who believes a terse email is perfectly clear, the presenter who assumes the room can see their nerves, and the team member who remembers mostly their own work on a shared project.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Michael Ross and Fiore Sicoly of the University of Waterloo gave the bias its name in research on shared work, reprinted as a chapter in the 1982 *Judgment under Uncertainty* collection.[^s1] They found that people working jointly tend to accept more responsibility for the outcome than their partners think they deserve, and argued the pattern is common wherever members assign credit themselves, such as deciding author order.[^s1] Their explanation was partly about memory: our own contributions are easier to recall than other people's, so they loom larger.[^s1] A closely related 1977 finding, the false consensus effect, showed the same pull in social perception: people's own choices colour their estimates of how many others would choose the same.[^s5][^s6] Later work by Nicholas Epley, Justin Kruger and colleagues extended the idea to everyday messages, showing that senders overestimate how well their intended tone comes across.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Most communication failures are invisible to the sender, and egocentric bias is a large part of why. In the Kruger, Epley, Parker and Ng studies, misunderstandings of sarcastic, serious, angry or funny emails were common even between people who knew each other, and senders were largely unaware of them.[^s2] Senders were typically overconfident that the recipient grasped their intent, and the authors trace this to the difficulty of stepping outside one's own perspective; email strips out the voice and face that might otherwise correct the reader.[^s2] The bias also distorts conversations about credit. Schroeder, Caruso and Epley found that group members' claims of responsibility routinely add up to more than 100%, and that over-claiming grows with group size because larger teams leave more of other people's work to overlook.[^s4] In a workplace that means friction over recognition, retrospectives where each person feels under-thanked, and status updates that over-weight the author's own workstream.[^s4]

## Evidence

The core finding has held up well. The false consensus effect was one of 28 classic and contemporary findings retested in Many Labs 2, run across 125 samples with 15,305 participants in 36 countries and territories, and it reproduced; only about half of the 28 did.[^s5][^s6] Many Labs 2 also found replication effects were generally smaller than the originals, so treat the size of any single classic result with caution.[^s6] Over-claiming was replicated across two field studies (academic author teams and MBA study groups) and two experiments that directly varied group size.[^s4] The Kruger email work is a 2005 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* paper rather than a multi-lab replication.[^s2] One important limit comes from Eyal, Steffel and Epley's 25 experiments: telling people to imagine another's perspective reduced egocentrism but did not reliably improve accuracy, and sometimes raised confidence without improving judgments.[^s3] Removing the bias is not the same as knowing what the other person thinks.[^s3]

## How to spot it

Watch for moments when your own knowledge is doing the work the message should do.[^s2][^s3]

- You are sure your tone is obvious because you can hear it as you reread the draft.[^s2]
- You feel you carried the project and suspect colleagues feel the same about their part.[^s4]
- You assume "everyone would agree" with a choice mainly because you made it.[^s5]
- You confidently predict a stakeholder's reaction without having asked them anything.[^s3]

## How to fix it

1. **Ask instead of imagine.** Eyal and colleagues found that getting the other person's perspective through conversation improved accuracy where imagining it did not; ask a quick question or have them summarise back.[^s3]
2. **Count others first.** Before estimating your own share of a team effort, list each colleague and what they contributed; considering others' contributions first reduced over-claiming.[^s4]
3. **Read the email in a flat voice.** Strip out the tone you intend and ask whether the words alone carry it; if not, say it plainly or pick a richer channel.[^s2]
4. **Treat your confidence as a weak signal.** High confidence about what someone else thinks is not evidence of accuracy, so check before acting on it.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a cognitive bias, specifically one that anchors social judgments on our own perspective.[^s1][^s3]
- **Demonstrated by** Ross and Sicoly's research on responsibility for joint work, which named the bias.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** the "Egocentrism over e-mail" studies, which showed senders overrate how well tone survives email.[^s2]
- **Demonstrated by** Schroeder, Caruso and Epley's 2016 over-claiming studies, where claims of credit grew with group size.[^s4]
- **Supported by** Many Labs 2, whose large replication reproduced the false consensus effect.[^s5][^s6]
- **Undermines** clarity, because senders stop adding the context and cues readers need.[^s2]
- **Related:** the curse of knowledge, illusion of transparency and spotlight effect are narrower forms; perspective-getting and checking understanding are the evidence-backed counters; perspective-taking reduces egocentrism without guaranteeing accuracy.[^s2][^s3]
