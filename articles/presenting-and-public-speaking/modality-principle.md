# Modality principle

The modality principle says that when you show a visual (a chart, process diagram, screenshot or animation), the words that explain it should usually be spoken rather than printed on the screen next to it.[^s1] For presenters the practical version is short: let the slide carry the picture and let your voice carry the explanation. The idea comes from multimedia-learning research and is often stated as the modality effect, the learning gain observed when pictures are paired with narration instead of written text.[^s5]

## Origin

The effect was first reported by Mousavi, Low and Sweller in 1995: students studying geometry worked examples did better on later problems when the diagram was on paper and the explanatory statements were read aloud than when both were printed.[^s6] Sweller, Paul Ayres and Slava Kalyuga later framed it within cognitive load theory as a cousin of the split-attention effect, a way to stop learners having to integrate two visual sources at once.[^s2] Richard Mayer named it one of his research-based principles of multimedia design, defining it as deeper learning when words are spoken rather than printed.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Working memory has partly separate channels for what we see and what we hear. If a slide holds a diagram and a paragraph, both compete for the eyes; the audience reads or looks, rarely both. Moving the words into speech frees visual attention for the graphic.[^s1][^s2] In Mayer's 2023 summary the principle held in 18 of 19 experimental tests with a median effect size of d = 1.00, the largest of the principles for managing essential processing.[^s1] That is why text-heavy decks that a speaker reads aloud, the habit known as death by PowerPoint, waste the audience's attention: Mayer's companion redundancy principle finds that adding printed text that duplicates the narration does not help, though that effect is small (median d = 0.10).[^s1]

## How to apply it

- **Put the explanation in your voice.** Show the chart or diagram with a headline or a few keywords, and narrate what to look at and why it matters.[^s5]
- **Do not read your slides.** If a sentence is printed, either cut it or say something different; avoid captions that repeat your narration word for word.[^s1]
- **Keep text that people must see.** Technical terms, unfamiliar names, numbers to note down and content for second-language listeners are exceptions where printed words may work better.[^s1]
- **Match text to who controls the pace.** The benefit is largest when the presenter sets the pace, as in a live talk, a recorded walkthrough or an auto-advancing format; for a pre-read deck the reader controls the pace, so fuller text is fine.[^s3][^s5]

Example: an analyst presenting quarterly churn replaces a slide holding a line chart plus five bullet points of commentary with the same chart, a one-line headline ("Churn rose only in the self-serve tier") and a highlighted line. She talks the room through the spike while they look at it, and puts the full commentary in the follow-up email, where readers can take their time.[^s1][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The research base is large but the headline numbers have shrunk. Ginns's 2005 meta-analysis of 43 effects found a large benefit (d = 0.72), strongest when learners could not control the pace; Reinwein's 2012 re-analysis of 86 effects confirmed the effect but found publication bias, with a corrected estimate near d = 0.20.[^s5] A 2016 meta-analysis of 91 studies found small overall benefits for retention and transfer (both about d = 0.25), larger under system-paced presentation, animated pictures and short materials, and no reversals.[^s3] Not every lab finds it: Huib Tabbers and Wilco van der Spoel failed to produce the effect in two experiments with a narrated lightning animation, and concluded that the failures cast doubt on both the dual-channel explanation and the principle's value as a design rule.[^s4] Most studies use short instructional lessons with students rather than workplace talks, so treat it as a sound default for explaining visuals, not a guarantee.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

The modality principle is applied in presentations[^s5] and in PechaKucha, whose auto-advancing, images-only format leaves the words to the speaker.[^s7] It develops slide-design skill and addresses the read-the-slides pattern of death by PowerPoint.[^s1] It is explained by the cognitive theory of multimedia learning[^s1] and by cognitive load theory's account of limited working memory.[^s2] It complements the coherence principle, which removes extraneous material while this one moves the essential words into speech.[^s1] It was introduced by John Sweller's group, demonstrated by Mousavi, Low and Sweller (1995),[^s6] and named as a design principle by Richard Mayer.[^s1] It is supported by Ginns's 2005 meta-analysis[^s5] and Wang, Xie and Li's 2016 meta-analysis,[^s3] and challenged by Tabbers and van der Spoel's 2011 double replication failure.[^s4] It sits beside assertion-evidence slides and presentation rehearsal, since narrating a visual well takes practice.
