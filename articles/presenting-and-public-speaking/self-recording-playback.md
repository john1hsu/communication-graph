steps:
  - Before you record, write a three-item checklist of delivery behaviours to look for, such as hand movement, eye contact and pace.
  - Prop your phone at audience eye level and record one full, standing run-through with the slides or notes you will really use.
  - Before you press play, jot down how you think you came across, then watch as if the speaker were a colleague you are coaching.
  - Watch once with the sound off for gestures, posture and fidgets, and once with sound for pace, pauses and filler words.
  - Note what to keep and pick no more than two habits to change, writing a concrete replacement for each.
  - Record a second run, check whether those two habits changed, and ask a colleague to watch one clip if you can.

# Self-recording playback

Self-recording playback means filming yourself giving a practice run of a talk, pitch or briefing and then watching it back to find and fix delivery habits. Public-speaking textbooks recommend it because you cannot see your own posture, hands and eye contact while you speak, and a recording shows them the way the audience will.[^s6] Speaking organisations single out video as the easiest way to catch uncontrolled gestures and wandering eyes.[^s5] It is the practice step that builds the skill of gesturing well: you plan gestures, see whether they actually happen, and cut the nervous ones.[^s5][^s6]

## Origin

Videotaped feedback entered public-speaking courses once classroom video equipment became common, and communication researchers began testing it experimentally. In 1998 John Bourhis and Mike Allen pooled twelve published experiments on video feedback in public-speaking instruction.[^s1] Clinical psychologists developed a related version for people with social anxiety, adding a short preparation step before viewing so that the recording corrects an overly harsh self-image rather than confirming it.[^s2] Today the same idea is a phone propped on a bookshelf, and both public-speaking textbooks and speaking clubs suggest it.[^s5][^s6]

## Why it matters

Speakers judge their delivery from the inside, and anxious speakers in particular often rate themselves far more harshly than observers do.[^s2] Across the twelve studies in Bourhis and Allen's synthesis, students who received videotaped feedback did better on speech content, speaking skill and objective tests, liked the course more, and did not become significantly more anxious.[^s1] In a 2020 accounting class, students who reviewed their own presentation videos with a self-assessment questionnaire reported clearer awareness of weak spots such as eye contact and voice, with little added anxiety.[^s4]

## How to apply it

1. **Choose what to look for first.** Write down three behaviours to check, such as hands, eye contact and pace; a 2016 study found that people practising in front of a plain mirror image, with no criteria or feedback, did not reduce their gesture problems.[^s3]
2. **Record a realistic run.** Stand, use your real slides or notes, and keep the camera at audience height so the clip shows what listeners will see.[^s6]
3. **Predict, then watch as an outsider.** Note how you think you came across, then watch as though coaching a stranger; this preparation helped socially anxious speakers form a more accurate, less harsh view of their performance.[^s2]
4. **Split the viewing.** A muted pass makes hand and body habits stand out; a second pass with sound covers pace and fillers.[^s5][^s6]
5. **Change one or two things and re-record.** Plan a specific gesture for a key point, or a replacement for a fidget, and check the next recording, since planned gestures feel awkward until rehearsed with the words.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Watching without criteria.** Reacting to your voice or appearance in general produces discomfort rather than a fix; an observer view without targeted feedback did not improve gesture use in the 2016 study.[^s3]
- **Fixing everything at once.** Toastmasters warns that over-choreographed gestures look stiff and can make you lose your place.[^s5]
- **Judging by feelings.** Some speakers rate themselves far more harshly than observers do; the outsider stance matters most for them.[^s2]
- **Skipping a second viewer.** Self-review raises awareness, but peer or coach feedback adds a view you cannot supply yourself.[^s4][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The core evidence is positive but dated and classroom-based: Bourhis and Allen's synthesis covers twelve published studies, mostly with students, and its abstract does not report how large the effects were.[^s1] Later classroom studies of video self-assessment mostly rely on students' own reports of awareness and confidence, and the 2020 study compared an ungraded practice with a graded final talk, which its authors flag as a confound.[^s4] The anxiety benefit is narrow: in a 2010 trial with 22 adults with social anxiety disorder, video feedback with preparation improved self-ratings, but cut anticipatory anxiety only for those whose self-ratings were much harsher than observers', and the effect faded over time.[^s2] Evidence that a recording specifically improves gesture is indirect. A 2016 experiment with 40 professionals found that automated feedback on gesturing reduced gesture problems while a mirror-only group did not improve, which suggests the benefit comes from targeted feedback rather than simply seeing yourself.[^s3]

## Connections

Self-recording playback is a practice technique for public speaking, and Bourhis and Allen's 1998 synthesis is the study that demonstrates its benefit to speaking skill.[^s1] It develops the skill of gesturing while speaking, because video is the clearest way to see whether planned gestures happen and nervous ones stop.[^s5] It complements presentation rehearsal, which textbooks pair with recording yourself, and is applied when preparing presentations.[^s4][^s6] It may help with speaking anxiety for speakers whose self-image is much harsher than reality, a weaker link than the skills benefit.[^s2] Watching as an outsider echoes self-distancing, and the gap between how speakers feel and how they look relates to the spotlight effect and eye contact.[^s2][^s6]
