# Cognitive theory of multimedia learning

The cognitive theory of multimedia learning (CTML) is Richard Mayer's account of how people make sense of messages that combine words (spoken or printed) with pictures (charts, diagrams, photos, animation).[^s1] It rests on three assumptions: people handle verbal and visual material in separate but interacting channels; each channel can process only a few pieces of information at once; and real understanding needs active work, in which the audience selects the relevant words and images, organises them into a verbal and a pictorial model, and integrates the two with what they already know.[^s1][^s3] For anyone who builds slides, explainer videos, dashboards or illustrated reports, it is the theory behind most evidence-based design advice.

## Origin

Mayer began the research programme as a graduate student at the University of Michigan in 1969, asking how to teach so that people can transfer what they learn to new problems, and developed it over decades at the University of California, Santa Barbara.[^s1][^s2] He drew on Allan Paivio's dual-coding theory, on limited-capacity work by George Miller, Alan Baddeley and John Sweller, and on Merlin Wittrock's idea of generative learning; the flowchart version of the model settled in the 2000s.[^s1] Mayer and Roxana Moreno's 2003 paper turned the theory into nine practical ways to reduce overload, such as narrating instead of printing, segmenting, signalling and weeding out extras.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The theory sorts the mental effort a message demands into three kinds. **Extraneous** processing is caused by poor design and wastes capacity, so reduce it; **essential** processing comes from the genuine complexity of the content, so manage it; **generative** processing is the audience's effort to make sense of it, so foster it.[^s1][^s3] Overload happens when the sum exceeds what the audience can handle, which is the everyday experience of a dense deck read aloud.[^s2] From this, Mayer derives 15 design principles, backed by more than 200 experiments: five for cutting extraneous processing (coherence, signalling, redundancy, spatial and temporal contiguity), four for managing essential processing (including segmenting, pre-training and modality) and six for fostering generative processing (including a conversational tone and a human voice).[^s1] Its base claim, the multimedia principle, is that people learn more deeply from words and graphics than from words alone (13 of 13 comparisons, median d = 1.35).[^s3]

Workplace example: an operations lead has to explain a new escalation process in a 15-minute all-hands. Version one is six slides of bullet text, read aloud, with clip art. Applying the theory, she shows one flow diagram built up step by step, highlights each step as she narrates it, keeps short labels next to the boxes they describe, drops the clip art, and sends the detailed written procedure afterwards. The audience no longer has to read and listen to the same words at once, and the picture and the explanation arrive together.[^s3][^s6]

## Evidence

Most individual principles have strong laboratory support: in Mayer's 2023 summary, coherence held in 18 of 19 tests (median d = 0.86), signalling in 26 of 28 (0.70), temporal contiguity in 8 of 8 (1.31) and modality in 18 of 19 (1.00), while redundancy was weaker (8 of 12, d = 0.10).[^s3] The theory is judged by how well it explains these patterns, and it comes with boundary conditions: many effects are strongest for audiences with little prior knowledge and for fast, presenter-paced material, and some fade or reverse for experts or self-paced reading.[^s3] Critics question parts of the mechanism. Tabbers and van der Spoel failed twice to reproduce the modality effect and found no support for the separate-channels explanation, which they argued undermines both its rationale and its value as a design rule.[^s4] A 2022 review notes that the theory says little about how long working memory holds information or about retrieval, and that its load-based account of segmenting and contiguity may miss the role of chunking.[^s5] Mayer himself lists open problems: motivation, emotion, social cues and metacognition are not yet fully built in, and most evidence comes from short laboratory lessons with students rather than workplace training or meetings.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

CTML was introduced by Richard Mayer, was influenced by dual-coding theory and by the limited-capacity research behind cognitive load theory, and is studied in educational psychology.[^s1] Understanding cognitive load first makes it easier to follow. It is applied whenever words and visuals are combined in a presentation or recorded lesson.[^s6] Its practical side is supported by Mayer and Moreno's 2003 synthesis of experiments,[^s2] and its dual-channel mechanism is challenged by Tabbers and van der Spoel's 2011 double replication failure.[^s4] It is the parent theory of the coherence and modality principles, and it underlies slide-design advice and critiques of death by PowerPoint and information overload, alongside assertion-evidence slides and signposting.[^s3][^s6]
