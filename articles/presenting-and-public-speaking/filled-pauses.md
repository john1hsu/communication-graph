# Filled pauses

Filled pauses are the "um", "uh" and "er" sounds - and, in the looser coaching sense, filler words such as "like" and "so" - that speakers produce while they work out what to say next. As an anti-pattern the problem is not that they occur but their *rate*: a talk or briefing dense with fillers is judged less effective, while a handful per minute does little or no harm.[^s3] The common coaching goal of eliminating every "um" goes further than the evidence, which is why this node is marked contested.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

The term treats these sounds as pauses that happen to be filled with noise - a symptom of a speaker struggling to plan. Psycholinguists Herbert Clark and Jean Fox Tree challenged that view in a 2002 paper in *Cognition*. Analysing large corpora of British and North American conversation, they argued that "uh" and "um" are conventional English words that speakers choose and plan: "uh" announces a short delay and "um" a longer one. After "um", a pause followed far more often than after "uh" (about 61% vs. 29% in their main corpus), and the pauses were longer.[^s1] Applied behaviour analysts later began treating high filler rates as a speaking habit that can be measured and trained down.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Listeners draw conclusions from fillers. In a 1995 study, Nicholas Christenfeld found that people assume a speaker's "ums" signal nervousness or poor preparation, even though the "ums" in his recordings had neither cause. Fillers shaped impressions even when listeners could not report hearing them.[^s2] A 2024 study in the *Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis* varied filler rates in speeches at 0, 2, 5 and 12 per minute and had a crowdsourced panel rate the speaker. Ratings fell in most categories at the higher rates, especially for filler sounds at 12 per minute; at 5 per minute ratings did not drop.[^s3] In a business setting this matters most where you are being judged on readiness: a status update, a pitch, or an answer to an executive's question.

## How to spot it

- Record a real talk or meeting contribution and count fillers per minute. Rates near 12 per minute are where the 2024 study found clear damage; around 5 or fewer it found none.[^s3]
- Notice *where* they fall. Because "um" signals a coming delay, clusters usually mark the moments you had not planned - the opening of an answer, a transition, a number you are recalling.[^s1]
- Don't rely on how fluent you felt. Listeners in Christenfeld's study were affected by fillers they did not consciously notice, so self-impression is a poor guide.[^s2]
- Note *how* you hesitate, not just how often. In a 2019 study of 68 managers' business presentations, short, more nasal filled pauses ("mm") were underestimated by listeners and went with higher ratings of speaking performance, while long, drawn-out ones did not.[^s6]

## How to fix it

1. **Measure a baseline.** Count fillers in two or three recorded practice runs so you can see change.[^s4]
2. **Build awareness.** In habit-reversal training, speakers first learn to detect each filler as it happens, so the habit stops running on autopilot.[^s4]
3. **Use a competing response: pause silently.** When you feel a filler coming, close your mouth and pause briefly instead. All six participants in Mancuso and Miltenberger's 2016 study cut their filled pauses immediately after awareness plus competing-response training.[^s4]
4. **Plan your first sentence.** Fillers mark planning delays, so script the opening line of your talk, each transition, and the first sentence of likely answers.[^s1]
5. **Aim for few, not zero.** A low rate did not hurt ratings in the 2024 study, and chasing zero can make delivery stiff.[^s3] Christenfeld found that a filled pause was rated better than a silent pause of the same length - the best impression came from no pause at all - so the goal is smoother planning, not long silences.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

Fillers are not pure noise for listeners. Fraundorf and Watson (2011, *Journal of Memory and Language*) inserted "uh" and "um" into recorded stories and found that listeners later recalled the plots better than with fluent versions, using coughs of matching length as a timing control.[^s5] This is one lab study of short stories with fillers at natural rates, so the safe reading is that occasional fillers are harmless or mildly helpful, while dense fillers cost the speaker. The behavioural intervention studies are small single-case designs with a handful of student speakers each, so they show that reduction is possible rather than how large the effect is for professionals.[^s4] The rating study measured perceived effectiveness from short recorded speeches, not outcomes such as decisions or deals.[^s3] Norms also vary by language: the 2019 phonetic study used German speakers.[^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader:** part of paralanguage and vocal delivery - the voice cues that carry meaning beyond the words, here signalling a planning delay.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Christenfeld (1995), which showed fillers lower impressions even when listeners do not notice them.[^s2]
- **Challenged by** Clark and Fox Tree (2002), who argued from corpus data that "uh" and "um" are meaningful signals rather than errors, and by Fraundorf and Watson (2011), whose recall benefit undercuts zero-filler advice.[^s1][^s5]
- **Undermines** credibility: at high rates, fillers lower listeners' ratings of a speaker's effectiveness.[^s3]
- **Contrasts with** silence and pauses - deliberate silent pauses are the usual replacement, though Christenfeld found silence is not automatically better than a filler.[^s2]
- **Related:** public speaking anxiety, presentation rehearsal, and habit-reversal training, the method shown to reduce filler rates.[^s4]
