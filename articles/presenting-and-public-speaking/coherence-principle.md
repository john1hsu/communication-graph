# Coherence principle

The coherence principle says that people learn more deeply from a presentation or lesson when extraneous material is left out rather than put in.[^s1] "Extraneous" means anything that does not serve the point: an interesting but off-topic fact, a dramatic video clip, a stock photo or a joke that sits beside the content without explaining it. Researchers call the attractive-but-irrelevant kind *seductive details*.[^s1][^s3] For a workplace presenter the principle is a test you apply to every element of a deck: does this help the audience understand my point, or does it only make the slide feel livelier?[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

The principle comes from Richard Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning, built from a long series of controlled experiments. Mayer groups it with signaling, redundancy and the two contiguity principles as ways to cut *extraneous processing*: mental work that does not serve the goal but still uses up limited capacity.[^s1] A landmark 2001 study by Mayer, Heiser and Lonn showed students a short narrated animation on how lightning forms. Adding six interesting but irrelevant narrated sentences lowered both recall (d = 0.56) and problem-solving transfer (d = 0.55). Inserting short, vivid video clips of lightning strikes also cut transfer (d = 0.86).[^s2] The authors argued that the extra material pulls in the wrong prior knowledge and gets in the way of building a cause-and-effect model of the topic. Their results ran against the popular idea that adding interest always helps learning.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Presenters add decoration because they fear losing the room, but in the lab the trade usually costs understanding. Mayer's 2023 summary reports the coherence principle held in 18 of 19 experimental tests, with a median effect size of d = 0.86.[^s1] A 2020 meta-analysis by Sundararajan and Adesope pooled 58 studies with more than 7,500 learners and found that people given seductive details scored lower on learning measures than people given the same lesson without them.[^s3][^s4] The harm was larger when the irrelevant item sat next to an informative diagram and when it stayed on screen as a static image or joke, which is exactly how many slide templates and "fun" visuals are used.[^s4] In business terms, a decision-maker who remembers your clever photo but not your recommendation has been given a worse briefing.

## How to apply it

1. Write the one sentence the audience must understand, then judge every element against it.[^s1]
2. For each image, clip, anecdote, sound and statistic, ask whether it explains, evidences or illustrates the point. If it only decorates, cut it or move it to an appendix or handout.[^s1][^s3]
3. Keep anything playful away from the key chart or diagram, and don't leave it on screen while people study the evidence.[^s4]
4. Keep engaging material that is relevant: a real customer example that shows the problem respects the principle; a joke about Mondays does not.[^s4]
5. Pair coherence with signaling: once the noise is gone, use headings, highlighting and spoken pointers to show what matters most.[^s1]

**Before/after (workplace).** An analyst presents quarterly churn to a sales leadership team. *Before:* the key slide has a cartoon of a leaking bucket, an animated arrow, a side box of "fun facts" about the industry, and the churn-by-segment chart in a corner. *After:* the slide headline states that small-business churn doubled after the price change, the chart fills the slide with that segment highlighted, and the industry facts move to the appendix. *Why:* nothing on screen competes with the evidence, and the one vivid element is the data that proves the claim.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence

The core effect has replicated in many lab tests and a large meta-analysis, which is why it is a sensible default.[^s1][^s3] Its size depends on conditions. Mayer notes it may be strongest for people with lower working-memory capacity, for system-paced lessons such as a live talk, and when the extraneous material is highly interesting.[^s1] Some well-run studies found no harm. In a 2012 experiment with 95 students, Rey varied how many decorative elements appeared in a text's header, footer and flow chart and found no effect on recall, transfer or study time.[^s5] A 2018 eye-tracking study found that seductive text and pictures added to bar graphs slowed readers by a few seconds but did not raise their error rate. The authors suggest that graph-literate readers learned to ignore the clutter.[^s6] Most experiments also used short student lessons of about 6 to 12 minutes and measured only learning, not emotional benefits such as reduced anxiety, which the meta-analysis authors flag as open questions.[^s4] Status is therefore *mixed*: cutting seductive details reliably helps or does no harm, but neutral decoration is less costly than vivid tangents, and effects in workplace briefings are extrapolated.[^s1][^s5][^s6]

## Common mistakes

- Treating the principle as "no images or stories". Relevant visuals and examples are what good slides need; only the irrelevant ones go.[^s1][^s4]
- Cutting the explanation instead of the decoration, which leaves a sparse slide that still confuses.[^s1]
- Moving distracting material into the narration. Off-topic spoken asides harmed understanding too in the lightning study.[^s2]

## Connections

The coherence principle is part of Richard Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning and is explained by limits on working memory and cognitive load.[^s1] It develops slide design and helps prevent the information overload that crowded decks create.[^s1] It is applied in presentations as well as lessons.[^s4] Mayer, Heiser and Lonn's 2001 lightning experiments demonstrate it, and Sundararajan and Adesope's 2020 meta-analysis supports it.[^s2][^s3] Rey's 2012 null result for decorative elements and Strobel, Grund and Lindner's 2018 graph study challenge how far it generalises.[^s5][^s6] It sits alongside assertion-evidence slides, signposting and concise writing, and echoes Edward Tufte's data-ink ratio; it is one antidote to death by PowerPoint.[^s1][^s4] Applying it requires audience analysis, because what counts as relevant depends on who is listening.[^s1]
