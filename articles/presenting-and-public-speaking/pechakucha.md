# PechaKucha

PechaKucha is a presentation format with one hard rule: 20 images, each on screen for 20 seconds, advancing automatically whether or not the speaker is ready. The talk therefore lasts exactly 6 minutes 40 seconds.[^s1] Because the speaker cannot linger, skip ahead or add "one more slide", the format forces decisions about what matters before the talk rather than during it. At work it is used for project show-and-tells, conference lightning sessions, team retrospectives and student or new-hire presentations where many people need to speak in one session.[^s2][^s5]

## Origin

The format was devised by the Tokyo architects Astrid Klein and Mark Dytham of Klein Dytham architecture, and first run as a one-off event in Tokyo in 2003.[^s1] The name is Japanese for chit-chat, and the founders have said plainly that it was a response to architects who talk too much.[^s1][^s2] Dytham told NPR that they tried 10 slides at 10 seconds (too short) and 30 at 30 (too long) before settling on 20 by 20, and that 20 seconds is long enough for an audience to actually look at an image.[^s2] PechaKucha Nights have since spread to more than 1,000 cities, and the format inspired imitators such as Ignite.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

Most slide talks fail by sprawl: too many slides, too much text and no time limit the speaker respects. PechaKucha removes those failure modes by construction. In a randomised trial with 134 nursing students, a PechaKucha lecture and a conventional PowerPoint lecture on the same topic produced equivalent test scores, but satisfaction was significantly higher in the PechaKucha group.[^s4] Two psychology-classroom experiments found the same pattern: students rated PechaKucha talks more positively, including on how purposefully visuals were used, while recall a week later did not differ from 5- or 10-minute PowerPoint talks.[^s3] The practical upshot is that you can often cover the same ground in less time with a more engaged audience, not that people learn more.

## What works here

- **Start from the message, not the slides.** Write the single point you want people to leave with, then sketch a 20-beat storyboard (roughly one idea per 20 seconds) before opening any slide software.[^s5]
- **Use images, not text.** The format assumes the speaker carries the words and the slide carries a picture, chart or single number; in one university case study, poor visuals, including excessive animation, were among the reasons presentations failed.[^s5]
- **Script loosely, rehearse heavily.** Twenty seconds is about 50 spoken words. Rehearse with the auto-timer on until your talking points land with each transition; reading from notes was another common cause of failed talks.[^s5]
- **Use it where brevity is the goal.** It suits progress updates, project showcases and sessions where many people present back to back; in the same case study students most valued its brevity and conciseness, and peer attention stayed high.[^s5]

Example: a product team replaces six 30-minute sprint-review presentations with six PechaKucha talks in one 45-minute session, leaving time for questions. Each engineer shows one screenshot or metric per slide and explains it in a sentence or two, the image-led, speaker-carries-the-words split the format was designed around.[^s1][^s5]

## Pitfalls

- **Expecting better learning.** The controlled studies show higher satisfaction, not better recall or test scores, so do not adopt it as a learning intervention on its own.[^s3][^s4]
- **Using it for nuanced or theoretical content.** Students in one study felt it worked for practical material but left too little room for theory or argued positions.[^s5]
- **Underestimating preparation.** Sources disagree on whether the format takes more preparation than a normal talk, so budget for at least several timed run-throughs.[^s5]
- **Adding pressure for anxious speakers.** The fixed timer can make some people more nervous and may discourage audience questions; offer practice runs and a Q&A slot after the timed section.[^s5]

## Connections

PechaKucha was introduced by Astrid Klein and Mark Dytham.[^s1] Its evidence base is demonstrated by Beyer, Gaze and Lazicki (2013)[^s3] and by Bakcek and colleagues' 2020 randomised trial, which contrasted it with conventional text-heavy PowerPoint lecturing, the habit called death by PowerPoint.[^s4] It complements presentation rehearsal, because the auto-advancing timer punishes speakers who have not practised and rewards those who have; and, under time pressure, it can feed speaking anxiety in some presenters.[^s5] It is a specific kind of presentation that depends on slide-design skill, sits alongside assertion-evidence slides and the coherence principle as an alternative to text-heavy decks, and shares the elevator pitch's discipline of saying less, more clearly.
