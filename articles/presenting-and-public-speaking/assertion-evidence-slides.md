steps:
  - Before opening the slide software, write the talk's main message, then list the three to six points the audience must accept to believe it.
  - Turn each point into one slide whose headline is a complete sentence of no more than two lines that states the point, not the topic.
  - Under each headline, place the visual that best proves the sentence - a chart, diagram, photo, table or equation - and delete decorative images.
  - Strip the body text down to short labels on the visual; move anything you would have bulleted into what you say aloud or into a handout.
  - Read only the headlines in order and check that they tell the whole argument; fix any slide whose headline could not stand on its own.
  - Rehearse explaining each visual in your own words rather than reading the headline back to the audience.
example:
  situation: An operations analyst is presenting the cause of a rise in late deliveries to the regional leadership team.
  before: "Headline: 'Q3 delivery performance'. Bullets: 'On-time rate down', 'Carrier changes in July', 'Warehouse B staffing', 'Weather events', 'Next steps'."
  after: "Headline: 'Late deliveries doubled after July because Warehouse B lost its night shift'. Body: a line chart of weekly on-time rate for each warehouse, with Warehouse B's drop marked at the week the night shift ended."
  why: The first slide names a topic and leaves the audience to work out which bullet matters. The second states the conclusion in the headline and shows the one chart that proves it, so leaders who only glance at the slide still take away the cause.
selfCheck:
  - If someone read only my slide headlines, would they get my argument and my conclusion?
  - Does each slide make exactly one point, and does the visual actually prove that point rather than decorate it?
  - Am I planning to explain each visual, or am I going to read the headline and bullets aloud?
  - Is the material complex enough to need visual evidence, or would a simple spoken point serve the audience better?

# Assertion-evidence slides

Assertion-evidence slides replace the default "topic headline plus bullet list" with two parts. The headline is a full sentence that states the single point the slide makes, and the body is visual evidence for that point - a graph, diagram, photo, film clip or equation, with text reduced to labels.[^s4] Instead of a slide titled "Q3 results" with five bullets, you show a slide headed "Q3 margin fell because freight costs rose 18%" above the freight-cost chart.

## Origin

The approach was developed by Michael Alley, an engineering-communication researcher at Penn State, as a challenge to PowerPoint's built-in topic-subtopic layout.[^s4] He set it out in a 2005 *Technical Communication* article with co-author Neeley and in his book *The Craft of Scientific Presentations*, and his workshop materials frame it as a way to inform and persuade audiences about technical content.[^s4] Alley's group later tested it with cognitive psychologist Joanna Garner in a series of studies funded by the U.S. National Science Foundation.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

A topic headline tells the audience what the slide is about but not what to conclude, so each listener has to infer the point from the bullets while also listening to you.[^s5] The design draws on Richard Mayer's multimedia-learning principles - pairing words with relevant pictures, cutting redundant text and signalling the key idea - and on cognitive load theory, which predicts that slides demanding less reading leave more working memory for understanding.[^s1] For workplace talks, the practical gain is that the headlines carry the argument: a manager skimming the deck later, or a listener who drifted for a minute, still gets the conclusion, much like an answer-first written message.[^s4][^s5]

## How to apply it

Follow the steps above. Alley's own guidance adds some concrete limits: keep headlines left-aligned and to two lines at most, use a bold sans-serif font at about 28 points for headlines and 18-24 points for body text, and keep the amount the audience must read to roughly 15 words a minute of talk.[^s4] Where a list really is the best evidence, keep it to two to four items arranged visually rather than as a block of bullets.[^s4] Teaching sites that adopted the method stress the same core move: replace each topical title with the claim you want remembered, then let a visual carry the support.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Writing a topic, not a claim.** "Customer churn" is still a topic; "Churn doubled among customers who never finished onboarding" is an assertion.[^s4][^s5]
- **Decorative "evidence".** Stock photos and clip art do not prove anything; the visual must show the data, mechanism or example behind the sentence.[^s4]
- **Shrinking visuals to fit.** Critics note that insisting on a visual for every slide can produce tiny, unreadable figures; enlarge the one visual that matters or merge slides.[^s6]
- **Reading the headline aloud.** A long sentence headline can tempt nervous speakers to read it word for word; explain the evidence instead.[^s6]
- **Using it as a handout.** The slides are designed to support a speaker, so they carry less text than a document; send a written summary for readers who were not there.[^s4]

## Evidence

The strongest test is Garner and Alley's 2013 experiment: 110 engineering undergraduates heard the same eight-minute narrated lecture on how MRI detects tumours, with either common-practice bullet slides or assertion-evidence slides.[^s1] The assertion-evidence group wrote better explanatory essays (a large effect, d = 0.81), held fewer major misconceptions, scored higher on a delayed recall test, and reported lower mental effort; there was no overall difference on an immediate multiple-choice test, only on its inference items.[^s1] A 2016 follow-up found that 59 students required to build assertion-evidence slides understood the material better the next day than 61 who chose their own format, most of whom used topic-subtopic slides.[^s2]

The limits are real. Most studies come from the method's own developers, used one technical topic and student samples, and bundled several design changes together, so they cannot say whether the sentence headline, the visual or the reduced text did the work.[^s1] An independent 2023 study of 108 engineering students at Kuwait University replicated the lower perceived cognitive load but found no difference in understanding, motivation or self-efficacy.[^s3] A University of Washington technical-communication scholar also argued, in an informal assessment, that the comparison slides in these experiments were weaker than competent standard slides and that the method neglects the speaker's role in live talks.[^s6][^s1] Treat the approach as a well-reasoned, promising default for explanatory and technical presentations rather than a proven rule for every setting.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

- **Introduced by** Michael Alley of Penn State, who proposed it as an alternative to PowerPoint's topic-subtopic default.[^s4]
- **Addresses** death by PowerPoint - text-heavy bullet slides that the audience reads instead of listening.[^s4]
- **Applied in** presentations and teaching talks, where it has been adopted by university teaching centres.[^s5]
- **Explained by** cognitive load: the design aims to cut extraneous reading so working memory goes to understanding.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Garner and Alley (2013) and **supported by** Garner and Alley (2016) on presenters' own understanding.[^s1][^s2]
- **Challenged by** Alzayed and Alzamel (2023), whose independent study found lower cognitive load but no comprehension gain.[^s3]
- **Related:** bottom line up front and presentation structure (the headline states the conclusion first), the curse of knowledge (topic titles assume the audience already sees the point), and presentation rehearsal (practise explaining each visual).[^s4][^s5]
