# Slide design

Slide design is the skill of deciding what goes on each slide of a talk, and how it looks, so the slides help the audience follow and remember the speaker's point. A well-designed slide makes one point, shows evidence for it, puts the most important thing in the most visible place, and leaves the explaining to the speaker.[^s3] The skill covers content choices (what to include and what to cut), layout and emphasis, charts and images, and legibility for everyone in the room or on the call.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

Slide design has no single inventor; current guidance rests on two research traditions. The first is Richard Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning, built from experiments since the 1990s on how people learn from words and pictures together. Mayer's 2023 summary lists principles such as coherence (cut extraneous material), signaling (highlight what matters), spatial contiguity (put labels next to what they describe) and redundancy (don't print what you are already saying over a graphic).[^s1] The second is Stephen Kosslyn's work applying perception and memory research to visual displays. In 2012 Kosslyn and colleagues set out eight principles, including discriminability, salience, limited capacity and relevance, and tested real slideshows against them.[^s3] Narrower methods such as assertion-evidence slides apply these principles to one layout.[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

Most slides break basic rules of perception and memory. In a 2012 analysis of 140 slideshows from research, education, government and business, essentially every deck overloaded viewers' working memory or used elements that were hard to tell apart. Violation rates were similar in every category.[^s3] Common failures included lists of more than four bullets, bullet sentences running past two lines, and whole slides set in capitals, italics or bold.[^s3] In a companion survey of 205 people who often sat through slide talks, the complaints were speakers reading slides word for word, slides too full to absorb before the next one appeared, and main points buried in irrelevant detail.[^s3] A third study found that viewers often could not say what was wrong with a flawed slide, which suggests the skill has to be learned; it does not come from intuition.[^s3]

## What good looks like

- **One point, made visible.** Each slide serves one idea, and its biggest, boldest or highest-contrast element is the one that matters most. Emphasis follows importance (Kosslyn's salience principle).[^s3]
- **Words plus relevant pictures, nothing decorative.** Pairing words with a relevant graphic beats words alone. Interesting but irrelevant images, facts and clips hurt understanding (multimedia and coherence principles, median d = 1.35 and 0.86 in Mayer's tallies).[^s1]
- **Key terms on screen, sentences in the voice.** Mayer advises against putting the narration on screen as full text, though the measured effect is small (median d = 0.10).[^s1] A 2012 meta-analysis of 57 studies is more nuanced. Text plus speech often beat speech alone, especially for newcomers, for fast-moving material and when there were no pictures. Much of that gain came from showing *key terms* drawn from the narration, not the full script.[^s2] So the practical rule is to show a few anchor words, not paragraphs.[^s1][^s2]
- **Labels beside the thing they name.** Put chart labels on the lines and bars, not in a separate legend, and highlight the one data point you will talk about.[^s1][^s3]
- **Readable for everyone.** Text and key visuals should be readable from the back row. Use plain fonts with even stroke width and strong contrast between text and background and between chart colours. Animate only when motion helps understanding, because some motion distracts or makes people ill. Send the slides to participants, interpreters and captioners in advance.[^s4]

## How to practise it

1. Before opening the software, write the one sentence the audience must remember. Then list the few points that support it; each point becomes one slide.[^s3]
2. For each slide, choose the single visual that best proves the point, and delete anything decorative.[^s1]
3. Cut the body text to key terms, and move the full explanation into your speaker notes or a handout.[^s2][^s4]
4. Do a "squint test": blur your eyes or view the slide as a thumbnail. Whatever still stands out should be the point. If it isn't, change the size, weight or colour.[^s3]
5. Check legibility the way the audience will see it: on the projector or a shared laptop screen, from a distance, in greyscale. Fix any line or label you can't tell apart.[^s4]
6. Rehearse describing each visual aloud, so people who can't see the screen still get the information.[^s4]

**Before/after (workplace).** A facilities manager asks leadership to fund a new HVAC contract. *Before:* the slide is titled "Building systems", with seven bullets of maintenance history in 14-point type, a stock photo of a thermostat and a four-colour pie chart with a separate legend. *After:* the headline reads "Emergency repairs cost 40% more than a service contract would", the only visual is a bar chart of repair costs by year with the contract price marked as a labelled line, and the history goes in a handout. *Why:* the main claim is now the most visible thing on the slide, the chart labels sit on the data, and nothing on screen competes with what the manager says.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The multimedia principles behind slide design are well replicated, but in a particular setting. Mayer's tallies come mostly from short, system-paced lessons tested on students. Several effects are boundary-dependent: coherence matters most for people with less working-memory capacity, and redundancy shrinks or reverses when pacing is slow, text is short or the audience is listening in a second language.[^s1][^s2] Kosslyn's study shows that flaws are common and that audiences find them annoying. It does not measure whether fixing them changes decisions in the room.[^s3] The status here is therefore *mixed*. The principles are good defaults, but how much they matter in a 20-minute business briefing is an extrapolation. For multilingual or technical audiences, more on-screen text may be the better choice.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Slide design is a skill used in the presentation context and sits under visual communication more broadly.[^s4] It requires audience analysis, because what counts as relevant, overloading or unfamiliar depends on who is watching.[^s3] It is mainly explained by Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning and by cognitive load theory, and it draws directly on Richard Mayer's principles.[^s1] Done well, it addresses death by PowerPoint and the information overload that text-heavy decks cause.[^s3] It is supported by Kosslyn et al.'s 2012 audit of real slideshows and by Adesope and Nesbit's 2012 redundancy meta-analysis.[^s2][^s3] Assertion-evidence slides are one specific technique within it. Signposting does the same job in speech that headings and highlights do on screen. Rehearsal and vocal delivery cover the speaking side the slides are meant to support.[^s1][^s4]
