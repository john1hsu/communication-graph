steps:
  - Expect your heart rate, breathing and alertness to rise before the talk, and stop treating that as proof that something is wrong.
  - Remind yourself, in a sentence, what the arousal is for - it moves oxygen and attention to where you need them for the task.
  - Just before you start, say "I am excited" (aloud or under your breath) instead of telling yourself to calm down.
  - Turn the excitement toward the opportunity - name one thing that could go well, such as the decision you want the audience to make.
  - Pair the reframe with preparation and rehearsal; it changes how the nerves feel, not whether you know your material.
  - Afterwards, judge the talk by what the audience understood or decided, not by how anxious you felt.
example:
  situation: A finance analyst is about to present a quarterly variance review to the leadership team for the first time and notices her hands shaking in the corridor.
  before: "Calm down, calm down. They'll see I'm nervous. Just get through the slides without messing up."
  after: "My heart's racing because this matters - that's energy I can use. I'm excited to show them why the logistics overspend is a one-off and get the forecast approved."
  why: The first version fights the arousal, which rarely works, and keeps attention on what could go wrong. The second accepts the arousal, labels it as excitement and points it at a concrete opportunity - the shift the reappraisal studies tested.

# Anxiety reappraisal

Anxiety reappraisal is a quick mental reframe for the nerves that come before a presentation, interview or other judged performance. Instead of trying to calm down, you accept the arousal - fast heartbeat, alertness, butterflies - and relabel it as excitement or as useful energy for the task.[^s1][^s2] It is not a cure for public speaking anxiety; it is a way to change what the arousal means to you in the minutes before you speak.

## Origin

Two research strands, both from the early 2010s, gave the technique its shape. Jeremy Jamieson and colleagues developed *arousal reappraisal*: before a stressful speech, participants read short summaries explaining that stress responses evolved to help performance by delivering oxygen where the body needs it.[^s2][^s3] Alison Wood Brooks at Harvard Business School tested a shorter version: simply saying "I am excited" rather than "I am calm" before singing, speaking or doing maths under pressure.[^s1] Brooks argued that anxiety and excitement are both high-arousal states, so moving from one to the other only changes how the feeling is read, whereas calming down asks people to lower their arousal too - which is harder.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The default advice before a presentation is to relax, but people under evaluation often cannot simply switch off arousal. In Brooks's studies, heart rate stayed high in every condition, and reappraisal did not reduce self-reported anxiety; what it did was increase excitement and shift people toward seeing the task as an opportunity rather than a threat.[^s1] Jamieson's team found that reappraisal increased people's sense that they had the resources to cope and produced a healthier cardiovascular pattern (more blood pumped, less vessel constriction) without dampening the stress response itself.[^s2] For anyone who presents at work, that is the appeal: the technique does not ask you to stop feeling nervous, only to read the feeling differently.

## How to apply it

Use the steps above in the last few minutes before you speak. The two published versions differ in form. The short version is a self-statement - "I am excited" - said just before you start.[^s1] The longer version is a minute or two of reminding yourself why stress arousal helps: a faster heart and breathing deliver more oxygen and focus to the task.[^s3][^s4] Both work by changing the label, not by suppressing the body's response, so do not combine them with a frantic effort to relax. Aim the excitement at something concrete that could go well, since Brooks's mechanism study found that an opportunity mind-set carried the benefit.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as a substitute for preparation.** None of the studies tested reappraisal in place of knowing the material; it is a final-minutes add-on to rehearsal, not a replacement.[^s1][^s5]
- **Expecting the anxiety to disappear.** Reappraisal raised excitement but left self-reported anxiety and heart rate largely unchanged in the original studies, so still feeling nervous does not mean it failed.[^s1]
- **Broadcasting excitement where it costs you.** Brooks noted that showing excitement could backfire in settings such as negotiations, where it reveals information.[^s1]
- **Using it for a clinical-level fear.** Reappraisal helped socially anxious and non-anxious people alike in single lab sessions, but the authors did not present it as a standalone treatment for social anxiety disorder.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The supporting studies are real but small. Brooks (2014) reported that 140 people who said "I am excited" before a judged speech were rated by observers as more persuasive, competent and confident than those who said "I am calm".[^s1] In a speech-and-arithmetic stress test with 85 community members, about half with social anxiety disorder, arousal reappraisal reduced observed anxiety and shame, with only a marginal gain in rated speech quality.[^s3] A classroom field experiment with 93 community-college maths students found that a short reappraisal reading lowered exam anxiety and raised exam scores compared with a placebo, though it collected no physiological measures.[^s4]

The performance claim is where the evidence is contested. A 2025 direct replication of Brooks's public-speaking study reproduced the self-reported shift - people felt more excited - but found no difference in any observer rating of persuasiveness, competence, confidence or persistence, and no difference on an indirect anxiety measure.[^s5] The fair reading for practitioners: reappraisal reliably changes how arousal feels and is cheap to try, but do not count on it alone to make the audience rate you higher.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

- **Addresses** public speaking anxiety by changing how pre-talk arousal is interpreted rather than trying to remove it.[^s1]
- **Applied in** presentations and other judged speeches, the setting of Brooks's public-speaking study.[^s1]
- **Introduced by** Alison Wood Brooks (the "I am excited" self-statement) and Jeremy Jamieson (arousal reappraisal).[^s1][^s2]
- **Demonstrated by** Brooks (2014), and **supported by** Jamieson, Nock and Mendes (2013), Beltzer et al. (2014) and the 2016 classroom field experiment.[^s2][^s3][^s4]
- **Challenged by** Poynter and Smith Pasqualini (2025), whose replication found the feeling shift but not the performance gain.[^s5]
- **Related:** presentation rehearsal, the illusion of transparency and social anxiety, which shape how much the nerves get in the way.[^s2][^s3]
