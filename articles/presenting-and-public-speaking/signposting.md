steps:
  - Before drafting cues, write your talk's three to five main points as one-line headlines; signposts can only show a structure that already exists.
  - In the opening, give a one-sentence preview that names the points in order ("I'll cover the problem, two options, and what I need from you").
  - At every move between main points, say a full transition sentence that closes the last point and names the next one ("That's the cost; now the timeline").
  - Flag the one or two things the audience must remember with an explicit emphasis cue ("If you keep one number from today, it's this").
  - After a dense or long section, give a one-sentence internal summary before moving on.
  - Close by reviewing the same points you previewed, in the same words, then state the decision or action you need.
  - In rehearsal, check that each cue is spoken aloud and matches the slide or section it introduces.
example:
  situation: An operations lead has eight minutes in a weekly leadership meeting to explain why a warehouse migration slipped and what she needs.
  before: "So, the migration. We had some vendor problems in August, and the scanners came late, and then there was the labour issue, and also the cutover weekend clashed with the audit... anyway, the new date is probably mid-December, and it'd help to get some budget for temp staff."
  after: "I'll cover three things: why we slipped, the new date, and one decision I need from you. First, why we slipped: two causes - late scanners and the audit clash. That's the cause; second, the new date: 14 December, with one risk. Finally, the decision: approve temp staff for cutover week. So - two causes, a new date, and one approval."
  why: The preview lets listeners hold a three-part map, the transitions mark when each part ends, and the closing review repeats the structure, so executives who drift for a minute can rejoin and the request is not lost at the end.

# Signposting

Signposting is the speaker's habit of telling the audience, out loud, how the talk is organised: what is coming, where one point ends and the next begins, and which idea matters most. Communication textbooks group the tools into transitions (sentences that link points), signposts (short words or phrases such as "similarly", "on the other hand" or "finally"), internal previews that announce what comes next, and internal summaries that recap what was just covered.[^s6] A reader can see headings and paragraphs; a listener cannot scroll back, so the speaker has to make the structure audible.[^s6][^s5]

## Origin

Previews, transitions and summaries are long-standing staples of public-speaking instruction, and current speech-centre handouts still teach them as the basic way to move between ideas: start a transition with a signpost word, write it as a full sentence, and place one after the introduction, between every main point and before the conclusion.[^s5] Communication-education researchers later tested the same moves as "organizational cues" in lectures, and educational psychologists study the broader family of cues that highlight structure and key content under the name *signaling*.[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

In a 2001 experiment using scripted, videotaped lectures, students learned more immediately afterwards when the lecture contained organizational cues and they took notes, and the cued lectures also helped delayed retention when combined with an engaging delivery style.[^s1] A follow-up study by the same researcher found that listeners typically capture under 40% of lecture content in their notes, and that prominent organizational statements led them to record more main points and details - which in turn predicted test scores.[^s2] At work the stakes are similar: an executive who loses the thread of a status update misses the ask, and without connecting cues a talk can feel like a list of unrelated good ideas.[^s6]

## How to apply it

Build the structure first, then make it audible. Preview the points in the opening, mark every move between points with a transition sentence that closes one idea and opens the next, and use a "review-preview" when a section ends: one line of recap, one line on what follows.[^s5] Use emphasis cues sparingly for the single idea listeners must keep, and end by reviewing the same points you previewed so the map you gave at the start is confirmed at the finish.[^s6] On slides, a visible agenda or section marker can reinforce the spoken cue, but it should not replace it; the research cited here tested spoken cues in lectures and visual or verbal cues in learning materials, not slides alone.[^s1][^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Mechanical signposts with no structure behind them.** "Moving on" or "next slide" tells listeners that something changed, not how the new point relates to the last one; a good transition names both.[^s5]
- **Previewing one structure and delivering another.** If the opening promises three parts and the talk wanders into five, the cue misleads rather than helps; internal summaries and the closing review should mirror the preview.[^s6]
- **Over-signalling.** Flagging every point as "really important" erases the contrast that emphasis cues rely on; meta-analyses note that effects depend on how and when cues are used.[^s3][^s4]
- **Treating cues as a substitute for delivery.** In the lecture studies, organizational cues worked best alongside engaging, immediate delivery and active note-taking, not in isolation.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The broader signaling literature is supportive but bounded. A 2020 meta-analysis of 29 experiments (44 effect sizes, about 2,700 learners) found an overall benefit of d = 0.38 for cues in multimedia learning, with large variation between studies and stronger effects in better-controlled designs.[^s3] A 2021 research summary drawing on several meta-analyses rated the effect as strong for retention and moderate for transfer, attributed it mainly to reduced cognitive load, and warned that most studies come from classrooms rather than job training and that results for moderators such as prior knowledge are inconsistent.[^s4] Evidence on spoken signposting specifically comes mostly from student-lecture experiments, so its size in short workplace briefings is an extrapolation; the status here is therefore *mixed* rather than robust.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

Signposting is applied in the presentation context, where listeners depend on spoken structure.[^s6] It is supported by the 2001 lecture-cue experiment (`cm:titsworth-2001`) and by the 2020 signaling meta-analysis (`cm:alpizar-adesope-wong-2020`).[^s1][^s3] Its usual explanation is cognitive load theory: cues reduce the effort needed to work out how ideas fit together.[^s4] It sits under public speaking and message structure, pairs naturally with an answer-first opening such as bottom line up front or the Pyramid Principle, and with thought groups and presentation rehearsal for delivery; its internal summaries are the speaker-side counterpart of a listener's summarizing.[^s5][^s6]
