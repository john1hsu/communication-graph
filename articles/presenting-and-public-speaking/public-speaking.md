# Public speaking

Public speaking is the skill of preparing and delivering a spoken message to a group, whether that is a team update, a client pitch, a training session or a toast at a leaving do. Textbooks usually sort speeches by general purpose: informing (a report to coworkers, job training), persuading (a sales pitch, a proposal) or marking an occasion (an award, a farewell), with real talks often mixing all three.[^s2] It is the capability behind every presentation (`cm:presentation`); the presentation is the situation, public speaking is what the speaker brings to it.

## Origin

The oldest systematic treatment is Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, which framed persuasive speaking as the ability to see what could persuade in a given case, and named three means of persuasion: the speaker's character, the audience's emotions and the argument itself.[^s6] Aristotle also split public speech into deliberative (advising on future action), judicial (accusing or defending past action) and ceremonial praise or blame, and his third book covers style, arrangement into a few parts and delivery.[^s6] Modern communication departments turned that tradition into teachable, gradable competencies. Schreiber, Paul and Shibley's Public Speaking Competence Rubric (2012) is one widely used version: an 11-item rubric tested on two samples of recorded speeches, which found a fairly simple three-factor structure and scores that tracked instructors' speech grades.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Most consequential workplace talk is spoken to several people at once, and the same competencies recur whether the room is a boardroom or a video call: picking what the audience needs, organising it, supporting claims and delivering it audibly and credibly.[^s2] The skill is also entangled with fear. Surveys keep finding speaking before a group among the most commonly named fears; a 2012 replication of a 1973 survey found 61% of college students ranked it first.[^s3] Treating public speaking as a set of learnable competencies, rather than a talent some people have, gives anxious speakers something concrete to work on.[^s2][^s4]

## What good looks like

The PSCR-based competencies give a practical checklist.[^s1][^s2] A skilled speaker:

- chooses a topic and angle that suit this audience and occasion;[^s2]
- opens by orienting listeners and establishing why they should trust the speaker;[^s1]
- uses a clear organising pattern and closes by restating the point and giving closure;[^s2]
- finds and weaves in strong supporting material, and builds persuasive claims on credible evidence and sound reasoning;[^s1]
- uses clear, vivid language, varied voice and nonverbal behaviour that matches the words;
- adapts in the moment to the audience, and uses visual aids that help rather than compete.[^s2]

**Workplace example.** An analyst is asked to brief the operations team on a new returns process. *Before:* she reads twelve dense slides in order, ending with "any questions?" *After:* she opens with the one change that affects their shifts, signposts three steps, illustrates each with a real return from last week, and closes by restating what changes on Monday and who to ask. Why it works: the second version hits topic fit, introduction, organisation, supporting material and conclusion from the rubric, which is what listeners use to follow and remember a talk.[^s1][^s2]

## How to practise it

1. **Pick one competency per talk.** Use the rubric items as a menu and work on the weakest, rather than trying to fix everything at once.[^s1]
2. **Speak more, in rising stakes.** Graduated exposure, from a one-minute update to a full briefing, was among the most effective interventions in a 2025 review of public speaking anxiety studies (exposure-based effect g = 1.25).[^s3]
3. **Combine skill work with nerve work.** A university speech course that trained skills and exposed students to progressively harder speeches significantly reduced scores on the Personal Report of Public Speaking Anxiety, with a particularly strong effect for women.[^s4] The same review found skills training effective in the one study that tested it alone, and combined treatments strongest for the thinking and behavioural sides of anxiety.[^s3]
4. **Get scored feedback.** Ask a colleague to rate a rehearsal against the rubric items; rubric scores in testing tracked expert grades, so they are a reasonable proxy.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but uneven. Anxiety-reduction interventions show large average effects (overall g = 1.17), but heterogeneity was high and the 2025 review deliberately included only successful interventions, so it likely overstates what a typical programme achieves.[^s3] Course studies such as Hunter, Westwick and Haleta's are single-site case studies without control groups.[^s4] For wider outcomes, a Cornell review of youth public speaking programmes found only preliminary, mostly self-reported, cross-sectional evidence, with no effect sizes and no control for who chose to take part.[^s5] And the PSCR measures performance on classroom speeches; it was validated against instructors' grades, not against workplace results.[^s1] Treat claims that one course or trick transforms careers as outrunning the evidence.

## Connections

Public speaking is a component of broader communication competence (`cm:communication-competence`) and depends on audience analysis (`cm:audience-analysis`), the first rubric competency.[^s1] It is applied in the presentation (`cm:presentation`) context and in reports, training and pitches.[^s2] Its theory comes from rhetoric (`cm:rhetoric`) and is shaped by Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (`cm:aristotles-rhetoric`), whose appeals to character, emotion and argument still frame modern teaching.[^s6] Building the skill helps address public speaking anxiety (`cm:speaking-anxiety`), as shown by Hunter, Westwick and Haleta (2014) and the 2025 McWilliam, Beattie and Callow review.[^s4][^s3] Its delivery sub-skills include vocal delivery (`cm:vocal-delivery`), signposting (`cm:signposting`) and handling questions (`cm:handling-questions`), and presentation rehearsal (`cm:presentation-rehearsal`) and self-recording playback (`cm:self-recording-playback`) are the main ways to practise it.[^s1][^s2]
