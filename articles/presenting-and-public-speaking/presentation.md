# Presentation

A workplace presentation is a planned talk in which you hold the floor for a set time to inform a group, persuade it, or ask it for something: a project update, a proposal to a steering committee, a client pitch. Unlike a conversation, the audience mostly listens, and unlike a document, they cannot re-read what they missed, so the speaker carries the job of making the message easy to follow in real time.[^s4][^s6] Most workplace presentations also lean on slides, and the slides can help or get in the way.[^s1]

## Origin

Presenting is the modern business form of the classical oration, but the setting that dominates today, a speaker in a meeting room or video call talking over projected slides, is shaped by presentation software. Kosslyn and colleagues (2012) note how routine slide software has become in business, education, government and research, and studied it precisely because so many people now present this way.[^s1] Guidance on structure still borrows the old sequence of opening, body and close: university writing centres teach students to open broadly, narrow to a thesis, develop each point in turn, and close by summarising and widening back out.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Presentations are often where decisions, budgets and reputations get made, and audiences notice when they go badly. In a 2012 survey of 205 people who regularly attend slide presentations, about 85% said they had sat through talks whose main point was buried in irrelevant detail, and the most annoying problems also included slides too dense to absorb in time and speakers reading their slides word for word.[^s1] An audit of 140 publicly posted slideshows in the same paper found these flaws everywhere: over 90% of decks had lists longer than four items or bullets running beyond two lines, and research talks were no better than business ones.[^s1]

## What works here

**Set the goal before the slides.** Decide what you want the audience to know, feel and do; the goal also tells you afterwards whether the talk worked.[^s4] Find out who will be in the room, what they already know and what objections they hold; with a mixed group, say so and spend a minute laying foundations before going deeper.[^s4]

**Give it a simple, audible structure.** Abrahams suggests "What? So what? Now what?": state the point plainly, say why it matters to this audience, then say what you want them to do or decide next.[^s5] Build a spoken transition between each main point so listeners know where they are.[^s6]

**Design slides for limited attention.** People can take in only so much at once, so keep each slide to one point, cut decorative detail, and avoid long bullet lists the audience must read while you talk.[^s1] In a controlled study with 110 engineering students, slides with a full-sentence headline and a supporting visual produced better essay answers (d = 0.81) and better recall about a week later (d = 0.89) than default topic-and-bullets slides, with fewer misconceptions.[^s2] The study used students and a technical topic, so treat it as strong guidance rather than proof for every business talk.[^s2]

**Rehearse aloud, with your visuals and a listener.** Practice is the most important part of delivery: time yourself, use the real slides, and ask someone to watch and give feedback; speak from a brief outline rather than reading or memorising a script.[^s6][^s4]

**Plan for questions.** Paraphrase each question before answering; it shows you listened, checks you understood, and gives you a moment to think.[^s4]

**Example.** A team lead has 15 minutes with department heads to propose a shared on-call rota. *Before:* she opens with her name and an agenda, then shows twelve bulleted slides of incident history, reads them aloud, and reaches the proposal with two minutes left. *After:* she opens with "Outages are costing each of your teams about a day a month; I want your agreement today to pilot a shared rota", spends most of the slot on three headline-and-chart slides showing the cost, the proposal and the risks, and closes by asking for the pilot decision before taking questions. The second version gives the audience the point, the reason and the ask in order, and leaves time to decide.[^s5][^s2]

## Pitfalls

- **Burying the point.** Irrelevant detail that hides the main message is the problem audiences report most often.[^s1]
- **Reading the slides.** Speakers who read slides or notes word for word are among the most irritating, and using speech to rescue overloaded slides tends to make listeners tune out.[^s1]
- **Overloaded slides.** Long lists and multi-line bullets ask the audience to read and listen at once.[^s1][^s2]
- **Planning around a "10-minute attention span".** The widely repeated claim that attention collapses after 10-15 minutes rests on few primary studies, weak methods and a much-cited review that barely measured attention; Bradbury (2016) concludes the data do not support it, and that attention varies more with the speaker than with the format.[^s3] Change pace and invite interaction because it keeps people engaged, not because a clock says so.[^s3]
- **Trusting your eye for slides.** Observers asked to pick the better of two slides chose wrongly about a fifth of the time and often could not explain correct choices, so check slides against explicit rules rather than instinct.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Demonstrated by** Kosslyn et al. (2012), whose audit, audience survey and perception experiment document how common and how annoying slide-presentation flaws are.[^s1]
- **Explained by** cognitive load: the authors ground several of their principles in the limited amount of information people can hold and process at once.[^s1]
- **Complements** assertion-evidence slides, which Garner and Alley (2013) found improved comprehension and recall in a presentation setting.[^s2]
- **Complements** presentation rehearsal, which writing-centre guidance treats as the most important rule of delivery.[^s6]
- **Complements** paraphrasing, recommended for handling audience questions.[^s4]
- **Requires** audience analysis, since the goal and depth depend on who is in the room.[^s4] It also relates to public speaking, speaking anxiety, executive briefings and death by PowerPoint.[^s1][^s6]
