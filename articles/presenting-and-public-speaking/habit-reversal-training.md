steps:
  - Record two or three short practice talks and count your fillers per minute to get a baseline.
  - Write a one-line description of the habit you are targeting, such as "um" or "uh" at the start of a sentence.
  - Replay a recording and mark every filler the moment you hear it, until you catch nearly all of them.
  - Practise the competing response - close your mouth and take a brief silent pause - each time you feel a filler coming.
  - Give a new practice talk, ideally with a colleague tapping the table when you miss one, and recount your rate.
  - Repeat a short booster session before high-stakes talks if your rate drifts back up.

# Habit reversal training

Habit reversal training is a behavioural method for breaking a repetitive habit by making the person aware of each occurrence and teaching a response that cannot happen at the same time. For speakers, the usual target is filled pauses - "um", "uh", clicks and overused "like" - and the competing response is a short silent pause.[^s3][^s5] It is one of the few remedies for filler words that has been tested directly in public speaking studies rather than passed on as coaching folklore.

## Origin

Psychologists Nathan Azrin and R. G. Nunn introduced habit reversal in 1973 in *Behaviour Research and Therapy*. They argued that nervous habits persist because they are chained to other actions, run largely outside awareness, get heavy practice and are socially tolerated. Their package taught clients to notice each instance, practise a movement opposite to the habit, and get social support for holding back. In twelve clients with habits such as nail-biting, tics and lisping, a single session nearly eliminated the habit on the first day.[^s1] Behaviour analysts later stripped the method down to its awareness and competing-response parts and applied it to speech: Mancuso and Miltenberger's 2016 study targeted "uh", "um", "er", clicks and misused "like" in student speeches.[^s3]

## Why it matters

High filler rates make a speaker sound less prepared, and most advice for fixing them is simply "stop saying um", which does not tell you how. Habit reversal gives a concrete routine. All six speakers in the 2016 study cut their filled pauses immediately after training.[^s3] In a 2017 study of four university students, awareness training reduced filled pauses, tongue clicks and overused "like", and participants said they used less filler and felt they spoke better overall.[^s4] The method suits workplace speaking because its components are short: Pawlik and Perrin (2020) tested a brief version - awareness training plus a silent pause - and all four participants reduced their disfluencies.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Start with awareness. In a 2022 component analysis, simply describing the habit precisely reduced disfluencies for 4 of 9 speakers; the rest needed practice detecting it on video, and some also needed live detection practice with feedback, before their rate fell.[^s6] So begin with a clear description, add recording-based spotting if that is not enough, and add a colleague who signals each miss if you still slip. Then pair awareness with the competing response: when you sense a filler coming, close your mouth and pause. Speakers in the 2017 study needed booster sessions to keep improving, so plan a quick refresher before an important talk.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the baseline.** Without a count from a recording you cannot tell whether training worked; every speech study measured rate before and after.[^s3][^s4]
- **Practising only alone.** Gains in solo practice do not always carry over. In the 2020 study, the effect generalised to a real class presentation for only one of four participants, so test the habit in front of people.[^s5]
- **Treating the pause as optional.** The competing response is what replaces the sound; one 2020 participant used it only moderately.[^s5]
- **Expecting a one-off fix.** Booster sessions were needed for continued gains, and side effects on untargeted behaviours and speech rate varied between speakers.[^s4][^s6]

## Evidence and limits

As a clinical treatment, habit reversal is well supported: a 2011 meta-analysis of 18 studies with 575 participants found a large effect against control conditions (d = 0.80) across tics, nail-biting, stuttering and other repetitive behaviours.[^s2] The public speaking evidence is narrower. The studies are small single-case designs with university students giving short speeches, and they show reliable reductions in filler counts rather than better business outcomes.[^s3][^s5][^s6] Generalisation to real audiences was inconsistent, which is why this node is marked mixed rather than robust.[^s5] Because a low filler rate costs a speaker little, the aim is to bring a high rate down, not to reach zero.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a vocal-delivery skill, since it changes how the speaker's voice fills planning gaps.[^s3]
- **Addresses** filled pauses: the awareness-plus-competing-response package cut "um" and "uh" for every participant in the first speaking study.[^s3]
- **Introduced by** Nathan Azrin, with R. G. Nunn, as a treatment for nervous habits and tics.[^s1]
- **Supported by** Bate et al. (2011), the meta-analysis showing a large effect across habit disorders.[^s2]
- **Applied in** presentations, where the effect carried over to a real class presentation for one speaker.[^s5]
- **Complements** presentation rehearsal: the training uses repeated practice speeches and booster sessions, so it fits naturally into rehearsal time.[^s4]
- **Related:** silence and pauses (the competing response) and public speaking anxiety.[^s5]
