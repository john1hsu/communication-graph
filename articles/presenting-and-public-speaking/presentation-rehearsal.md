steps:
  - Start at least several days ahead and split practice into short sessions (opening, body, close) rather than one late-night cram.
  - Rehearse the whole talk out loud, standing, at full volume, with the slides or notes you will actually use.
  - Time every full run-through and cut material until you finish comfortably inside the slot.
  - Record one run on your phone, watch it back, and note one or two specific delivery habits to fix.
  - Give at least one run-through to a small live audience - three or four colleagues - and ask each for one thing that was unclear and one thing to keep.
  - Do a final rehearsal in the real room or on the real video setup, then stop practising and sleep.

# Presentation rehearsal

Presentation rehearsal means practising a talk the way you will give it: out loud, standing, with your real slides or notes, timed, and at least once in front of other people who tell you what they heard.[^s6] It is the difference between knowing your material and being able to deliver it. Of all preparation activities studied in public speaking classes, rehearsing aloud and rehearsing before listeners are among the most consistently linked to better-rated speeches.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

"Practise your speech" is ancient advice with no single inventor, but its effects were first measured by communication-education researchers. In 1994 Menzel and Carrell surveyed 119 public speaking students about how they prepared and compared that with the quality of their speeches; total preparation time, the number of rehearsals given to an audience and time spent rehearsing aloud all tracked better performance.[^s1] Smith and Frymier (2006) then compared students who practised alone with those who practised in front of listeners.[^s2] University speaking centres have turned these findings into standard coaching routines.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Silent review hides the problems that sink real talks. A deck that "feels" ten minutes long often runs fourteen when spoken, transitions that look obvious on screen come out tangled, and nervous habits are invisible from the inside. In Menzel and Carrell's sample, both the time spent rehearsing aloud and the number of spoken rehearsals correlated with speech quality, while higher speech anxiety correlated with lower quality.[^s1] Pearson, Child and Kahl's 2006 analysis of semester-long preparation journals found that overall preparation time, and time spent on delivery and practice in particular, went with higher speech grades.[^s3] Students who rehearsed in front of an audience scored higher than those who rehearsed alone, and those who practised before four or more listeners scored higher than those with one to three.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Treat rehearsal as a series of short, purposeful sessions rather than one long cram. Grand Valley State University's Speech Lab advises starting at least a week out, practising sections at different times of day, rehearsing on your feet in a space like the real room, and using notes only as prompts rather than a script.[^s6] Run the full talk aloud with a timer, because spoken length is what counts. Record a run and listen back to it - the Speech Lab suggests replaying recordings during a commute to absorb the flow.[^s6] Then give one rehearsal to a small group of colleagues; the audience is the part of rehearsal with the clearest evidence behind it.[^s2] Ask them for specific reactions, such as where they got lost and what question they would ask, rather than general praise. For a video call, rehearse on the same camera, screen-share and microphone setup. Finally, stop in time to sleep; the Speech Lab recommends rest over a last late practice.[^s6]

## Common mistakes

- **Clicking through silently.** Silent review does correlate with quality, but spoken rehearsal is where timing and phrasing problems surface.[^s1]
- **Rehearsing only alone or in a mirror.** Audience practice is the condition linked to better scores, and larger practice audiences did better still.[^s2] A mirror is a last resort.[^s6]
- **Memorising a script word for word.** Coaching guidance favours knowing the structure and using notes as a guide, so delivery stays natural and one lost word does not derail you.[^s6]
- **Cramming the night before.** Spreading practice over several days and sleeping before the talk is the standard advice.[^s6]
- **Expecting nerves to vanish.** Repetition can take the edge off anxiety, but the decline is uneven, so pair rehearsal with other anxiety techniques.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The core evidence is correlational and comes from university speech courses: students who prepared more, rehearsed aloud and rehearsed before audiences got better ratings, but stronger or more motivated students may also simply prepare more, and grade point average itself predicted performance.[^s1][^s3] Smith and Frymier's audience comparison is a single study of student speakers.[^s2] For anxiety, a 2019 pilot with 19 adults giving three brief speeches to virtual audiences found distress dropped after the first speech, but only partially and not steadily within each speech; the sample was small and non-clinical.[^s5] More broadly, a 2014 meta-analysis found that deliberate practice explained 26% of performance differences in games but under 1% in professions, a caution against assuming that more hours alone guarantee excellence.[^s4] Rehearsal is therefore rated mixed: well-supported as a habit of good speakers, with modest and context-dependent effect sizes.

## Connections

- **Broader:** public speaking, the skill that rehearsal builds and is part of.[^s1]
- **Develops** public speaking: spoken and audience rehearsals predicted rated speech quality.[^s1]
- **Addresses** public speaking anxiety, weakly: repeated speeches produced partial habituation of distress.[^s5]
- **Applied in** presentations, from class talks to workplace briefings, as speaking-centre guidance describes.[^s6]
- **Supported by** Menzel and Carrell (1994), the preparation-performance survey, and Smith and Frymier (2006), the audience-practice study.[^s1][^s2]
- **Related:** habit reversal training uses practice speeches to fix filled pauses, and deliberate practice is the wider theory of structured practice - with the caveat that its effects in professional settings are small.[^s4]
