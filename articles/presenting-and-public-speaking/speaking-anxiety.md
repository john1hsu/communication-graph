# Public speaking anxiety

Public speaking anxiety is the fear, worry and physical arousal - racing heart, shaky hands, a wobbly voice - that people feel before and while speaking to a group.[^s6] It is the most commonly feared situation in both student and community samples, with roughly a fifth to a third of community respondents reporting it, according to a 2019 meta-analysis.[^s1] At work it shows up well beyond the keynote stage: a two-minute stand-up update, a client readout or a question asked in an all-hands can trigger it.

## Origin

Communication researcher James C. McCroskey built the first standard measure, the 34-item Personal Report of Public Speaking Anxiety, in 1970; it became the starting point for his wider work on communication apprehension, of which speaking anxiety is the public-speaking form.[^s5] On his norms, scores run from 34 to 170, with above 131 counted as high.[^s5] Social psychologists later linked the fear to the illusion of transparency: speakers believe their nerves are far more visible than they are.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Fear of public speaking is not just unpleasant. About half of people with it go on to develop broader social anxiety, the 2019 meta-analysis notes.[^s1] In a career, it often works by avoidance: people turn down presenting, let a colleague brief the executive, or stay quiet in meetings, so their work is less visible than it should be. When they do present, anxious rumination focuses attention on threats rather than on the opportunity to make their case.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

- **It is treatable.** Ebrahimi and colleagues (2019) pooled 30 randomized trials with 1,355 participants. Psychological interventions, mostly cognitive and behavioural, reduced fear of public speaking with a moderate-to-large effect (Hedges' g = 0.74). The effect was larger at follow-up (g = 1.11), suggesting people kept improving after treatment. Internet-delivered programmes did about as well as face-to-face ones.[^s1]
- **Limits.** Self-reported improvements were larger than physiological or behavioural ones. The authors found some signs of publication bias, many trial arms had fewer than 20 people, and almost all samples were self-identified adults rather than diagnosed patients.[^s1]
- **Audiences see less than you feel.** In a 2010 classroom study, speakers rated their own anxiety higher than audiences did, and the most anxious speakers overestimated their visibility the most. Savitsky and Gilovich (2003) found the same, and telling speakers this reduced the effect.[^s4]
- **"Get excited" is less settled than the advice suggests.** Brooks (2014) had people say "I am excited" rather than "I am calm" before a judged speech, and observers rated their speeches as more persuasive and competent.[^s2] A 2025 replication found the same self-reported shift but no difference in observer-rated performance.[^s3] Treat reappraisal as a way to feel better, not a proven way to perform better.

## How to spot it

- You avoid or hand off speaking opportunities that would help your work get seen.[^s1]
- Before a talk: anxious rumination focused on everything that could go wrong.[^s2]
- During it: sweaty palms, shaking hands, pacing, a shaky voice - signs that, the Hamilton College guidance notes, others notice far less than the speaker does.[^s6]
- Afterwards: judging the talk by how nervous you felt rather than by what the audience understood or decided.[^s4]

## How to fix it

1. **Prepare the content, then rehearse aloud and standing.** Knowing your material removes one source of worry. Rehearse out loud first alone and then with a small, friendly audience, getting closer to the real setting each time.[^s6]
2. **Take small, repeated exposures.** The evidence comes mostly from cognitive and behavioural programmes, including exposure. At work, volunteer for short, low-stakes slots (a two-minute update) before a long, high-stakes one.[^s1]
3. **Correct the transparency illusion.** Remind yourself that observers rate your nervousness lower than you do. Just knowing this helped speakers in the original study.[^s4]
4. **Prepare your body and the room.** See the room beforehand, find a friendly face, and do a few slow breaths and some stretching out of sight before you start.[^s6]
5. **Reframe arousal if it helps.** Calling your racing heart "excitement" may make you feel more confident, but do not count on it to improve how the audience rates you.[^s2][^s3]
6. **Get structured help when fear limits your career.** Guided cognitive-behavioural programmes, including internet-delivered ones, have trial evidence behind them. University speaking centres and counselling services offer practice and support.[^s1][^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader / instance of:** a form of communication apprehension, the wider construct McCroskey went on to measure with the PRCA scales.[^s5]
- **Supported by** the Ebrahimi et al. (2019) meta-analysis, which documents both how common it is and how treatable.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Savitsky and Gilovich (2003) and **supported by** MacInnis, Mackinnon and MacIntyre (2010), both on how speakers overestimate how visible their nerves are.[^s4]
- **Explained by** the illusion of transparency, which makes nerves feel visible and so raises them further.[^s4]
- **Related:** the spotlight effect, social anxiety, anxiety reappraisal (whose performance benefit failed to replicate in 2025), presentation rehearsal and team psychological safety.[^s1][^s3]
