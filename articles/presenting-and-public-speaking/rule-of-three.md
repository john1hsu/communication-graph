steps:
  - List every point or reason you could make, then rank them by how much each matters to this audience's decision.
  - Keep the top three as your main points; fold the rest in as sub-points under one of them or move them to a backup slide or appendix.
  - Phrase the three in parallel form - same grammar, similar length - so they sound like one set ("faster, cheaper, safer").
  - Preview the three in your opening and number them as you go ("first", "second", "third") so listeners can track where you are.
  - When you want an idea to land, put the most important or most concrete item last, then pause after the third item.
  - Before you present, check whether you are making a sales-style pitch; if so, resist adding a fourth or fifth benefit, because extra claims can read as overselling.
example:
  situation: A product manager has five minutes at a quarterly review to argue for moving a team to a new testing tool.
  before: "The new tool is faster, it has better reporting, it integrates with our tracker, the vendor's support is responsive, it's cheaper at our scale, our sister team likes it, and it should help with onboarding too."
  after: "I'm recommending we switch for three reasons: it cuts test runs from 40 minutes to 12, it costs 20% less at our size, and it plugs into the tracker we already use. The other benefits are on the backup slide."
  why: Seven unranked benefits blur together and start to sound like a sales pitch; three ranked, parallel reasons are easy to hold in mind and repeat to others, and nothing true is lost because the rest stays available.

# Rule of three

The rule of three is the practice of packaging what you say in threes: three main points in a talk, three reasons for a recommendation, or three parallel words or phrases in a key sentence. Classical rhetoric calls the sentence-level version a *tricolon* - three parallel units of matching length, as in Caesar's "I came, I saw, I conquered."[^s4] In workplace speaking it is mostly a structuring discipline: choose the three things that matter, say them in a form listeners can hold, and keep the rest in reserve.[^s5]

## Origin

The tricolon is one of the classical figures of parallelism and balance catalogued by rhetoricians since antiquity.[^s4] Its modern study as a speaking device comes from conversation analysis of political oratory: Max Atkinson identified three-part lists, alongside contrasts, as devices that signal to an audience when a point is complete and invite applause, and Heritage and Greatbatch tested this in a 1986 analysis of 476 speeches at British party conferences.[^s3] Consumer psychologists Suzanne Shu and Kurt Carlson later gave the advice its best-known experimental test, coining the phrase "charm of three" for the point at which extra claims stop helping.[^s1] Speech-teaching departments now routinely advise speakers to keep short talks to about three main points and to number them aloud.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Listeners cannot reread a talk, so the number of separate points a speaker asks them to track matters; the University of Pittsburgh's guidance advises no more than three main points for talks under ten minutes and warns that more than five makes a talk hard to follow and remember.[^s5] In persuasion, Carlson and Shu's experiments found that when people know the speaker is trying to persuade them, impressions improve with each positive claim up to three, but a fourth claim triggers skepticism that colours all the claims - so more evidence can make a pitch weaker.[^s1] In the speech studies, three-part lists accounted for 12.6% of collective applause, second only to contrasts, which suggests that audiences recognise the pattern and know when to respond.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Start by ranking, not by counting: decide which three points matter most to this audience, then subordinate or park everything else.[^s5] Phrase the three in parallel so they sound like a set, preview them in the opening and number them as you deliver them, which also works as a signposting cue.[^s5][^s4] Use the device sparingly at sentence level for the lines you most want remembered; an analysis of an engineering TED talk shows how a speaker can state three requirements in one sentence to make a technical case memorable.[^s6] Delivery still matters: researchers who followed up Atkinson's work found that stress, rhythm and timing are what tell the audience a list is complete, so pause after the third item rather than rushing on.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Padding to reach three.** Inventing a weak third point to fit the pattern dilutes the two strong ones; the rule is a ceiling for main points, not a quota.[^s5]
- **Overselling.** Adding a fourth and fifth benefit to a pitch can tip listeners into doubting all of them, especially when they already see you as an advocate.[^s1]
- **Treating it as a guarantee.** Three-part lists in speeches drew applause, but delivery and content also shaped responses, and not all applause followed the textbook devices.[^s3]
- **Mechanical overuse.** Stringing triplets through every sentence makes the device obvious and tiresome; one or two well-placed lists carry more weight.[^s6]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but narrower than the popular "three is magic" claim. Carlson and Shu's experiments showed the peak at three only when a persuasion motive was present, and examined how cognitive load and claim order change the pattern.[^s1] A 2022 study in the *Journal of Consumer Psychology* replicated the charm-of-three effect for readers who were not forming mental images, but found that when they were imagining the product, more than three claims produced better evaluations, through greater immersion and less skepticism.[^s2] The applause research is observational and drawn from political speeches; later studies found that only about 61% of applause was synchronised with speech as Atkinson described and that delivery largely determines whether a list works.[^s3] Most of this work comes from advertising and political rhetoric, not internal business presentations, so applying it to a team meeting is reasonable extrapolation rather than proof; the status here is therefore *mixed*.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

The rule of three is applied in the presentation context, where the Pitt guidance recommends about three numbered main points.[^s5] Its main experimental basis is Carlson and Shu's charm-of-three studies (`cm:shu-carlson-2014`), and the observational basis is Heritage and Greatbatch's 1986 applause study (`cm:heritage-greatbatch-1986`).[^s1][^s3] It is challenged, or at least bounded, by Wang and colleagues' 2022 finding that mental imagery reverses the effect (`cm:wang-et-al-2022`).[^s2] It complements signposting, because numbered, previewed points are themselves structural cues, and it sits under message structure and persuasion, beside rhetorical appeals and the wider study of rhetoric.[^s4][^s5] It pairs naturally with an elevator pitch or the Pyramid Principle, which both ask for a short set of grouped supporting points, and with primacy and recency effects and cognitive load, which explain why the number and order of points affects what listeners keep.[^s5][^s1]
