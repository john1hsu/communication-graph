# Concise writing

Concise writing is the skill of getting a message across in as few strong words as the reader needs. Purdue's Online Writing Lab stresses that this is not the same as using the fewest possible words: the aim is that every word earns its place, and weak or redundant ones are cut or replaced with a more precise word.[^s2] U.S. federal plain-language guidance frames it the same way, telling writers to question every word and ask whether their audience actually needs it.[^s1] At work it applies to words, sentences and whole ideas.[^s3]

## Origin

The best-known statement of the rule is William Strunk Jr.'s command to "omit needless words," from the short style guide he privately printed in 1918 for his English classes at Cornell; E. B. White, a former student, revised and expanded it as *The Elements of Style* in 1959.[^s5] Federal plain-language guidance later made cutting excess words standard in official writing.[^s1] More recently, behavioural scientists Todd Rogers and Jessica Lasky-Fink tested the advice in field experiments and made "less is more" one of six principles in their 2023 book *Writing for Busy Readers*.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Work readers are busy and skim. Rogers and Lasky-Fink argue that readers judge how much effort a message will take partly from its length, and that long messages are more often skimmed, abandoned partway or set aside, so requests and deadlines get missed.[^s3] In one of their surveys, 165 of 166 professionals said they would deal with the shorter of two otherwise similar messages first.[^s3] Length also costs time: the authors estimate that one three-paragraph email to 120 staff consumes about four hours of combined reading.[^s3]

## What good looks like

- **Every word does a job.** Vague phrases are replaced by one specific word, and intensifiers such as "very", "really", "totally" and "actually" are dropped unless they change the meaning.[^s1][^s2]
- **No doublets.** Legal and bureaucratic pairs such as "due and payable" or "cease and desist" become one word ("due", "stop").[^s1]
- **Sentences are combined, not stacked.** Information that does not need its own sentence is folded into another one.[^s2]
- **Terms stay consistent.** The same thing keeps the same name, because swapping in synonyms for variety makes readers wonder whether you mean something different.[^s1]

**Example.** A team lead asks 40 colleagues to complete a capacity survey. The first draft runs to three paragraphs on why leadership wants the data, how last year's planning went and what the survey covers, with the link and deadline in the last line. The revision reads: "Please complete this five-minute capacity survey by Thursday so we can staff Q1 projects. [link]. Questions: reply here." This mirrors Rogers and Lasky-Fink's school-board experiment, where a 49-word survey request drew a 4.8% response rate against 2.7% for a 127-word version with the same core content.[^s3]

## How to practise it

1. **Edit for subtraction on purpose.** Adams and colleagues found across eight experiments that people trying to improve something default to adding elements and overlook changes that remove them, especially when nothing prompts them to consider removal or when they are under cognitive load.[^s4] Rogers and Lasky-Fink report that in one of the Adams studies, 83% of participants asked to improve a summary added words instead of cutting.[^s3] So ask "what can I delete?" before revising.
2. **Interrogate each word,** asking whether it adds something important and unique; delete it or replace it if not.[^s2]
3. **Sweep for modifiers and doublets** with your editor's find function, then reread the sentence without each one.[^s1]
4. **Cut at the idea level.** List the points in your draft, mark the ones the reader needs in order to act, and move the rest to a link or a follow-up.[^s3]
5. **Ask a reader, not an expert.** Federal guidance recommends having subject-matter experts review text from the audience's point of view, because the writer rarely notices which words only insiders need.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The core claim has direct field support: in the randomized school-board experiment run by Rogers and Lasky-Fink, the shorter email lifted responses by about 80% and also led more respondents to expect the survey to be quick (29% versus 15%), which fits their argument that length signals effort.[^s3] Adams et al. (2021) help explain why writers seldom cut on their own.[^s4] The status here is `mixed` rather than `robust`, because the workplace evidence comes from a small number of studies, mostly one-to-many requests where a quick response is the goal, and the authors report them in their own book rather than as a body of independent replications.[^s3] The guidance itself warns against reading concision as minimum word count: Purdue's emphasis is on the strongest words, not the fewest, and federal guidance asks writers to judge each word by what their particular audience needs.[^s2][^s1] In practice that means over-cutting is its own failure: a reader new to the topic may lose context they needed, and a one-line reply that suits a close peer may read as curt to a client or a senior leader from a more indirect culture, so add back the greeting, the reason or the background that this reader needs.

## Connections

- **Part of plain language:** federal plain-language guidance treats cutting excess words and modifiers as one of its core principles.[^s1]
- **Addresses wordiness,** the target of Purdue OWL's methods of replacing vague words, interrogating each word and combining sentences.[^s2]
- **Addresses information overload and is applied in email:** Rogers and Lasky-Fink frame shorter messages as the writer's answer to readers who face dozens or hundreds of emails a day.[^s3]
- **Supported by** *Writing for Busy Readers*, whose field experiments supply most of the direct evidence for concise workplace messages.[^s3]
- **Addresses subtraction neglect:** deliberately asking what to delete counters the default toward additive changes documented by Adams et al.[^s4]
- **Related:** Strunk's "omit needless words" is the classic source of the advice.[^s5] It pairs with active voice, bottom line up front and readability formulas (see those articles for their sources).
