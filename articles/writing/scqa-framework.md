steps:
  - Write the answer first, in one sentence - the recommendation you want the reader to accept or act on.
  - Write the question that answer responds to, phrased as "how should we..." rather than "should we...", and pitched at a decision the reader can actually make.
  - Write the situation - one to three sentences of facts the reader already knows and would not dispute, with a concrete figure or date.
  - Write the complication - the specific change that makes the situation untenable, quantified where you can, and tight enough to raise only your one question.
  - Read the four parts aloud in order; if the complication does not lead naturally to the question, or the answer does not answer it, fix the weak link before drafting the body.
  - Put the answer at the end of the opening paragraph or slide, then organise the rest of the document as support for it.
example:
  situation: A support team lead emails the head of customer operations to ask for a change to weekend staffing.
  before: "Hi Priya, I wanted to share some thoughts on the weekend queue. We've been looking at the numbers over the last few months and there are a few things going on, including some changes in ticket mix, some staffing gaps and a couple of tooling issues. I've put together some options below for discussion..."
  after: "Priya - Situation: our weekday queue meets the four-hour response target. Complication: since the March launch, weekend tickets have doubled and weekend response time is now eleven hours, with churn complaints rising. Question: how do we bring weekends back to target without new hires? Answer: move two agents to a Tuesday-Saturday rota from next month; details and costs below."
  why: The rewrite starts from facts Priya already accepts, names one quantified problem, and states the question and answer in the first paragraph, so she can approve or push back without reading the analysis.
selfCheck:
  - Would my reader agree with every sentence in my situation without needing proof?
  - Does my complication raise exactly the question I answer, and not a different one?
  - Is my question one this reader has the authority to decide?
  - Could someone who stops after my opening paragraph say what I recommend?

# SCQA framework

The SCQA framework is a four-part way to open a business document or presentation. You state the **situation** - facts the reader already knows and accepts; the **complication** - what has changed to make that situation a problem; the **question** the complication raises; and your **answer**, the recommendation the rest of the document supports.[^s2][^s4] Its job is to bring a reader from common ground to your conclusion in a few sentences, so they understand both what you recommend and why it matters now.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

SCQA comes from the writing method Barbara Minto developed at McKinsey & Company, where consultants needed a faster and more persuasive way to present complex recommendations to clients.[^s1] Within that method - now taught as the pyramid principle or "Minto approach" - SCQA is the introduction that sets up the analysis, while the pyramid organises the supporting argument beneath the answer.[^s1][^s4] The framework has since spread well beyond consulting: universities teach it for business papers, and quality-improvement practitioners use it to report the results of structured problem solving.[^s1][^s4]

## Why it matters

Readers rarely care about your answer until they feel the problem. Opening with agreed facts lowers resistance, a quantified complication creates urgency, and an explicit question tells the reader exactly what the document will resolve.[^s2][^s3] It also disciplines the writer: if you cannot state a single question that your complication raises, your analysis probably has no clear point yet.[^s4] For time-pressed senior readers, the first three parts can each be a single sentence, short enough to fit on one or two slides before the answer.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Draft backwards from the answer, then check that each part leads to the next.[^s2] Keep the situation uncontroversial and concrete - figures, dates, trends the reader would not dispute - and build from broad context to the specific facts that matter.[^s2][^s3] Make the complication credible with data and connect it to what the reader values, such as cost, customers or risk.[^s2] Frame the question around how to act rather than whether to act, and match it to the reader's decision rights.[^s2][^s3] Then lead with the answer: a busy reader should be able to grasp your recommendation from the opening paragraph alone, with two to four supporting themes following it.[^s3]

In a live presentation, the same sequence works as the first one or two slides; the answer comes next, followed by its supporting points and then the data behind each.[^s4] In a short email, SCQA can shrink to four sentences, as in the example above.[^s2][^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **A disputed situation.** If the reader argues with your opening facts, the frame collapses; claims that need proof belong in the body, not the situation.[^s2][^s3]
- **A vague or compound complication.** A complication should be specific enough to raise only one question; several loosely related problems make the reader unsure what you are answering.[^s4]
- **History instead of context.** A long account of how the team did the work pushes the recommendation out of the first paragraph, where a busy reader looks for it.[^s3]
- **Saving the answer for the end of the document.** SCQA is an opening, not a suspense plot; the answer belongs at the close of the introduction, before the evidence.[^s3][^s4]
- **A "whether" question.** Asking whether to act invites a debate you have already settled; asking how to act moves the reader to the decision.[^s2]

## Evidence

SCQA itself has not been tested in controlled studies; support for it comes from consulting and teaching practice, so its evidence status is practitioner.[^s1][^s4] Indirect support comes from reading research: problem-and-solution is one of a small set of recognised expository text structures,[^s6] and in a training study with younger and older adults, teaching readers to use text structure - combined with clear signals in the text - increased total recall and recall of the most important information.[^s5] Those findings concern readers' strategies rather than the SCQA opening specifically, so they suggest why an explicit problem-to-answer structure helps but do not show that this four-part formula outperforms other clear openings.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader:** SCQA is a pattern of message structure - a deliberate order for the opening of a message.[^s2]
- **Part of / introduced by:** it is the introduction within Barbara Minto's pyramid principle, developed at McKinsey.[^s1]
- **Structures:** it gives business papers and reports their opening.[^s1][^s2]
- **Applied in:** presentations, where the first three parts can sit on one or two slides before the answer.[^s4]
- **Complements:** bottom line up front - SCQA supplies the minimum context, BLUF insists the answer arrives in the opening paragraph.[^s3]
- **Supported by:** Meyer and Poon's 2001 structure-strategy study, as indirect evidence that explicit text structure aids recall.[^s5]
- **Related:** MECE grouping and signposting organise what follows the answer; executive summaries and narrative persuasion use a similar problem-to-resolution arc.[^s1][^s4]
- **Requires:** audience analysis, because the situation must be what *this* reader already accepts and the question one they can decide.[^s2][^s3]
