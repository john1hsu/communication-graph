# Plain language

Plain language is the principle of writing for a specific audience so that those readers can find, understand and act on the content without rereading or asking for help.[^s1] It is not "dumbing down": U.S. federal guidance calls that a common myth and says the right level depends on who is reading, so a note for specialists can use their technical terms while a note for the public cannot.[^s1] Its usual tools are writing for the reader, organising so the main point comes first, active voice, short sections, lists and tables, and familiar words instead of jargon.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Plain-language campaigns grew out of law, government and business, where long, legalistic documents cost readers time and money; legal-writing scholar Joseph Kimble's book surveys sixty empirical studies across those three sectors.[^s5] In the United States the principle became law with the Plain Writing Act of 2010, which requires executive-branch agencies to write new or substantially revised public documents clearly, concisely and in a well-organised way suited to their audience. The Act also requires staff training, a named senior official and annual compliance reports, and OMB guidance in 2011 pointed agencies to the federal plain language guidelines.[^s3]

## Why it matters

At work, most writing is read by people with less context than the writer: a client reading a status update, an executive skimming a recommendation, a new hire following a process note. Digital.gov notes that writers often fail to notice that terms they know well may mean little to their readers, and that jargon draws more reader complaints than any other writing fault.[^s2] Plain language turns that blind spot into a routine check. In Kimble's summary of comparative studies, plain versions were preferred by about two-thirds of nearly 300 U.S. judges who rated a lawsuit paper, and members of the public answered questions about plain-language court forms correctly 81–95% of the time, against 61–65% for the originals.[^s4]

## How to apply it

1. Name the reader and what they already know before drafting; if several audiences share one document, give each its own section.[^s1]
2. Lead with what the reader needs to do or decide, then summarise long material up front.[^s1]
3. Write in the active voice, so each sentence says who does what.[^s1]
4. Replace insider terms with everyday words; keep a technical term only when it prevents misunderstanding, and define it where it first appears rather than in a glossary.[^s2]
5. Test the draft on real readers instead of judging it yourself, because plain language is defined by whether it works for them.[^s5]

**Example.** A project lead updating a client might write: "Following UAT, P1 defects have been remediated and the cutover is contingent on CAB sign-off." In plain language: "We fixed all the urgent bugs your team found in testing. We can switch to the new system once our change board approves it, which we expect on Thursday." The second version names the actors, swaps internal acronyms for the reader's words and states what happens next, which is the pattern the federal guidance recommends.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence

Studies of rewritten documents generally favour plain versions. Kimble reports that in an early-1980s test of revised FCC marine-radio rules, correct answers rose from about 53% to 84% while answer time nearly halved, and in a mid-1990s survey 61% of responding Michigan lawyers preferred a judicial opinion revised for plainness.[^s4] Much of this evidence comes from advocates, relies on surveys with modest response rates and compares whole rewrites, so it cannot say which specific technique did the work.[^s4][^s5] A 2022 systematic review of plain language summaries of research screened 7,714 records, found 33 empirical studies with heterogeneous designs, and concluded that most criteria in writing guidelines had no empirical support yet.[^s6] The status here is therefore `mixed`: rewriting for an audience reliably helps in the studies available, but the evidence for individual rules is thin and should not be treated as settled.[^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader:** plain language is a disciplined route to clarity, defined by whether the intended audience can understand and use the text.[^s1]
- **Addresses the curse of knowledge and jargon:** the federal guidance traces jargon to writers not realising that familiar terms are opaque to their readers, and prescribes everyday words and in-place definitions.[^s2]
- **Applied in plain language summaries,** the lay summaries of research that Stoll and colleagues reviewed.[^s6]
- **Supported by** Kimble's *Writing for Dollars, Writing to Please*, which collects the empirical case for plain language in business, government and law.[^s5]
- **Requires audience analysis:** the guidance calls writing for your audience the first rule of plain language.[^s1]
- **Related:** active voice and answer-first structure (bottom line up front) are core plain-language habits; readability formulas and paraphrase testing are ways to check a draft, and testing on readers is what the principle ultimately relies on.[^s1][^s5]
