# Business report

A business report is a document that records and conveys information to a specific audience for a specific purpose; it is usually longer than a page and shorter than a book, and its purpose decides its type, length, visuals and style.[^s1] Textbooks divide reports into two families. **Informational** reports present facts about events, activities or conditions without judgement, such as meeting minutes, expense reports and status updates. **Analytical** reports add analysis to solve a problem or make a recommendation, such as feasibility studies, justification reports and proposals.[^s1] Reports travel as memos inside the organisation, letters outside it, emails, or web pages, but the delivery format matters less than matching the structure to the report's type.[^s1]

## Origin

The modern business report grew out of technical and administrative reporting, and its standard formal anatomy is now taught in business-communication textbooks: title page, contents, abstract or summary, introduction, body (background, method, results, analysis and recommendations), conclusion, references and appendices.[^s1] Government style guides codify a similar four-part shape (landing page, preliminary content, main body, endmatter) and, for long reports, ask for a summary of findings, conclusions and recommendations.[^s2] In 2001 the Canadian Health Services Research Foundation popularised the "1:3:25" format for reports aimed at decision makers: one page of main messages, a three-page executive summary, then no more than 25 pages of plain-language report.[^s3] A 2020 comparison of rapid-review reports found that about half of those produced outside journals used this kind of graded-entry front end, while almost all journal versions kept the academic IMRaD layout.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Reports are written for readers who will not read them in full. Engineering-writing teaching at Carnegie Mellon warns that readers higher in an organisation have less technical background and less time, so writers should expect selective reading and put an overview at the start of the report and of each section.[^s6] The decision-maker version of this point is blunter: if the writer does not state what the findings mean for the reader, someone else will interpret them.[^s3] A report that hides its conclusion in chapter four may be accurate and still fail at its job.

## What works here

- **Decide the type and the reaction before drafting.** For an analytical report, put the recommendation near the start when the reader is likely to be neutral or positive; when they are likely to resist, lead with the background and evidence and reach the recommendation at the end.[^s1]
- **Layer the content (graded entry).** Open with a short list of main messages, then a summary of a few pages, then the full report; the main messages should say what the findings mean for the reader's decisions, not just repeat the results.[^s3]
- **Name the summary for its job.** Australian government guidance prefers headings such as "Summary" or "Recommendations" over the generic "Executive summary", and wants a landing page that tells readers whether the report is worth opening.[^s2]
- **Make it navigable.** Give chapters short titles that say what they cover, rarely use more than three heading levels, and add a contents list.[^s2] Start each section with a one-paragraph overview so a skimming reader still gets the point.[^s6]
- **Move bulk to the back.** Put long tables, methods detail and non-essential material in appendices so they do not slow the main text.[^s2] Decision-maker formats keep methods to a line or two in the summary.[^s3]

**Example.** An operations analyst's 30-page report on warehouse delays opens with three pages of project history and reaches the recommendation, a second shift for six months, on page 22. Rewritten, page one lists three main messages (delays come from the evening peak, a second shift costs less than overtime, decide by month-end to cover the holiday season), a two-page summary follows, and the analysis and data sit behind them for anyone who wants to check.[^s3][^s1]

## Pitfalls

- **Opening with history instead of the point.** Readers who skim need an overview at the start of the report and of each section, so project background belongs after the findings, not before them.[^s6]
- **Assuming a full read.** Treating the audience as people with time to read every page is a common misconception in report writing.[^s6]
- **Main messages that only summarise.** Restating findings without implications, or ending with "more research is needed" when you could state the open questions, leaves readers without guidance.[^s3]
- **Forcing recommendations the evidence will not carry.** Main messages should not overstate certainty.[^s3]
- **Neglecting accessibility.** Government reports are expected to be accessible to everyone, including people with disability, which affects format choices such as HTML over PDF.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

Most report-format advice comes from textbooks, style guides and practitioners rather than controlled tests.[^s1][^s2] The closest research is on summaries of research for policymakers. A 2016 systematic review of six randomised trials found that evidence summaries are probably easier to understand than full reviews, and in one trial more readers preferred a graded-entry report to the full review, but the effect of summaries on actual use of evidence in decisions was unclear.[^s5] The 2020 rapid-review comparison found that key messages and executive summaries were still uncommon even in reports written for decision makers, and that readability was similar across formats.[^s4] These studies come from health policy, so their transfer to general business reports is plausible but not directly tested.[^s5]

## Connections

A business report **complements** plain language: decision-maker formats ask for writing a bright reader without specialist training can follow.[^s3] It **contrasts with** the academic IMRaD structure, which puts methods before results and rarely leads with key messages.[^s4] Its layered, summary-first design is **supported by** Petkovic and colleagues' 2016 review showing that summaries are easier to understand than full reports.[^s5] The executive summary is one of its parts and project status reports are a recurring subtype; the pyramid principle, informative headings and bottom line up front are techniques for structuring it, and a wall of text is what an unlayered report turns into.[^s1][^s2][^s6]
