steps:
  - Before drafting, write the single sentence you want the reader to accept or act on - your answer, not your topic.
  - Write the question that sentence answers, and the situation and complication that make the reader ask it.
  - List every supporting point, then sort them into two to four groups that each answer "why?" or "how?" about the top sentence.
  - Check each group - its points should be the same kind of idea, not overlap, and together cover the claim - and put them in a deliberate order (time, structure or importance).
  - Turn each group into a summary sentence and a heading, place the evidence under it, and reread the top sentence and headings alone to see whether they tell the whole story.
example:
  situation: An operations analyst writes up a three-week investigation into rising machine-tooling costs for the plant manager.
  before: "Report on tooling costs. Week 1: we collected cost data from all six machines. Week 2: we ran tests on feed rates and coolant. Week 3: we analysed the results and found several interesting patterns, which are described below, followed by some options we considered..."
  after: "Recommendation: standardise coolant pressure on all six machines; we expect tooling costs to return to last year's level within a quarter. Situation: the machines ran within budget until spring. Complication: tooling costs have since risen sharply. Question: what should we change? Three findings support the answer - 1) the cost rise tracks the machines with low coolant pressure, 2) a two-week trial at higher pressure cut tool wear, 3) the change needs no new equipment. Data for each is in the sections below."
  why: The rewrite moves from the order the analyst worked in to the order the reader thinks in - answer, then grouped reasons, then evidence - so the manager can decide from the first paragraph and check any reason in its own section.
selfCheck:
  - Could my reader state my conclusion after reading only the first paragraph and the headings?
  - Does every point under a heading actually support that heading, and are my groups free of overlaps and gaps?
  - Am I presenting the order in which I did the work, or the order in which my reader needs the answer?

# Pyramid principle

The pyramid principle is a way of structuring a document or presentation so that it reads top-down: a single governing recommendation sits at the top, two to four grouped arguments support it, and detailed evidence and data form the base.[^s2] It is usually paired with a short situation-complication-question-answer (SCQA) introduction that sets up the problem before the answer is given.[^s2][^s3] Where bottom line up front tells you what to say first, the pyramid tells you how to organise everything that follows.[^s2]

## Origin

Barbara Minto developed the approach at McKinsey & Company, where she worked from 1963 to 1973 in Cleveland and London as the firm's first female MBA professional.[^s1] Editing associates' reports, she noticed she kept rearranging their ideas into the same pyramid shape, and concluded that the shape depended on three rules: a summary point must follow from the points beneath it, ideas in a group must be the same kind of idea, and they must sit in a logical order.[^s1] She decided the underlying problem was muddled thinking rather than poor language, taught the method in courses across the firm, and published it as *The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking* in 1985, later revising it herself as *The Minto Pyramid Principle*.[^s1] She also coined the abbreviation MECE - mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive - for the test that a group of ideas should neither overlap nor leave gaps.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Busy readers - managers, clients, assessors - want the answer quickly, and a conclusion-first structure lets them get it before deciding how much detail to read.[^s2] Problem solvers often lose their audience because they report findings in the order they did the work (define, measure, analyse, improve) instead of the order the audience needs; the pyramid separates the structure of communication from the structure of the investigation.[^s3] Minto herself frames the pyramid as a thinking tool as much as a writing format: building it exposes whether your points actually add up to your conclusion.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Start with the reader's question, not your material. The SCQA opener states a situation the reader already accepts, the complication that disturbs it, the question that complication raises, and your answer.[^s3] Then build downwards: the answer is supported by a few key points, and each key point by its data, so every level summarises the level below.[^s1][^s3] Keep groups to two to four items, give each a clear heading, and make each section understandable on its own, because readers jump straight to the part they care about.[^s2] Test every group against MECE - no overlaps, nothing important missing.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Narrating the process.** Reporting week by week, or step by step through a problem-solving method, buries the answer under the history.[^s3]
- **Key points that are hard to find.** Monash's guide asks for clear headings and key points a reader can spot at a glance; a bare label such as "Costs" names a subject without stating what you found about it.[^s2]
- **Mixed or overlapping groups.** Putting causes, actions and risks in one list breaks Minto's rule that a group holds one kind of idea.[^s1]
- **Using it where an essay is expected.** Monash contrasts Minto-style business papers with academic essays, which build an argument through scholarly evidence in introduction-body-conclusion form; match the format to the reader.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The pyramid principle is practitioner guidance; we found no controlled study comparing pyramid-structured business documents with the same content in another order, so its status here is `practitioner`.[^s1][^s2] Reading research on text signals is consistent with part of its logic. A 1989 review by Robert Lorch found that nearly all signals - titles, headings, overviews, summaries - improve memory for the information they cue, though usually not for unsignalled material.[^s4] In three 1996 experiments, Lorch and Lorch found that headings, overviews and summaries made no difference for a simple text but changed what readers recalled and how they organised it when the text structure was complex.[^s5] Those studies used expository passages and student readers, not workplace decisions, so they support the value of making structure visible rather than proving the pyramid itself.[^s4][^s5] The approach also assumes a reader who wants the answer first; for readers expecting a gradual build-up, such as academic assessors, it can be the wrong genre.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Broader:** the pyramid is a message-structure framework that extends conclusion-first writing to whole documents.[^s2]
- **Introduced by Barbara Minto** at McKinsey and set out in her book *The Pyramid Principle*.[^s1]
- **Complements bottom line up front** - both open with the answer, and the pyramid adds the grouped reasons and evidence beneath it; BLUF is a sensible prerequisite.[^s2]
- **Addresses burying the lede** by putting the recommendation and key points where a reader cannot miss them.[^s2]
- **Structures business reports** and similar papers such as policy briefs and investment recommendations.[^s2]
- **Applied in executive briefings**, where problem-solving results are presented to managers who need the answer before the analysis.[^s3]
- **Supported (indirectly) by Lorch and Lorch's signalling experiments**, which show that visible organisational signals shape recall of complex texts.[^s5][^s4]
- **Related:** the SCQA framework is its standard introduction and MECE its grouping test;[^s3][^s1] executive summaries, audience analysis and assertion-evidence slides apply the same answer-first, reader-centred logic.[^s2]
