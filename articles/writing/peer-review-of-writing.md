steps:
  - Before sending a draft, tell the reviewer who the real reader is, what that reader must do after reading, and what kind of comments you want now (structure, argument or polish).
  - As reviewer, read the whole draft once for meaning without marking grammar or word choice.
  - Write down in one sentence what you think the main point and the request are, and compare that with what the writer intended.
  - Comment first on purpose, organisation, missing evidence and anything the intended reader would not understand; leave wording and typos for the final pass.
  - Make every comment specific and explained - anchor it to a passage, say how it affected you as a reader, and say why it matters.
  - Name at least one thing that works so the writer knows what to keep.
  - As writer, sort the comments, decide which to act on, revise, and if possible swap roles on the next document.
example:
  situation: An analyst asks a teammate to review a two-page proposal asking a department head to fund a reporting tool.
  before: "Looks good! Change 'utilize' to 'use' in para 3, add a comma after 'However', and I'd make the table font bigger."

# Peer review of writing

Peer review of writing is the practice of swapping drafts with a colleague who reads each one as its intended reader would and comments before it is sent, starting with purpose and structure and leaving wording for later. It serves two people at once: the writer gets a reader's view while there is still time to revise, and the reviewer practises spotting in someone else's draft the problems that are hard to see in their own.[^s2][^s3] Public-sector plain-language guidance builds it into the workflow, asking writers of significant documents to have colleagues read and comment, and to check whether those readers grasp the main point and respond as intended.[^s4]

## Origin

Peer response grew out of writing instruction, where university writing centres teach reviewers to read for meaning first and to treat grammar as a late concern.[^s2] Researchers then asked whether the practice actually improves writing. Kwangsu Cho and Christian Schunn, working at the University of Pittsburgh's Learning Research and Development Center, framed reviewing as a way of learning to write: reviewers must explain to a peer what is unclear and why, and in doing so build their own sense of what readers need.[^s3] Government plain-language programmes adopted the same idea for workplace documents, pairing a colleague read-through with testing on real readers.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Writers know what they meant, so they read their own drafts with the gaps already filled in; a colleague without that background notices where the point is buried or a step is missing. Students interviewed in Cho and Schunn's studies said that reviewing taught them what to avoid and pushed them to take a more audience-oriented view of their own writing.[^s3] The benefit flows in both directions. In the studies Cho and Schunn summarise, students who wrote comments on sample or peer papers went on to write stronger first drafts than those who only read the papers, and students who merely rated papers without commenting did not show the same gain - explaining the problem seems to be what does the teaching.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Set the review up before sending the draft: name the real reader, the action you need from them, and the kind of feedback that would help at this stage, since comments on wording are wasted on a draft whose structure will change.[^s2] As reviewer, read once for meaning without marking errors, then check whether the purpose is clear, the main points are identifiable and supported, and the reader would be left with unanswered questions.[^s2] Explain the reasoning behind each comment rather than just ordering a change; when something is unclear, say what you think the writer means and ask whether that is the intent; and name what works so the writer knows what to keep.[^s2] Anchor comments to specific passages and add a short summary note at the top that previews the main issues.[^s2] For an important document, follow the colleague review with a test on a few target readers and a final proofread for facts, consistency and typos.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is jumping straight to line edits: correcting commas while the request still sits on page two. Writing-centre guidance treats surface comments as appropriate only once the draft is otherwise polished.[^s2] Other pitfalls are vague verdicts ("flows well", "a bit confusing") that give the writer nothing to act on, a curt tone that makes the writer less willing to use the feedback, and reviewing without knowing who the real reader is.[^s2] Evaluating without explaining also wastes the reviewer's own learning, since rating alone did not improve reviewers' later drafts in the studies Cho and Schunn report.[^s3] Finally, a colleague is not the audience: for high-stakes documents, plain-language guidance recommends checking with real target readers as well.[^s4]

## Evidence

A 2019 meta-analysis by Huisman and colleagues pooled 24 studies of formative peer feedback on university students' academic writing. Peer feedback produced larger writing gains than no feedback (g = 0.91, but from only two studies) and than self-assessment (g = 0.33), and gains similar to teacher feedback; comments combined with grades worked better than either alone.[^s1] The authors flag serious limits: few well-controlled studies, very high heterogeneity, and pretest-posttest designs that can inflate effects.[^s1] A broader 2020 meta-analysis by Double, McGrane and Hopfenbeck of 54 control-group studies across subjects found a small-to-medium benefit of peer assessment on performance (g = 0.31), a similar edge over teacher assessment, and a non-significant difference from self-assessment, and judged the effect robust across contexts.[^s5] Almost all of this evidence comes from classrooms, not offices, so the size of the benefit for business writing is untested; the evidence status is therefore mixed.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

- **Develops business writing:** reviewers who explain problems in others' drafts write better drafts of their own, and writers revise with a reader's view in hand.[^s3]
- **Addresses the curse of knowledge:** a reader without the writer's background exposes the gaps, and reviewers report becoming more audience-oriented.[^s3]
- **Applied in business reports and other formal documents,** where government guidance asks for colleague review and reader testing before release.[^s4]
- **Supported by** the meta-analyses of Huisman et al. (2019)[^s1] and Double, McGrane and Hopfenbeck (2020).[^s5]
- **Related:** it sits under editing for clarity and feedback, depends on audience analysis to know whose eyes the reviewer is borrowing, and pairs with receiving feedback on the writer's side and usability testing when real readers can be reached.[^s2][^s4]
