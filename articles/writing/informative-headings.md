# Informative headings

Informative headings are headings that tell the reader what a section says, not just what it is about. "Unused licences cost us $90K a year" is informative; "Analysis" is a label. Guidance from the 1981 *Guidelines for Document Designers* onwards asks writers to use enough words that a heading describes the content beneath it, because single abstract words such as "Purpose" or "Provisions" can mislead or tell the reader nothing.[^s1] Policy-writing advice goes a step further: a heading should state the section's message, so the headings on their own tell the story of the paper.[^s6]

## Origin

The idea comes from the document-design and plain-language movements. In 1981 a team at the American Institutes for Research, including Ginny Redish, published *Guidelines for Document Designers* under a U.S. National Institute of Education contract; guideline A-3, "Use informative headings", drew on reading research showing that headings help readers understand and find information, and that vague headings can mislead them.[^s1] U.S. federal plain-language guidance later turned this into a ranking of heading types: questions where you know what readers will ask, then specific statements, with topic labels last because words such as "Scope" or "Application" are often too vague to help.[^s2] Government style manuals now treat informative headings as standard practice, though they differ on questions: the Australian Government Style Manual prefers statements, because a question puts the key idea at the end and slows scanning.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Busy readers scan before they read. Headings are the main tool they use to judge whether a section is relevant, to jump to what they need, and to refresh their memory before a meeting.[^s3][^s6] Label headings make every reader open the section to find out what it says; informative headings let a director or client pick up the argument from the headings alone and dive in only where they disagree.[^s6] Plain-language guidance also treats useful headings as one of the main ways to break up dense pages so that readers can find specific information.[^s2]

## How to apply it

- **Draft headings first, from the reader's questions.** Writing headings around your audience's concerns before the body reveals the main groups of information and keeps the document organised.[^s2]
- **State the point, ideally with a verb.** Prefer "Conclusion: the pilot failed to cut wait times" to "Conclusion", and add a key figure where it strengthens the message.[^s6]
- **Front-load keywords.** Put the most informative words first, because readers, including screen-reader users, often judge a heading by its first two or three words.[^s3]
- **Keep them short and consistent.** Aim for about one line, use a consistent style within each level, and stop at three levels so readers don't lose their place.[^s2][^s3][^s6]
- **Test with a table of contents.** Generate one and check that the headings alone show the key messages and logical flow, and that they agree with the executive summary.[^s6]

**Channel and audience.** In a long email, two or three bold statement headings do the same job as section headings in a report. Question headings suit public-facing pages and FAQs where you know what people will ask; statement headings suit decision papers and executive summaries, where the reader wants your conclusion.[^s2][^s3][^s6]

## Common mistakes

- **Generic labels** such as "Background", "Discussion" or "More information", which could sit on top of any document.[^s3][^s6]
- **Clever or obscure wording** that makes the reader decode the heading before they can use it.[^s6]
- **Headings that over-promise.** A heading can't rescue a disorganised document, and readers lose trust when the text beneath it doesn't deliver what it states.[^s1]
- **Headings that drift from the summary.** If the executive summary says one thing and the headings imply another, the reader gets two stories.[^s6]
- **Too many levels or overlong headings,** which bury the content they are meant to reveal.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

Research on headings supports them, with limits. In two experiments, Lorch and Lorch (1996) found that headings improved recall of unfamiliar topics but not familiar ones, and made briefly discussed topics more likely to appear in readers' summaries.[^s4] A 2012 review by Lemarié, Lorch and Péry-Woodley concluded that texts with headings generally produce better recall and summaries, that readers search much faster when headings identify topics, and that question-style labels also sped search.[^s5] The older studies behind the 1981 guidelines found that headings aided understanding of prose that followed, but one study of product warranties found no comprehension gain, only a preference for headed versions.[^s1] Most experiments compare headings with no headings, using students and short texts; direct tests of informative versus label headings in workplace documents are scarce, so the specific advice to write statement headings rests mainly on practitioner and government guidance.[^s1][^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Informative headings are **applied in** the executive summary: policy-writing guidance asks that headings and summary carry the same key messages.[^s6] They **structure** the business report, turning its table of contents into a readable outline of the argument.[^s6] They **address** the wall of text, giving dense pages entry points readers can scan.[^s2] They **complement** plain language, which treats useful headings as a core organising tool.[^s2] They are **supported by** Lorch and Lorch's 1996 experiments on recall and summarisation,[^s4] and **explained by** text-signalling theory, which holds that headings draw attention to topics and build a mental map of the text's structure that guides later retrieval.[^s5] They pair naturally with bottom line up front and the pyramid principle, whose summary statements make good headings, and with signposting, their spoken counterpart.
