steps:
  - Finish the draft first, then pick one long or important sentence to revise at a time.
  - Circle the prepositions (of, in, about, for, by, on) and box every form of "to be" (is, are, was, be, been).
  - Ask where the action is; look for it hidden in a noun such as decision, recommendation or review.
  - Turn that action into a simple verb and make the person or team doing it the subject.
  - Delete slow wind-ups ("It is important to note that", "The purpose of this email is to") so the sentence starts with its subject.
  - Remove redundancies and leftover words, then count words removed divided by the original count (the lard factor).
  - Read the revised passage aloud and restore any context, courtesy or emphasis the reader still needs.
example:
  situation: A project lead's status email asks the steering committee to choose a vendor.
  before: "It is the recommendation of the project team that a decision on the selection of a vendor be made by the steering committee by the end of the month."
  after: "The project team recommends that the steering committee choose a vendor by month-end."
  why: The actions buried in "recommendation", "decision" and "selection" become two verbs with named actors, the wind-up disappears, and 29 words drop to 13 (a lard factor of about 55%) without losing the request or deadline.
selfCheck:
  - In each key sentence, can I point to who does what, as a subject followed by a verb?
  - Which nouns ending in -tion, -ment or -ance am I using where a verb would carry the action?
  - Does my first sentence start with its subject, or with a wind-up I could delete?
  - After cutting, does the message still carry the context and tone this particular reader needs?

# Paramedic method for revising prose

The Paramedic Method is a short, mechanical editing routine for rescuing sentences that are long, abstract and passive. You mark prepositions and forms of "is", find the action the sentence is really about, put that action into a simple verb with the doer as its subject, and then strip out slow openings and redundancies.[^s1] Its purpose is to help professional writers cut unneeded words, write in the active voice and make sentences clearer and more reader-centred.[^s1] It works on one sentence at a time and is meant for revision, not for drafting.[^s3]

## Origin

The method comes from Richard A. Lanham, a professor of English at UCLA (now emeritus), in his book *Revising Prose*, whose fifth edition Pearson published in 2006.[^s2][^s1] Lanham's diagnosis is that much bad writing imitates what he calls the "Official Style": a bureaucratic register heavy with passive verbs, strings of prepositional phrases and jargon. The book offers a few simple rules to translate it into plain English, and the publisher claims they can remove up to two-thirds of excess wordiness.[^s2] Purdue's widely used version, adapted by Richard Johnson-Sheehan, condenses the method to seven steps.[^s1] Writing-centre handouts add Lanham's "lard factor", the share of the original words you managed to cut, as a quick score for each revision.[^s3]

## Why it matters

General advice such as "be concise" tells writers what to aim for but not what to change. The Paramedic Method turns that goal into checks you can see on the page: clusters of "of", "by" and "in", and an "is" sitting where an action verb should be, usually mean the real action has been turned into a noun.[^s1] Purdue's demonstration sentence ("In this paragraph is a demonstration of the use of good style...") shows the pattern: the action hides in "demonstration", and the fix ("This paragraph demonstrates good style in reports") halves the length.[^s1] Its practice sentences shrink from 25 to 9 words, 23 to 15 and 35 to 24.[^s1] At work this matters most in emails, status reports and proposals, where readers have to find out who is doing what before they can act.

## How to apply it

Draft first; then run the steps above on your opening sentence, your request and your longest sentences, rather than on every line.[^s3] Mark the text physically or with your editor's highlight tool: prepositions in one colour, forms of "to be" in another, nominalisations (nouns such as *decision*, *review*, *implementation*) boxed.[^s3][^s1] For each sentence, ask who is acting on whom, and rebuild it around that actor and a single verb.[^s1] Then delete wind-ups like "The point I want to make is that" and phrases that repeat what the sentence already says.[^s1] Lanham's fuller version, as summarised in one handout, adds checks on sentence length and rhythm: mark where each sentence ends to spot monotony, and read the passage aloud.[^s3] Working out the lard factor (words cut divided by original words) helps you track progress across a document.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Running it while drafting.** The method is a revision pass; applying it mid-draft slows writing and breaks your train of thought.[^s3]
- **Removing every passive.** Some genres rely on the passive on purpose. Purdue's "Reverse Paramedic Method" teaches scientific writers to recast sentences around the work rather than the researchers, while still cutting prepositions, wind-ups and redundancy.[^s5] Keep a passive when the actor is unknown or irrelevant.
- **Cutting meaning along with words.** Purdue's reverse-method page notes that some opening phrases are needed for the meaning and others are only padding, so you have to judge each one in context.[^s5]
- **Chasing a lard-factor score.** A high percentage is not the goal if the shorter sentence drops the reason, the deadline or the courtesy a client or senior reader expects.

## Evidence and limits

The method itself has not been tested as a package; its support is practitioner consensus and decades of classroom use, hence the `practitioner` status.[^s1][^s2] There is some evidence on one of its components, replacing nominalisations with verbs. In a 1998 study in the *Journal of Technical Writing and Communication*, Spyridakis and Isakson tested the common editorial advice to "denominalise" technical prose. They found that the verb-based version helped native English speakers focus on the more important information, while the nominalised version could work quite well for non-native readers.[^s4] So the routine is best treated as a strong default for native-speaker audiences rather than a universal rule: for global teams, check whether a revised sentence still reads easily to people working in a second language. The evidence also concerns recall of technical passages, not workplace outcomes such as response rates.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Develops concise writing and addresses wordiness:** Purdue presents the method as a lesson in writing concisely and cutting unnecessary words.[^s1]
- **Addresses the Official Style:** Lanham built the method to translate bureaucratic, passive, preposition-heavy prose into plain English.[^s2]
- **Introduced by Richard Lanham in *Revising Prose*.**[^s2]
- **Complements active voice:** making the doer the subject is one of the method's core steps.[^s1]
- **Supported by Spyridakis and Isakson (1998)** for its denominalisation step, with the caveat about non-native readers.[^s4]
- **Related:** it is a practical route to plain language and pairs with readability formulas for checking the result; the Reverse Paramedic Method adapts it for passive-voice scientific writing.[^s5]
