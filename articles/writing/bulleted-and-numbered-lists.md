steps:
  - Spot any sentence or paragraph that strings together three or more requirements, options, conditions or steps.
  - Write a lead-in sentence that says what the list is and who must do what with it.
  - Put each item on its own line, starting every item with the same grammatical form (all verbs, or all nouns) so each one completes the lead-in.
  - Number the items if the order matters or readers will refer back to them; otherwise use plain round or square bullets.
  - Keep the list short, roughly six to eight items at most, and split or group longer lists under subheadings.
  - Keep reasons, caveats and trade-offs in sentences around the list rather than shrinking them into fragments inside it.
example:
  situation: An operations lead emails twelve site managers about what they must send before a system migration.
  before: "Ahead of the migration on the 14th, we will need you to confirm your local admin contacts, and also it would help to have the inventory spreadsheet updated, plus please make sure any scheduled jobs are paused and the backup report is sent to IT by the 12th."
  after: "By Thursday the 12th, please send IT these four items so your site can migrate on the 14th: 1. Your local admin contacts. 2. The updated inventory spreadsheet. 3. Confirmation that scheduled jobs are paused. 4. Last week's backup report."
  why: The lead-in states the deadline and purpose, the numbers let managers reply "done 1-3, 4 by Friday", and no request is lost in the middle of a long sentence.

# Bulleted and numbered lists

A vertical list sets a series of items, such as requirements, conditions, exceptions or the steps of a process, on separate lines under a sentence that introduces them.[^s1] Writing guides distinguish two kinds: numbered lists for steps that must happen in order or items readers will need to cite by number, and bulleted lists for items of roughly equal weight with no required sequence.[^s1][^s2] Used well, a list turns a sentence the reader would have to untangle into a set of separate, checkable items; used badly, it chops an argument into fragments.

## Origin

Lists are an old typographic device, but their place in workplace writing owes much to the plain-language movement. The U.S. federal plain-language guidelines, maintained by the Plain Language Action and Information Network and drawing on Bryan Garner's legal-writing guidance and the SEC's 1998 *Plain English Handbook*, tell writers to use vertical lists to set off requirements and to show the order of steps.[^s1] Technical-communication textbooks such as David McMurray's *Online Technical Writing* codified the conventions now taught in business-writing courses: a lead-in, parallel items, and a choice between bullets and numbers.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Busy readers scan before they read. The federal guidance argues that lists show what matters most, make sequence clear, make steps easy to find and add white space that eases reading.[^s1] Experiments back part of this. In a reading study published in *Visible Language*, people recalled list content better when items were set on separate lines than when the same items ran on continuously, and readers of separated items could jump back to a specific point rather than rereading from the top.[^s4] In a 1998 study of medication instructions, list formats improved comprehension and recall compared with paragraphs and narrowed the gaps between older and younger readers and between readers with more and less working-memory capacity; the authors describe lists as environmental support for comprehension.[^s5] For workplace messages with several asks, that means fewer missed items and fewer follow-up questions.

## How to apply it

Start from a sentence that is doing too much. If it strings together three or more parallel things, recast it as a lead-in plus a list, as the federal guidelines do with a dense permit requirement.[^s1] Write the lead-in so it says who must do what with the items; the same guidance shows a supplies list that leaves unclear who must bring the supplies until a lead-in is added.[^s1] Make every item grammatically parallel and able to complete the lead-in, use sentence-style capitalisation, and punctuate items only when they are full sentences.[^s2] Choose numbers for sequence or reference and plain round or square bullets otherwise, because decorative symbols distract and may not display on every system.[^s1][^s2] Consolidate or regroup any list that runs past about six to eight items.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

- **Listing everything.** Lists lose their pull when overused; the federal guidance reserves them for important material, not trivia.[^s1][^s2]
- **No lead-in.** A bare list leaves readers guessing how the items relate, and a heading is not a substitute for an introductory sentence.[^s1][^s2]
- **Mixed items.** Items that switch between verbs, nouns and full sentences break parallelism, and in experiments the recall benefit of bullets shrank when items were heterogeneous.[^s2][^s3]
- **Bullets that hide reasoning.** After the 2003 Columbia shuttle loss, the accident board criticised NASA's habit of briefing engineering analysis on PowerPoint slides instead of technical papers; Edward Tufte's analysis of one slide argues that its nested bullets and sizes foregrounded reassurance and lost the caveats that signalled danger.[^s6]

## Evidence

The evidence supports lists for the items inside them, with costs elsewhere. Frank Jansen's five experiments with health flyers, direct-mail letters and recipes, published in 2014, found that bulleted lists improved recall of the listed items and raised readers' evaluations of the text compared with the same items enumerated inside a paragraph, but they reduced recall of the surrounding text.[^s3] Seki's and the medication studies show layout effects on recall, comprehension and reading paths, but they used small laboratory samples and specific text types, so effect sizes for everyday email are not established.[^s4][^s5] The strongest critique is about reasoning rather than recall: a bullet hierarchy can make claims look equal and independent when they depend on one another, which is why the Columbia board and Tufte favoured written technical analysis for high-stakes decisions.[^s6] Treat lists as a tool for discrete items and steps, and keep arguments in prose.

## Connections

Lists are one of the standard tools of plain language, and the federal guidelines present them as a way to break up the dense blocks of text that make up a wall of text.[^s1] Jansen's experiments are the clearest demonstration of what bullets do and do not improve.[^s3] Seki's layout study supports the claim that separated items help readers revisit specific points.[^s4] The working-memory account in the medication-instructions study links lists to cognitive load theory.[^s5] The Columbia accident board's criticism of bullet-point briefings, and Tufte's reading of it, is the main challenge to treating bullets as a substitute for reasoning, and connects this technique to "death by PowerPoint" and to the assertion-evidence slides alternative.[^s6] Lists pair naturally with bottom line up front and concise writing: state the point first, then list the items the reader must act on.[^s1][^s2]
