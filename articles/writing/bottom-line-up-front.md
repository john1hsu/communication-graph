steps:
  - Before drafting, write one sentence that states your conclusion, recommendation or request, including any decision and deadline you need.
  - Make that sentence (after a short purpose statement if the format calls for one) the opening of the message, and echo it in the subject line or title.
  - Add only the background the reader needs to act on it, most important first, and move history and method to the end or an attachment.
  - Put any secondary requests or audiences in clearly labelled sections below the main point.
  - Reread only the first two lines and ask whether a busy reader could act on them alone; if not, rewrite them.
  - Decide deliberately whether this message is an exception - serious bad news or a reader who expects context first - and if so use an indirect structure on purpose rather than by habit.
example:
  situation: A project analyst emails her director about a vendor problem that needs a decision.
  before: "Hi Sam, I wanted to give you some background on the reporting vendor. Over the last two months we have seen several late deliveries, and after a call with their account team on Tuesday it looks like their new platform will not support our quarterly export. I have looked at a few options..."
  after: "Subject: Decision needed by Thursday - switch reporting vendor for Q1. Sam, I recommend we move the Q1 reports to our backup vendor; it adds about two weeks of setup but keeps the March board deadline. Please approve or decline by Thursday. Background: our current vendor's new platform cannot produce the quarterly export (confirmed on Tuesday's call), and deliveries have been late twice since September."

# Bottom line up front

Bottom line up front (BLUF) means opening a message with its conclusion, recommendation or request and putting the supporting detail after it. The U.S. Army's correspondence regulation names it, alongside active voice, as one of the two essentials of Army writing style, which aims for writing a reader understands in a single rapid reading.[^s1] U.S. federal plain-language guidance gives the same instruction to civilian writers: state your purpose and the bottom line at the start, and save background for later.[^s2]

## Origin

The term comes from U.S. military staff writing. Army Regulation 25-50, *Preparing and Managing Correspondence*, describes effective Army writing as clear, concise, well organised and understood in one reading, and makes putting the main point at the beginning (bottom line up front) a core requirement.[^s1] For memorandums it prescribes the order: open with a short, clear purpose sentence, then give the recommendation, conclusion or most important information.[^s1] The idea spread into federal plain-language guidance, where agencies are told to organise documents around the reader's questions and lead with what matters most,[^s2][^s3] and into university writing teaching, where MIT's Communication Lab recommends BLUF for professional email and notes the term's military roots.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Readers at work are busy and impatient with hunting through text. The National Archives' plain-writing guidance warns that readers have little patience for searching, and that putting essentials near the top reduces frustration and produces better-prepared responses.[^s3] A message that opens with history forces the reader to guess what is wanted; MIT's guidance suggests summarising the key points in the first few sentences so that a reader can decide whether to read on at all.[^s5] BLUF also protects the writer: when the request is first, it is far less likely to be skimmed past or missed, which is one of the reasons textbooks give for stating news directly when a reader might otherwise overlook it.[^s6]

## How to apply it

1. **Find your bottom line before drafting.** Write the single sentence you need remembered or acted on, with the decision and deadline.[^s1][^s2]
2. **Lead with it.** In a memo, a short purpose sentence then the recommendation or conclusion;[^s1] in an email, the key point straight after the greeting, without filler such as apologies for interrupting.[^s5]
3. **Mirror it in the subject line.** A short subject line that reflects the main point helps the reader prioritise and find the message later.[^s5]
4. **Order the rest by importance to the reader,** with general information before exceptions and background or method at the end.[^s2][^s3]
5. **Test the opening.** Ask whether a reader who stopped after two lines could act correctly; if not, the bottom line is still buried.[^s3][^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Leading with the story.** Writers often narrate events in the order they happened; plain-language guidance says to arrange content around the reader's questions instead.[^s3]
- **A vague opener.** "Quick question about the project" names a topic, not a point; MIT's examples contrast this with openings that state the problem and the specific request.[^s5]
- **Using BLUF where an indirect approach fits better.** Business-communication textbooks reserve the direct order for routine or minor bad news, readers who want directness, or news a reader might miss, and suggest explaining first when bluntness could cause anger or misunderstanding.[^s6] Even then the news must be stated clearly, not hidden.[^s6]
- **Burying the bottom line under a long purpose statement.** The Army's model keeps the purpose to one short sentence before the main point.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

BLUF is expert and institutional guidance rather than a tested intervention; we found no controlled study comparing BLUF messages with the same content in another order, so its status here is `practitioner`.[^s1][^s2] Reading research is consistent with its logic. In a 1979 series of five experiments for the Office of Naval Research, David Kieras found that readers usually built a passage's main idea from its opening sentences, and that a theme sentence placed first was matched more closely in readers' summaries than the same sentence embedded mid-passage.[^s4] Kieras concluded that initial position cues readers to what is thematic but can influence rather than fully determine it, and his studies measured perceived theme in short technical passages, not workplace decisions.[^s4] The main limit is fit: the direct order can feel abrupt for serious bad news or for readers who expect context before conclusions, so audience analysis should decide when to use it.[^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader:** BLUF is a message-structure technique and one of the organising moves of plain language, which tells writers to state the purpose and bottom line first.[^s2]
- **Addresses burying the lede:** putting the essentials first is the direct fix for messages whose key point is lost in the middle.[^s3]
- **Applied in email** as an opening and subject-line discipline,[^s5] and **in memorandums**, where the Army prescribes a purpose sentence followed by the recommendation or conclusion.[^s1]
- **Complements active voice:** AR 25-50 pairs the two as the essential requirements of Army writing style.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with the indirect approach,** which places a buffer and explanation before negative news.[^s6]
- **Supported by Kieras's initial-mention experiments,** which show readers treat early sentences as the main idea.[^s4]
- **Related:** audience analysis decides what the bottom line is for a given reader; the pyramid principle and executive summaries apply the same conclusion-first logic to longer documents; high- and low-context cultures help explain why some readers expect context before conclusions.[^s2][^s6]
