# Business writing

Business writing is the craft of the documents that keep an organisation running: emails, memos, letters, reports, proposals, white papers and press releases.[^s1] Purdue's Online Writing Lab frames it as rhetorical work: you analyse the readers' needs, values and attitudes, then fit the content, tone and design to them and to the situation.[^s1] Unlike most essays, a work document has a reader who must decide, approve, reply or act, so its quality shows in whether that happens quickly and correctly.[^s3]

## Origin

Business writing became a university subject in the early twentieth century. In 1935–36, a committee of business-writing teachers led by C. R. Anderson of the University of Illinois, with colleagues from NYU, Columbia, Wisconsin and Northwestern, set up a professional association for college teachers of the subject, because existing bodies such as the Modern Language Association focused on literature.[^s6] It took the name American Business Writing Association in 1937, widened to "communication" in 1967 to cover speaking too, and has been the Association for Business Communication since 1985.[^s6] Government plain-language programmes later spread the same reader-first habits to official writing.[^s2] In the 2010s and 2020s, behavioural scientists such as Todd Rogers and Jessica Lasky-Fink began testing them in field experiments.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Written messages now carry much of the coordination at work. Rogers and Lasky-Fink cite estimates that professionals spend roughly 28% of the work week on email, and that people handle more than 100 emails a day.[^s3] In that flood, unclear writing costs time and leads to missed requests. In their randomised test with 7,002 school district leaders, a 49-word survey request was clicked by 4.8% of recipients, against 2.7% for a 127-word version.[^s3] An older study of 262 U.S. naval officers found that documents in a "high-impact" style (main point first, short paragraphs, headings, plain words) took 17-23% less time to read than the traditional bureaucratic style. Readers also understood them better and reread them less.[^s4]

## What good looks like

- **One reader, one purpose.** The writer knows who the document is for and what that reader must know or do. If different groups need different things, their content is kept apart.[^s1][^s2]
- **Bottom line first.** The purpose and main point open the document; background, exceptions and special cases come later.[^s2]
- **Built for skimming.** Short paragraphs, descriptive headings, lists and a clear route through long documents, such as a table of contents or linked subheadings.[^s2][^s3]
- **Plain, appropriate tone.** No slang or unexplained jargon, inclusive wording, and a register that suits both the reader and the relationship.[^s1]
- **Few asks.** Each extra request lowers the chance that any one of them gets done, so a message carries only the requests it must.[^s3]
- **Clean mechanics.** Substance comes before formatting, but proofreading and parallel structure are still part of the job.[^s1]

**Example.** A facilities coordinator emails 60 staff about an office move. *Before:* four paragraphs on the lease history and the landlord's timeline, with "please pack your desk by the 14th" in the final line. *After:* subject "Pack your desk by Fri 14 June - move weekend", then one opening line with the action and deadline, a three-item bulleted list (crates arrive Tuesday, label with your new desk number, IT disconnects Friday 4 pm) and a link to background for anyone curious. *Why:* readers who only see the preview still learn the action and date, and the history no longer buries the instruction.[^s2][^s3]

## How to practise it

1. **Write the reader and the action first.** Before drafting, jot down who will read the document and the single thing you need from them. Purdue suggests starting with audience analysis when you are stuck.[^s1]
2. **Put the conclusion on top.** Once you know your main point, move it and any request into the first sentences.[^s2]
3. **Revise top-down.** Fix content and soundness (higher-order concerns) before polishing wording, formatting and mechanics (lower-order concerns).[^s1]
4. **Cut requests and ideas, not just words.** Move side points to a link or a later message.[^s3]
5. **Format for the skimmer.** Add headings and lists, and use bold sparingly so that it still signals what matters.[^s2][^s3]
6. **Test it on a real reader.** Ask a colleague to read only the subject line and first paragraph and tell you what they think you want.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The reader-first approach has direct support: the naval-officer study on reading time and comprehension,[^s4] and Rogers and Lasky-Fink's field experiments on shorter, simpler, skimmable messages.[^s3] The status is `mixed` rather than `robust`. The studies are few, they measure narrow outcomes (reading time, response to a request) and many come from the same research groups.[^s3][^s4] Suchan and Dulek also argued in 1990 that clarity is not one universal style. What counts as clear depends on an organisation's language habits and on the social work its documents do, so a plain style can be judged less effective where readers expect a different register.[^s5] In practice, start with the reader-first habits, but check what your audience expects. Formal letters, some client cultures and senior readers who want more context may need more than the leanest version.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

- **Requires audience analysis:** Purdue treats analysing readers' needs, values and attitudes as the starting point for every workplace document.[^s1]
- **Addresses burying the lede:** federal plain-language guidance tells writers to put the purpose and most important information first and move background to the end.[^s2]
- **Addresses wall of text and is applied in email:** Rogers and Lasky-Fink's advice to make messages short and skimmable was developed and tested on email and text requests.[^s3]
- **Applied in memorandums** and the other genres Purdue lists for workplace writers, such as business letters, reports and press releases.[^s1]
- **Supported by Suchan and Colucci (1989),** whose high-impact documents were read faster and understood better than bureaucratic versions.[^s4]
- **Challenged by Suchan and Dulek (1990),** who argued for a contingency view in which clarity depends on organisational context.[^s5]
- **Related:** plain language, concise writing, message structure and the genres business writing produces (see those articles for their sources).
