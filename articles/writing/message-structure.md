# Message structure

Message structure is the arrangement of a message's content: which point comes first, how the remaining points are grouped, in what order they appear, and how that order is made visible to the audience. Workplace writing guidance puts the core rule simply: organise around the reader rather than around the path you took to your conclusion, which usually means giving the bottom line first and the reasoning after it.[^s2] U.S. federal plain-language guidance likewise asks writers to start from what readers already know and what questions they will bring, then put the most important information first and background near the end.[^s1]

## Origin

Arranging a message is one of the oldest topics in communication teaching, and modern speech textbooks still treat organising the body of a talk as a core skill. They ground it in mid-century speech experiments: Smith (1951) found that speeches with randomly ordered parts were judged more negatively and held less interest, Thompson (1960) found disorganised speeches harder to recall, and Baker (1965) found disorganised speakers less persuasive and less credible.[^s4] In writing, reading researchers such as Bonnie Meyer studied how the top-level structure of expository text (problem–solution, comparison, cause–effect and similar patterns) shapes what readers remember.[^s5] Plain-language and business-writing guides turned these ideas into practical patterns for workplace documents.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

Structure decides whether a busy reader finds the point. Readers increasingly see messages on phones and watches, so the first sentence often has to carry the main point on its own.[^s2] A clear order also lets people read only part of a document: academic writing guidance stresses making each section understandable to someone who reads only that section.[^s3] For spoken messages the stakes include credibility as well as comprehension, since audiences rate disorganised speakers less favourably and recall less of what they said.[^s4]

## How to apply it

- **Decide the main point and the reader's likely reaction first.** The best order depends on what the reader cares about and how they are likely to respond.[^s2]
- **Choose the direct pattern by default.** For routine, positive or neutral messages, requests and announcements, put the main point in the first sentence, then the details, then next steps; skip stock openers.[^s2]
- **Pick an ordering principle for the body.** Common patterns are general to specific, most to least important, chronological, by category and problem to solution.[^s2] Use chronological order for procedures, and cover what applies to most readers before exceptions and special cases.[^s1]
- **Make the structure visible.** Give long documents a reliable roadmap, such as a table of contents or links to headings that match reader questions.[^s1] Add signposts, such as previews, transitions and summaries, at the starts and ends of sections.[^s3]
- **Check the draft with a reverse outline.** List the main point of each section of a finished draft and ask whether the order works for both novice and expert readers.[^s3]

**Example.** An operations lead's email to a director opens with three paragraphs on supplier delays and only reaches the request, approval to switch carriers by Friday, in the last line. Restructured, the request and deadline come first, followed by the two reasons in order of importance and a short timeline, so the director can decide from the opening lines.[^s2]

**Bad news.** Traditional business-writing guidance recommends an indirect pattern for unwelcome news: a neutral subject line, a buffer, the reasons, then the refusal and a goodwill close.[^s2] The evidence for buffering is thin. In Limaye's 1988 study, offering alternatives improved how recipients saw the sender, while where the rejection appeared made little difference.[^s6] A practical reading is to state bad news clearly and early enough not to mislead, give a brief reason, and offer an alternative where one exists.[^s6]

## Evidence and limits

The research base is real but uneven. Meyer and Poon (2001) found that adults trained to look for a text's top-level structure recalled more of the total and the most important information across varied texts, and that explicit signals in the text helped readers use that structure.[^s5] The speech studies on disorganised talks are old and mostly used student audiences.[^s4] Studies of buffers in bad-news letters do not support the standard indirect formula.[^s6] Specific workplace formulas, such as which pattern suits which message, rest largely on practitioner and government guidance rather than controlled tests.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Message structure **complements** plain language, which treats organising for the reader as one of its core principles.[^s1] It is **applied in** email, where the first sentence should carry the point,[^s2] in presentations, where organisation affects recall and credibility,[^s4] and in delivering bad news, where the placement of the refusal matters less than offering alternatives.[^s6] It is **supported by** Meyer and Poon's 2001 study showing that attention to text structure and signalling improves recall.[^s5] Bottom line up front, the pyramid principle and informative headings are specific structuring techniques; signposting is its spoken counterpart; and a wall of text or a buried lede is what weak structure looks like.[^s2][^s3]
