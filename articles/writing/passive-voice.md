# Passive voice

The passive voice turns the receiver of an action into the grammatical subject, so the doer either moves to the end of the sentence in a "by" phrase or vanishes: "Finance approved the budget" becomes "The budget was approved by finance" or simply "The budget was approved."[^s3] It is a choice of style, not a grammatical error; the University of North Carolina Writing Center stresses that passives are correct English and become a problem only when they blur who did what.[^s3] Knowing what a passive actually is, and what it does to a reader, lets a writer keep the useful ones and fix the evasive ones instead of following a blanket rule.[^s1]

## Origin

Linguist Geoffrey Pullum dates warnings against the passive to composition manuals from 1907 onward, with Orwell's 1946 essay on political language among the most quoted.[^s1] His figures show the irony: depending on who did the counting, that essay's passive rate is about 20–26%, against roughly 13–17% for typical prose.[^s1] Modern plain-language programmes kept a softer version of the rule, recommending active voice by default while allowing passives for specific purposes.[^s5]

## How to recognise it

A true passive pairs a form of *be* (or, informally, *get*) with a past participle, and the subject is the thing acted on: "The invoice was rejected," "The client got overcharged."[^s1][^s3] Pullum also counts reduced forms such as headline passives ("Three injured in warehouse fire") and "needs" constructions ("The report needs checking").[^s1] Three look-alikes are not passive: past tense ("We rejected the invoice"), *be* describing a state ("She is the account lead"), and intransitive verbs where nobody acts on anything ("Sales fell").[^s1][^s3] Grammar checkers catch only some passives and flag some non-passives, so treat a flag as a prompt to look, not a verdict.[^s3]

## Why it matters

The main workplace risk is the agentless passive. The Australian Government Style Manual notes that "The reform was implemented" leaves readers asking who implemented it, and advises adding context that names the doer.[^s5] In action items, policies and incident reports, that missing doer becomes missing ownership: "The contract must be signed by Friday" does not say whether the reader, legal or the vendor signs.[^s5][^s3] UNC's handout adds the reputational angle, using "Mistakes were made" as the textbook case of a passive that hides responsibility, and urges readers to notice the move in others' writing.[^s3]

Yet passives often help the reader. Pullum's central point is about information flow: a passive lets the writer keep what the reader already knows at the front of the sentence and put new information, including a new actor, at the end.[^s1] A Government of Canada language adviser makes the same case: keeping the paragraph's topic in subject position lets readers concentrate on what is new, and converting such passives to active can make a passage jarring.[^s2] Passives also fit when the doer is unknown ("Her laptop was stolen"), obvious ("The election was held") or irrelevant, and when the result matters more than who produced it, as in many methods sections.[^s5][^s2][^s3]

## Using it deliberately

Before keeping or rewriting a passive, ask three questions. Does the reader need to know who acts, especially who must act next? If so, name them, usually by switching to active voice.[^s5] Is the thing acted on the topic of this paragraph? Then the passive may be keeping the thread, and the doer can still appear in a "by" phrase.[^s2][^s1] Am I using it to soften or avoid admitting a decision or error? In feedback, apologies and incident write-ups, readers notice a missing actor, and UNC's guidance treats that evasion as the passive's worst use.[^s3]

**Workplace example.** An incident summary reads: "Customer data was exposed after access settings were changed." Rewritten: "Customer data was exposed for six hours on 3 March after our platform team changed the access settings during a migration." The first passive stays because the data is the subject of the report; the second becomes active because readers need to know who changed the settings and that the team owns the fix.[^s5][^s2]

## Evidence

Psychology studies suggest voice shapes how responsibility is perceived, though the effects are modest and context-bound. In Bohner's 2001 study, 67 German students wrote descriptions of a filmed assault; they used the passive more often for the perpetrator's actions than for other actions, and heavier passive use correlated weakly with attitudes that shift blame toward the victim.[^s4] Bohner calls the correlations modest and the conclusions tentative, and notes that experiments on readers have produced mixed results.[^s4] In two experiments on organisational crisis responses, Fannes and Claeys (2023) found that passive wording lowered perceived organisational responsibility and limited reputational damage, but only for denials, not apologies.[^s6] Together these support the status `mixed`: voice can move judgements of who is responsible, but the evidence comes from a handful of studies with specific scenarios. For workplace writers, the ethical line is clear: using the passive to keep focus is legitimate; using it to hide a known, relevant actor misleads the reader.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

- **Contrasts with active voice,** the default that plain-language guides recommend; UNC treats the two as options to choose between, not right and wrong.[^s3]
- **Causes unclear ownership** when the passive is agentless and the reader needs the doer, as the Australian Style Manual's "the reform was implemented" example shows.[^s5]
- **Explained by given-new information structure:** passives let writers keep known topics first and put new information last.[^s2][^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Bohner (2001), linking passive use to how responsibility is assigned, and by Fannes and Claeys (2023), showing passive crisis denials reduce perceived responsibility.[^s4][^s6]
- **Applied in crisis communication,** where voice in a response statement changes how much blame an organisation receives.[^s6]
- **Related:** Strunk's early rule against the passive and Pullum's critique of it frame the debate;[^s1] nominalization and the "characters as subjects, actions as verbs" principle are neighbouring ways sentences hide actors, and the Paramedic Method is a common editing routine for both (see those articles for their sources).
