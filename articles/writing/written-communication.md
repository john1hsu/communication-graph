# Written communication

Written communication is any message carried by text instead of speech: an email, a chat message, a report, a shared document, a comment on a ticket. Communication textbooks contrast it with spoken communication along a few dimensions: writing is usually more formal, mostly asynchronous (the reader responds when they have time), and archived so it can be retrieved later, while speech is usually informal, happens in real time with immediate feedback, and survives mainly in memory.[^s1] Texting and email blur these lines, but the core trade-off holds: the writer gains time to think and a lasting record, and loses the voice, face and interruptions that let a speaker notice and repair a misunderstanding.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

The distinction between speech and writing is old, but its workplace stakes grew with email and chat, which made text the default channel for everyday coordination. Communication scholars such as Paynton and Hahn treat formality, timing, recording and privacy as the main differences, and note that people often treat email and texts as private conversations even though they can be saved and resurfaced.[^s1] Organisational researchers then asked what text does to emotion and impressions: Kristin Byron's 2008 review in the *Academy of Management Review* argued that email's features make emotional miscommunication likely, and a series of experiments by Nicholas Epley and colleagues compared how the same words land when read versus heard.[^s5][^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

Text hides tone, and writers rarely notice. In experiments by Justin Kruger, Nicholas Epley and colleagues, people sent email messages meant to sound sarcastic, serious, angry, sad or funny; misunderstandings were common even between people who knew each other, and senders were typically overconfident that their intent had come through, which the authors traced to egocentrism.[^s3] Byron's review drew on research on computer-mediated communication, emotion and perception to argue that receivers tend to read work email as more negative or more neutral than the sender meant.[^s5]

Text can also make the writer seem less thoughtful. In Juliana Schroeder and Nicholas Epley's job-pitch experiments, professional recruiters and other evaluators rated candidates as more intelligent and were more interested in hiring them when they heard a pitch than when they read the identical words; adding video to audio made no further difference.[^s4] In four later experiments, people who disagreed with a speaker judged them as more humanly thoughtful when they heard the opinion than when they read a transcript, an effect partly carried by intonation and pauses.[^s2]

## What works here

Writing is the right channel when the reader needs to act alone, refer back later, or receive the same message as many others, and when precision and a record matter.[^s1][^s6]

1. **Write for a specific reader.** Before drafting, ask what the reader already knows, what they need, and which questions they will have, because they cannot interrupt you to ask.[^s6]
2. **Lead with the main point.** State the purpose and bottom line first, put the most important information next, and move background toward the end.[^s6]
3. **Order for use.** Put steps in the order the reader will follow them, general information before exceptions, and use headings as a roadmap in long documents.[^s6]
4. **Make tone explicit.** Because readers lean toward a more negative or neutral reading, say plainly how you feel and what you intend rather than relying on irony or brevity.[^s5][^s3]
5. **Re-read as the recipient.** Check the draft from the reader's position, not your own, since senders overestimate how clearly their intent comes across.[^s3]
6. **Write as if it will be forwarded.** Treat any message as a permanent record that may reach people you did not choose.[^s1]

## Pitfalls

- **Disagreeing in text.** Hearing someone's voice makes a disagreeing listener see more mind behind the view; for contentious points, a call may keep the relationship intact.[^s2]
- **Persuading or introducing yourself only in writing.** Job seekers in Schroeder and Epley's studies expected to do as well in writing, yet were judged as less capable than when heard.[^s4]
- **Sarcasm and dry humour.** These are the messages most likely to be misread, and the sender is least likely to notice.[^s3]
- **Burying the point** under background, which forces the reader to dig for what you need.[^s6]
- **Assuming privacy.** Archived messages can be retrieved and made public long after they were sent.[^s1]

## Evidence

The core findings come from controlled experiments. The voice-versus-text effects replicated across several studies within each paper, but each used a small number of speakers (six to ten per experiment in the 2017 paper), mostly online evaluators, and exploratory persuasion measures, and the predicted role of agreement was not consistently supported.[^s2][^s4] Byron's account is a theoretical review built on earlier research rather than a new test.[^s5] The broad pattern - text is clearer for records and reference but weaker for tone, warmth and perceived thoughtfulness - is well supported; its size in real workplace channels is less certain, so status is `mixed`.[^s2][^s3][^s5]
