# Memorandum

A memorandum, or memo, is a document written for readers inside the same organisation. It opens with a block of To, From, Date and Subject lines, skips the salutation of a letter, and deals with one matter: announcing a change, reporting a finding, recommending a course of action or asking someone to act.[^s1][^s5] Textbooks describe memos as mostly informative and sometimes persuasive, sent one-to-many inside an organisation, and suited to routine, non-sensitive content where getting straight to the point is expected.[^s5] Government correspondence rules draw the same boundary: the U.S. Army uses the memorandum for correspondence within and between agencies and for internal business, but not for letters to families or private businesses.[^s3]

## Origin

The memo is younger than it looks. Business historian JoAnne Yates traced its rise in American firms in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, using archives from companies such as Du Pont and Scovill Manufacturing.[^s2] She found that it grew out of the business letter as a practical response to two changes: new management methods that relied on written internal reporting and control, and new writing technology. Rhetorical theory did not drive it, and communication textbooks recognised the genre well after firms were already using it.[^s2] The email header is a direct descendant: its date and subject fields come from the memo heading, which is why the same subject-line rules apply to both.[^s5]

## Why it matters

A memo is often the record a decision rests on. Readers must understand it in one reading and act. Army correspondence rules make main point first (bottom line up front) and active voice the two essential requirements of Army writing, ask for one-page memorandums with enclosures for the detail, and set sentences at about 15 words on average.[^s3] A memorandum for record serves a further purpose: it documents decisions or agreements reached in a meeting or phone call and records the authority for an action, so the team is not relying on memory later.[^s3]

The longer, narrative memo can also replace slides. In his 2017 letter to shareholders, Jeff Bezos described Amazon's practice of banning slide presentations in favour of six-page, narratively structured memos that everyone reads silently at the start of a meeting.[^s4] He warned that a great memo is written, reviewed by colleagues, set aside and rewritten, and that expecting one in an afternoon is a mistake about how long the work takes.[^s4]

## What works here

- **Write a specific subject line.** Name the topic and, if you can, the ask in eight to ten words, for example "Approve Q3 vendor shortlist by June 12" rather than "Vendors".[^s1][^s5][^s3]
- **Open with purpose and the main point.** In one or two sentences, say why you are writing and what you want. Give context only after that, and only as much as the reader needs.[^s1][^s5]
- **Lead the discussion with the most important information.** Move from general to specific and give the evidence that supports your recommendation; add a short summary only when the memo runs past a page.[^s1]
- **Keep it to a page and use enclosures.** Put tables, data and long background in attachments, and mention each one in the body.[^s3][^s1]
- **Make it skimmable.** Use paragraphs of three to five sentences, plus lists, tables and headings where they help.[^s5]
- **Close with a concrete next step.** State the action, the deadline and who to contact, and make the action easy to take.[^s1][^s5]
- **Write a memo for record after a decision.** Record what was agreed, by whom and on what basis, and send it to whoever must act.[^s3]

**Example.** *Before:* Subject "Update". The memo opens with two paragraphs on last year's vendor problems, and the request to approve a shortlist by Friday appears in the final line. *After:* Subject "Approve Q3 vendor shortlist by Friday". The first sentence asks the operations director to approve three named vendors by Friday so contracts can start in July, the next paragraph gives the cost and risk comparison, and the scoring table is an attachment. *Why:* the reader can decide from the subject line and opening sentence, and the detail is there for checking.[^s1][^s3][^s5]

## Pitfalls

- **Burying the purpose under background.** History-first openings hide the request; put the point in the first paragraph.[^s5][^s3]
- **Vague closings.** Lines such as "contact me at your earliest convenience" give no deadline or action.[^s5]
- **Subjective or ambiguous language.** Memos are expected to be objective and precise, because ambiguity causes mistakes; spell out dates rather than using numeric formats that readers in other countries may misread.[^s5]
- **Using a memo for the wrong audience or message.** Keep memos for internal, routine business; external contacts and sensitive news call for a letter or a conversation.[^s3][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

Advice on memo structure comes from writing centres, textbooks and government style rules rather than controlled experiments on memos themselves.[^s1][^s3][^s5] The scholarly work on the genre is mainly historical: Yates's archival study explains why the memo appeared and how it became the standard tool for internal management communication.[^s2] Amazon's six-page narrative is a single company's account by its founder, not a tested comparison with slide presentations, so treat it as one well-documented practice.[^s4]

## Connections

A memorandum **contrasts with** email: email inherited the memo's header and subject-line conventions but is a channel, not a format, and drops the memo's formality.[^s5] The genre is **studied in** organisational communication, where Yates traced how the memo developed as a managerial genre.[^s2] Amazon's narrative memo is an **alternative to** slide presentations for decision meetings.[^s4] Memos **complement** plain language, since Army rules ask for short words, short sentences and no jargon.[^s3] Bottom line up front, active voice and informative headings are techniques used to structure a memo; the executive summary, status report and business report are neighbouring genres; and burying the lede is the failure a memo's purpose-first opening is designed to prevent.[^s1][^s3][^s5]
