# Reverse outlining

Reverse outlining means building an outline *after* you have written a draft rather than before. You go through the draft one paragraph or section at a time, write down in a short phrase what each part actually says, and then read that list as a map of the document's real structure.[^s1] Because the map shows what is on the page rather than what you meant to put there, it exposes problems that are hard to see from inside the prose: points in the wrong order, paragraphs that wander off topic, paragraphs doing two jobs, ideas repeated in several places and gaps in the reasoning.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The technique has no single named inventor; it is a long-standing staple of university writing centres, which teach it as a way to revise the organisation of a working draft.[^s1][^s3] Its rationale comes from composition research on how writers revise. In a 1980 study published in *College Composition and Communication*, Nancy Sommers compared student writers with experienced adult writers and found that the students treated revision mainly as swapping words, while the experienced writers first looked for the shape and line of argument of the whole piece, kept an imagined reader in mind and only then turned to phrasing.[^s4] Reverse outlining gives less experienced writers a concrete routine for that whole-document pass.[^s4][^s3] Business-writing textbooks now teach the same method for workplace messages, framing each paragraph against the message's stated purpose.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Writers rarely end up with exactly the document they planned, and they are poorly placed to notice the difference because they already know what they meant.[^s1][^s2] A reader of a work email, proposal or report has none of that background, so a missing step in the logic or a request buried in the fifth paragraph costs them time or leads them to the wrong conclusion.[^s2][^s5] Fixing structure first also saves effort: there is little point polishing sentences in a paragraph that will later be moved, merged or cut.[^s3][^s4]

## How to apply it

Start by writing the document's purpose at the top of a page, copying the sentence from the draft that states it; if you cannot find one, that is your first finding.[^s5] Then work through the draft and, for each paragraph, record its main idea using only the words actually on the page, plus a note on how that idea advances the purpose.[^s5] Length is a useful signal: if a paragraph cannot be summarised in a handful of words, it may be underdeveloped or unfocused, and if it needs a long sentence, it is probably juggling several ideas.[^s3]

Next, read the summaries as a set. Look for paragraphs that share the same point and could be merged, paragraphs that hold two ideas and should be split, paragraphs with no clear point, points that do not connect to the purpose and jumps in the reasoning that need reordering or a transition.[^s5][^s2] Treat the summaries as movable pieces: rearrange them on the outline until the sequence works, then rebuild the draft to match.[^s3] A strong variant is to ask a colleague to reverse-outline your draft independently and compare their list with yours; where the two disagree, your point is not reaching the reader.[^s3]

In workplace writing, checking every paragraph against the stated purpose tends to pull the purpose or request to the front and push background that does not serve it out of the message.[^s5] Have the outline in front of you when you rewrite, so each topic sentence states the point you assigned to that paragraph.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

- **Summarising what you meant, not what you wrote.** The value comes from recording only what is on the page; filling gaps from memory hides exactly the problems the outline should reveal.[^s5][^s1]
- **Using it to fix grammar or wording.** Reverse outlining is a tool for organisation and focus, not for punctuation, usage or sentence-level problems.[^s3]
- **Using it too early or too late.** It needs a working draft to map, and it adds least to a nearly finished draft whose structure is already settled.[^s3]
- **Listing topics without purposes.** A list of subjects shows order but not relevance; noting how each point serves the purpose is what reveals paragraphs that should be cut.[^s5]
- **Changing the outline but not the draft.** Reordering points on paper only helps if you then rewrite the topic sentences and transitions so the new order reads smoothly.[^s2][^s5]

## Connections

Reverse outlining is a specific tool for the structural pass of **editing for clarity**, putting into practice the experienced writers' habit of settling the whole text's shape before its wording.[^s4][^s3] It is **applied in** written communication generally, and business-writing guidance uses it on everyday workplace messages as well as reports.[^s5] It **addresses** the wall of text by flagging paragraphs that carry too many ideas so they can be split or trimmed,[^s3] and it **addresses** the curse of knowledge by surfacing gaps in logic and flow that the writer misses because they already understand the intent.[^s2] It is **supported by** Sommers's 1980 finding that experienced writers revise the whole text's argument before its words.[^s4] It **contrasts with** sentence-level methods such as the paramedic method: the reverse outline works on organisation and focus and is not meant for wording or grammar, so the two are used in sequence, structure first.[^s3] It pairs naturally with message structure, informative headings and bottom line up front, which give the rebuilt draft its shape, and with peer review of writing, since a colleague's independent outline is a quick check on whether the structure lands.[^s3][^s5]
