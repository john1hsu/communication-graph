steps:
  - Set the draft aside, then write one sentence stating who the reader is and what they must know or do after reading.
  - Do a structure pass first - check that the main point or request comes first, that each section serves the purpose, and that headings and order match the reader's questions.
  - Do a paragraph pass - give each paragraph one job, open it with its point, and add the linking words or steps the reader cannot infer.
  - Do a sentence pass - make the doer the subject and the action the verb, split sentences that carry two ideas, and cut wind-ups and repetition.
  - Do a word pass last - replace jargon and undefined terms, then fix spelling and punctuation.
  - Test high-stakes text with two or three people from the real audience - ask them to say in their own words what it means and what they would do next, and revise wherever their answer differs from your intent.
example:
  situation: A team lead emails department managers about a change to how equipment is ordered.
  before: "Following the recent review of procurement workflows and in light of feedback received regarding processing delays, it has been determined that, effective from the start of next quarter, requests for equipment will be subject to an updated approval process, details of which are outlined in the attached document, which managers are asked to review."
  after: "From 1 January, send equipment requests to your finance partner instead of IT; they will approve them within three working days. Please tell your team before the holidays. The attached one-page guide shows the new form."
  why: The structure pass moved the change and the request to the front, the paragraph pass added the missing link (who approves and how fast), and the sentence and word passes replaced "it has been determined" and "subject to an updated approval process" with actors and verbs a manager can act on.
selfCheck:
  - Did I fix purpose, order and missing information before I started polishing words?
  - Could the intended reader restate my main point and next step in their own words after one reading?
  - Which term or step am I assuming the reader knows only because I know it?
  - Have I shown this to someone outside my own head, and did their paraphrase match what I meant?

# Editing for clarity

Editing for clarity is the revision work that turns a draft that makes sense to its writer into one that makes sense to its reader. Rather than reading a draft once and fixing whatever catches the eye, you edit in passes ordered from the biggest problems to the smallest: purpose, audience and organisation first, then paragraphs and sentences, then word choice, spelling and punctuation.[^s1] For important documents, the last step is to check the result against real readers rather than your own sense that it is clear.[^s3]

## Origin

The ordering comes from writing instruction. University writing centres teach it as "higher-order concerns before lower-order concerns": thesis, audience and purpose, organisation and development come first, and sentence structure, word choice and spelling come after.[^s1] The research behind it goes back to Nancy Sommers' 1980 study in *College Composition and Communication*. She compared 20 first-year university students with 20 experienced adult writers (journalists, editors and academics), each of whom wrote and twice revised three essays. The students treated revision mostly as rewording and rule-checking. The experienced writers revised at every level, from single words to the shape of the whole argument, worked in repeated cycles with a different focus each time, and revised with an imagined reader in mind.[^s2] Reader-side sentence diagnostics come from Joseph Williams' *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*, now co-authored with Joseph Bizup, whose premise is that readers, not writers, decide whether a message came across.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Most unclear workplace drafts fail on structure, not single words: the request sits in the third paragraph, a step is missing, or a term means something else to the reader. Polishing sentences cannot fix that, and polishing a paragraph that later gets cut is wasted effort, which is why structural concerns come first.[^s1] The reader's view also matters because writers cannot see what they already know. The U.S. government's plain-language guidance gives an example from the Veterans Benefits Administration: testing a letter showed that a vague phrase about when a replacement cheque would arrive would have generated phone calls, and that readers defined "service-connected disability" in different ways. Adding an approximate date and defining the term fixed both problems.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Start by writing one sentence that names the reader and what they must know or do. That sentence is the standard every pass is measured against.[^s1] In the structure pass, check that the main point comes first and that the order follows the reader's questions. In the paragraph pass, look for gaps the reader would have to fill in for themselves. Revisions that supply missing connections help readers most when they lack background knowledge or the motivation to work things out.[^s5] In the sentence pass, work through the topics Williams and Bizup treat as the core of clarity: actions, characters, cohesion and emphasis, followed by concision. In practice, ask who is acting and what they do in each sentence, check that each sentence links to the one before, and then cut words that add nothing.[^s4] Leave spelling and punctuation until last, unless errors are so dense near the top that they would stop the reader reaching the main point.[^s1] For letters, policies, instructions and web pages that many people will act on, run a paraphrase test. Digital.gov suggests one-to-one sessions with about six to nine readers who explain each section in their own words while you note, without correcting, where their reading departs from yours.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Starting at the word level.** Rewording before checking purpose and order is the student pattern Sommers found. It produces neater sentences inside a structure that still fails the reader.[^s2]
- **Editing in one pass.** Judging structure and commas at once starves the large problems of attention; experienced writers revise in cycles with separate focuses.[^s2]
- **Trusting your own reading.** You cannot read your draft without your own knowledge, so a paraphrase from someone else is the check that counts. Ask open questions such as "What would you do after reading this?" rather than "Is this clear?"[^s3]
- **Equating clear with short.** Cutting is one part of clarity, but adding a missing step, definition or date often helps more than deleting words.[^s5][^s3]

## Evidence

The evidence status is `mixed`. The descriptive research is solid: Sommers' comparison shows that skilled writers revise globally and with the reader in mind, while novices mostly reword.[^s2] A 1999 review in *Document Design* looked at studies that revised natural texts using three principles taken from Kintsch's model of how readers process text, an approach first applied by Britton and Gülgöz in 1991. Across different texts, languages and measures, revisions that added the missing connections improved learning, and the benefit was largest for readers with little domain knowledge, weak inference skills or low motivation.[^s5] These studies measured learning from instructional text, not workplace actions such as replies or errors avoided. The particular order of passes and the sentence rules are practitioner guidance rather than tested procedures.[^s1][^s4] Government guidance treats reader testing as the way to find out whether a particular document works.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Addresses the curse of knowledge:** paraphrase testing exists because readers often interpret text differently from what the writer intended, and testing exposes terms and gaps the writer could not see.[^s3]
- **Complements plain language:** reader testing is part of the U.S. government's plain-language guide series. Plain language sets the standard, and editing passes and tests are how a draft meets it.[^s3]
- **Develops concise writing:** concision is one of the lessons in Williams and Bizup's reader-centred method of revising sentences.[^s4]
- **Supported by Sommers (1980)**, whose comparison of novice and experienced writers is the classic evidence for top-down, reader-focused revision.[^s2]
- **Supported by Britton and Gülgöz (1991)** and later studies reviewed in *Document Design*, which found that revisions supplying missing connections improve readers' learning.[^s5]
- **Related:** the paramedic method and "characters as subjects, actions as verbs" serve the sentence pass; informative headings and bottom-line-up-front serve the structure pass; readability formulas give a rough final check.[^s4][^s1]
