# Feedback

Feedback is information, from a manager, peer, client, tool or the task itself, about how someone is performing or what they understand.[^s2] Its job is to close a gap: it helps the person see where they are heading, how they are doing against that goal, and what to do next.[^s2] At work it ranges from a quick "that slide landed well" to a formal performance review, and it is the parent idea behind specific techniques such as Situation-Behavior-Impact and feedforward.[^s4][^s1]

## Origin

Organizational psychologists made feedback a research topic in its own right. In a landmark 1979 review in the *Journal of Applied Psychology*, Ilgen, Fisher and Taylor noted that feedback was widely assumed to be essential to good performance, yet little was known about how it actually changed behaviour; they broke the process into whether feedback is perceived, whether it is accepted, and whether the recipient is willing to act on it.[^s4] Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 review traced research on feedback back to the early 1900s and showed that harmful effects had been reported all along but largely ignored.[^s1] In education, John Hattie and Helen Timperley's 2007 review supplied the widely used "where am I going, how am I going, where to next" model.[^s2] On the receiving side, Douglas Stone, a former associate director of the Harvard Negotiation Project, and Sheila Heen wrote *Thanks for the Feedback: The Science and Art of Receiving Feedback Well* (Penguin, 2015 edition).[^s5]

## Why it matters

Feedback is one of the few levers that reliably moves performance, but it is not safe by default. Kluger and DeNisi's meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes (23,663 observations) found an average improvement of d = 0.41, yet more than a third of feedback interventions lowered performance.[^s1] Their explanation, feedback intervention theory, is that feedback shifts attention: it helps when it keeps attention on the task and on how to learn it, and loses power as it pulls attention toward the self, for example toward one's standing or self-image.[^s1] For a manager, that means the same conversation can lift or sink a colleague's next piece of work depending on what it makes them think about.

## What useful feedback looks like

Hattie and Timperley distinguish four levels: feedback about the task (is this right?), the process (how was it done?), self-regulation (how is the person monitoring their own work?) and the self (praise or criticism of the person).[^s2] They found process and self-regulation feedback most powerful and personal-level feedback, including generic praise, the least useful because it carries little information about the work.[^s2] A 2020 re-analysis likewise found that feedback's effect depends heavily on how much information it carries.[^s3]

A workplace illustration: after a client workshop, "You're a natural presenter" is self-level feedback. "Starting with the client's own churn numbers got them engaged before the recommendations - do that again next week" is task and process feedback the presenter can repeat. "Before the next one, how will you check the room is following the cost section?" invites self-regulation. The last two keep attention on the work, which is where the meta-analyses suggest the gains come from.[^s1][^s2]

Feedback also has a receiver, and much of it fails at that end. Heen describes three kinds people want at different times (appreciation, coaching and evaluation) and warns that evaluation is the most volatile because its emotional charge can drown out the coaching inside it.[^s6] She suggests telling colleagues which kind you need, asking narrow questions such as "what's one thing I could do differently?", and looking for what might be right in criticism before rejecting it.[^s6] This matches Ilgen and colleagues' point that feedback only changes behaviour if it is perceived, accepted and acted on.[^s4]

## Evidence

The evidence is strong that feedback matters and equally strong that its effects vary widely, so its status here is mixed. Hattie and Timperley's synthesis of 12 meta-analyses (196 studies, 6,972 effect sizes) reported an average effect of 0.79 on learning, while noting that some forms helped and others harmed.[^s2] The 2020 meta-analysis of 435 studies, 994 effect sizes and more than 61,000 participants found a smaller, medium effect (d = 0.48) with significant heterogeneity, and concluded that feedback should not be treated as one uniform treatment.[^s3] It also found larger effects on cognitive and motor skills than on motivation and behaviour.[^s3] Two limits matter for workplace readers: much of this research comes from classrooms and laboratory tasks, and Kluger and DeNisi noted that the task features that moderate feedback were still poorly understood.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

Feedback is **demonstrated by** Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 meta-analysis (`cm:kluger-denisi-1996`) and **explained by** the feedback intervention theory (`cm:feedback-intervention-theory`) it proposed.[^s1] It is also **explained by** Hattie and Timperley's model of feedback questions and levels (`cm:hattie-timperley-feedback-model`).[^s2] The 2020 meta-analysis by Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie (`cm:wisniewski-zierer-hattie-2020`) **demonstrates** both its average benefit and its variability.[^s3] It is **studied in** industrial-organizational psychology (`cm:industrial-organizational-psychology`), where Ilgen, Fisher and Taylor framed it as a process of perception, acceptance and response.[^s4] Situation-Behavior-Impact and feedforward are narrower techniques for giving it, and direct versus indirect feedback describes how its delivery varies across cultures.[^s1][^s2] Receiving feedback is the other half of the exchange, and defensiveness is the main way it fails there.[^s6]
