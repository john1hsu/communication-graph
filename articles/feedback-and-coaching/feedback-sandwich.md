steps:
  - Decide whether a sandwich is needed at all - routine corrections in a healthy working relationship can be given on their own, and praise can be given separately when it is earned.
  - Pick one specific, important correction; if there are several, the issue is bigger than a sandwich and needs a fuller conversation.
  - Open with sincere praise for a specific behaviour you want the person to keep, ideally related to the same piece of work.
  - State the correction plainly as an observable behaviour, not a judgement of the person, and offer a concrete alternative.
  - Close with genuine, specific encouragement - not a generic compliment - and invite the person to respond, then paraphrase what they say.
  - Afterwards, check that the correction was heard by asking what they will do differently next time.

# Feedback sandwich

The feedback sandwich is a way of giving criticism in three layers: specific praise, then one piece of corrective feedback, then more praise.[^s1] Clinical psychologist Anne Dohrenwend, writing for family physicians who supervise staff, described it as a single specific criticism placed between two specific praises, meant to make negative feedback easier both to give and to hear.[^s1] It is one of the most widely taught feedback formulas, recommended in medicine, nursing, coaching, education and management.[^s3] The research on it, however, is thin and mostly unflattering, which is why this graph marks it as advice that outruns its evidence.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

The method has no single clear inventor. Dohrenwend's 2002 *Family Practice Management* article, a common reference point, credits the term "sandwiching" to Schwenk and Whitman's 1987 book *The Physician as Teacher* rather than claiming it as her own.[^s1] By 2015, Henley and DiGennaro Reed could describe it as recommended across many professions while noting that, before their work, studies of feedback order had measured how credible feedback seemed, not whether it changed performance.[^s3] They also note the public argument about it: a 2012 business tip endorsed the sandwich and a 2013 *Harvard Business Review* piece argued against it.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Most managers find giving criticism uncomfortable, and the sandwich is often the first technique they are taught to cope with that.[^s1] Its stated benefits are real goals: making criticism easier to accept, showing staff that the supervisor sees strengths as well as gaps, and helping new supervisors build credibility.[^s1] The risk is that the formula feels effective without being effective. In Parkes and colleagues' studies of medical students, recipients believed sandwiched feedback improved their next piece of work when there was no evidence that it did.[^s2] A technique that reassures both giver and receiver while leaving performance unchanged can quietly replace feedback that would actually help.

## How to apply it

If you use the structure, Dohrenwend's own version is stricter than the popular one: both pieces of praise must be specific and sincere, the criticism should be one specific behaviour with a suggested alternative, and the feedback should be timely and about behaviour rather than the person.[^s1] She adds that the employee should get a chance to respond and that the supervisor should paraphrase that response.[^s1] She also warns against applying it rigidly: not all feedback needs to be sandwiched, healthy working relationships let praise and criticism be given separately, and complex performance problems need more than one sandwich.[^s1] In practice this means treating the sandwich as an occasional option for a single, modest correction - not as the default shape of every feedback conversation.[^s1][^s2]

## Common mistakes

The commonest failure is using vague praise as padding. Dohrenwend says that insincere praise used to soften bad news - her example is praise before a dismissal - is not a true sandwich at all.[^s1] Henley and DiGennaro Reed summarise the two main criticisms: the surrounding praise can dilute the correction so it is missed, and routine pairing can devalue praise so people start waiting for the "but".[^s3] Another trap is the effort itself: Parkes and colleagues point out that writing proper sandwiches takes work, which matters when the payoff in performance is not there.[^s2] Finally, a sandwich cannot carry a serious or repeated performance issue; that needs a direct conversation.[^s1]

## Evidence

Controlled tests are few and small, and they mostly fail to find a performance advantage. In two studies of third-year medical students' written peer feedback (N = 20 and N = 350), published in 2013, sandwiched feedback changed students' perceptions but not their subsequent performance.[^s2] In a 2015 lab study with eight undergraduates doing simulated office tasks, Henley and DiGennaro Reed held the content constant and varied only the order; the sandwich was not the most effective sequence, corrective-first feedback ranked highest, and results varied a lot between individuals.[^s3] A 2021 study training staff to run a behavioural assessment found within-session feedback did best at first, and by the final role-plays there were no significant differences between the sandwich, constructive-then-positive feedback and the control; satisfaction did not differ either.[^s4]

The one positive result comes from a 2020 randomised experiment with 91 university students solving maths problems: those whose computer-delivered corrections were framed by two generic positive statements prepared longer and solved more problems on a second set than those given corrections alone or no feedback.[^s5] The authors call this partial evidence from one narrow setting and say it is unclear whether the order or simply the presence of positive statements produced the effect.[^s5] Taken together, the evidence does not show the sandwich is harmful, but it does not support the confidence with which it is taught.[^s2][^s3][^s4][^s5]

## Connections

The feedback sandwich is a narrower technique under feedback in general.[^s1] It is meant to address defensiveness by making criticism easier to receive, though the evidence that it does so in a way that changes behaviour is weak.[^s1][^s2] Dohrenwend presents it as a supervisor's tool for routine employee performance feedback, which is why it is linked to performance reviews.[^s1] It is challenged by Parkes, Abercrombie and McCarty (2013), Henley and DiGennaro Reed (2015) and Bottini and Gillis (2021), and weakly supported by Prochazka, Ovcari and Durinik (2020).[^s2][^s3][^s4][^s5] Related techniques include Situation-Behavior-Impact, a more specific way to frame the corrective layer, and feedforward, which shifts the conversation to future suggestions; Legg and Sweeny's research on whether people want good or bad news first, and the cultural variation captured in direct versus indirect feedback, bear on when cushioning helps.[^s1][^s3]
