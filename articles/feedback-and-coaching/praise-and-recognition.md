steps:
  - Notice the specific behaviour worth repeating - an action you saw, not a trait such as "smart" or "a natural".
  - Say what the person did, when, and what it made possible for the team, client or project, in one or two sentences.
  - Deliver it soon after the work, in the channel the person values (a quiet one-to-one, a team thread, or a note copied to their manager).
  - Keep it clean - do not attach a correction, a "but", or a new request to the same message.
  - Make recognition a weekly habit across the whole team, and check who you have not recognized lately.
  - Thank people who help you outside their job, naming what their help changed, so they know it was wanted.
example:
  situation: A project manager recognizes an analyst who rebuilt a broken client data feed the night before a steering-committee meeting.
  before: "Great job everyone, thanks for all the hard work this week! You're all rock stars."
  after: "Sam - thank you for rebuilding the client data feed on Tuesday night. Because the numbers reconciled, the steering committee approved phase 2 in the meeting instead of deferring it a month. The validation checks you added are now our standard; I've told Dana how you handled it."
  why: The rewrite names the person, the action and its concrete consequence, so Sam knows exactly what to repeat and that it mattered; the blanket message gives no information and quietly lumps a standout effort with routine work.

# Praise and recognition

Praise and recognition means telling a colleague what they did well and why it mattered: a sentence in a one-to-one, a note in the team channel, a thank-you to someone who helped outside their role. In the workplace research it sits alongside money and corrective feedback as one of the main ways managers reinforce behaviour they want repeated.[^s1] It is also one of the most under-delivered: in Gallup's employee surveys fewer than one in three people strongly agree that they were recognized or praised for good work in the past seven days.[^s3]

## Origin

The modern evidence comes from organizational behaviour management, which treats social recognition, alongside pay and performance feedback, as a reinforcer of work behaviour. Alexander Stajkovic and Fred Luthans pulled this literature together in a 2003 meta-analysis in *Personnel Psychology*, covering 72 studies and about 13,300 employees.[^s1] Gallup made recognition one of its twelve engagement items, and Rodd Wagner and Jim Harter described it in *12: The Elements of Great Managing*, arguing that people need recognition at least weekly.[^s3] A parallel line of social-psychology research, led by Adam Grant and Francesca Gino, studied the simplest form of recognition, saying thank you.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Recognition changes behaviour. In the Stajkovic and Luthans meta-analysis, social recognition alone raised task performance by an average of about 17% (d = 0.51), more than feedback alone (about 10%) and somewhat less than money (about 23%); all three together produced the largest gain, about 45%.[^s1] Thanks also changes whether people help again. In Grant and Gino's 2010 experiments, people thanked for helping were roughly twice as likely to help a second time, and fundraisers who were thanked made over 50% more calls the following week.[^s2] The mechanism was feeling socially valued, not feeling more competent, which is why recognition matters even for confident high performers.[^s2] Gallup also reports that employees who feel insufficiently recognized are about twice as likely to say they will leave within a year, a correlational finding from its survey data.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Make praise carry information. A large 2020 meta-analysis of feedback found that feedback consisting only of reward or punishment had a small effect (d = 0.24), while high-information feedback - what was done, against what standard, and what next - had a very large one (d = 0.99).[^s5] "Great job" tells someone they are liked; "your validation checks caught the mismatch before the client saw it" tells them what to do again.[^s5] Name the action, the moment and its consequence, as in a positive version of a situation-behaviour-impact statement.[^s1][^s5]

Make it timely and frequent. Gallup's managers are advised not to rely on annual awards or an executive's all-hands mention, and not to treat silence as approval; small, spontaneous recognition close to the work is what employees report.[^s3] When someone helps you, thank them explicitly: people often hold back help because they are unsure it is wanted, and thanks resolves that doubt.[^s2]

Spread it across the team. The engagement item asks about the last seven days, so the practical test is whether each person on your team has heard specific recognition recently, not whether the team as a whole was thanked at the quarterly meeting.[^s3] Choose the channel the person values - a private word, a team thread, or a note copied to their manager - since the point is that they feel their contribution is valued.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

Generic praise ("you're all rock stars") feels nice but gives no information, so it is the weakest form of feedback.[^s5] Praise used only to cushion criticism teaches people to brace for the "but"; give recognition on its own and corrections separately.[^s5] Praising work that did not meet the standard reinforces the wrong behaviour, because recognition strengthens whatever it follows.[^s1][^s5]

Two popular rules outrun their evidence. The claim that teams need a precise ratio of positive to negative comments (often 3:1 or 5.6:1) traces to Fredrickson and Losada's positivity-ratio model; Brown, Sokal and Friedman showed in 2013 that its mathematics was unfounded and the claimed critical ratio had no basis.[^s4] Mostly-positive interaction is still sound advice, but there is no magic number.[^s3][^s4] Likewise, the classroom finding that praising intelligence harms persistence while praising effort helps did not replicate in three studies of 624 children by Li and Bates, which found no reliable effect of praise type.[^s6] Praising specific choices and strategies is still more useful, because it is more informative, not because of a proven mindset effect.[^s5][^s6]

## Evidence

The core effect is robust in organizations: recognition reliably improves performance across 72 workplace studies, and thanks reliably increases helping.[^s1][^s2] The evidence is weaker for specific claims about ratios, praise types and engagement payoffs; Gallup's productivity figures come from correlational survey data, not experiments.[^s3][^s4][^s6] Hence `mixed`: a real effect whose popular rules are overstated.

## Connections

Praise and recognition is a form of feedback, and the feedback meta-analysis by Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie explains why specific praise beats generic praise.[^s5] It is supported by Stajkovic and Luthans's meta-analysis of recognition as a reinforcer and by Grant and Gino's gratitude experiments, which also explain its effect through perceived social worth.[^s1][^s2] It is challenged by Brown, Sokal and Friedman's critique of the positivity ratio and by Li and Bates's failed replications of praise-type effects.[^s4][^s6] It differs from the feedback sandwich, which uses praise as packaging for criticism, and borrows the structure of situation-behaviour-impact feedback.[^s5] Regular one-to-ones and engagement efforts are natural places to practise it.[^s3]
