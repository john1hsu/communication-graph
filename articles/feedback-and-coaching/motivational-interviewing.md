steps:
  - Check the fit first - use it when someone is genuinely torn about a change that serves their own goals, not to win agreement to something that mainly serves you.
  - Engage before you steer - spend the opening listening and reflecting until the person feels understood, without advice or warnings.
  - Agree the focus together, naming the one change worth talking about and asking permission before you raise it.
  - Ask open questions about why the change might matter to them and how they could do it, and reflect back the reasons they give.
  - Affirm real strengths and efforts, and when they voice reasons not to change, reflect them neutrally rather than arguing back.
  - Offer information only with permission - ask what they know, share briefly, then ask what they make of it.
  - Summarise their own reasons for change, then ask what, if anything, they want to do next; let them set the plan and the pace.

  after: "I'd like to hear how the dashboard move looks from your side - what's appealing about it, and what's holding it back? ... So the manual exports are eating Friday afternoons, and you'd like those back, but you're worried the new tool will break the formulas your stakeholders rely on. What would need to be true for the switch to feel safe? ... Would a short test with one report be a sensible first step, or something else?"

# Motivational interviewing

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a way of talking with someone who is unsure about a change. Its founders define it as a collaborative, person-centred form of guiding that elicits and strengthens a person's own motivation for change.[^s2] Rather than supplying arguments, the helper listens for the person's own reasons, draws them out and reflects them back, so the case for change is made in the person's own words.[^s1] Practitioners describe four overlapping processes - engaging, focusing, evoking and, when the person is ready, planning - carried out with four core skills known as OARS: open questions, affirmations, reflections and summaries.[^s5]

## Origin

William R. Miller first described MI in 1983, after colleagues in Bergen, Norway, pressed him to explain how he worked with problem drinkers.[^s1] In 1989 he met Stephen Rollnick, who had found the approach spreading through UK addiction services, and the two wrote the first MI book in 1991; Rollnick later carried it into general health care.[^s1] Its relational side draws on Carl Rogers's view that accurate empathy, warmth and genuineness help people change, while its more directive side - deliberately strengthening talk in favour of change - is what Miller and Rollnick say separates it from purely client-centred counselling.[^s1][^s2] Jonathan Passmore later proposed combining MI with standard coaching skills for workplace coachees whose performance problems stem from low motivation.[^s6]

## Why it matters

When a manager argues for a change, the other person often argues back, and the more they voice the case against, the worse the outcome tends to be.[^s1][^s4] In an early comparison, people counselled in the MI style voiced about twice as much talk in favour of change, and half as much resistance, as people given a persuasive style.[^s1] Miller and Rose link this to self-perception theory: people come to believe what they hear themselves say, so a conversation in which the employee states their own reasons does more than one in which the manager states them.[^s1] That makes MI useful in one-on-ones and coaching about stalled development goals, new ways of working or habits a person half-wants to change.[^s6]

## How to apply it

Start by checking that the person is ambivalent; someone already committed needs help planning, not motivating, and MI is not a cure-all.[^s2] Spend the opening engaging - reflecting what you hear until they feel understood - then agree a focus together.[^s5] While evoking, listen for change talk - their own statements in favour of change - and reflect it, and meet sustain talk with neutral reflection rather than rebuttal.[^s1][^s5] Offer information as a two-way exchange - ask, offer, ask again - and avoid unsolicited advice, warnings or confrontation.[^s5] Close with a summary of their reasons and let them choose any next step.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Using it as a trick.** Miller and Rollnick are explicit that MI is not a way to get people to do what they do not want; they call it unethical to use for another party's interests, such as selling a product.[^s2] At work, where the organisation often has a stake, be open about your own position and reserve MI for changes that serve the employee's goals.[^s2]
- **Running a pros-and-cons exercise.** Weighing both sides equally is decisional balance, a different technique; MI selectively strengthens the person's reasons for change.[^s2]
- **Assuming a workshop is enough.** MI is simple but not easy, and practitioners' self-ratings often exceed their observed skill; in a trial of 140 clinicians, feedback and coaching after a two-day workshop improved proficiency, while self-study did not.[^s1][^s2]
- **Pushing through a script.** In one trial dataset, poor outcomes went with jagged commitment patterns that the authors tied to a manual requiring therapists to move on regardless of readiness.[^s1]

## Evidence

The evidence is real but mostly from health and addiction settings. A 2010 meta-analysis of 119 studies found MI produced small, durable effects compared with weak controls (average g = 0.28), but no significant advantage over other specific treatments (g = 0.09), with results depending on how and to whom it was delivered.[^s3] Effects also varied between sites and practitioners, and some trials found no benefit.[^s1] A 2018 meta-analysis of 36 primary studies found that MI-consistent skills were strongly associated with clients' change talk (r = .55) and that sustain talk predicted worse outcomes (r = .19), supporting the proposed mechanism.[^s4] Workplace applications such as Passmore's coaching model rest on that clinical base rather than on workplace trials, so for managers it is best treated as a promising, evidence-informed approach rather than a proven performance tool.[^s6][^s3]

## Connections

MI is a structured form of coaching and builds the coaching skill, especially with low-motivation coachees.[^s6] It was introduced by William R. Miller and Stephen Rollnick and influenced by Carl Rogers.[^s1] Self-perception theory helps explain why hearing one's own reasons matters, and MI addresses defensiveness by not arguing against it.[^s1] Its effects are supported by Lundahl and colleagues' 2010 meta-analysis and its mechanism by Magill and colleagues' 2018 process meta-analysis.[^s3][^s4] It requires active listening, and its core skills overlap open-ended questions and summarizing.[^s5]
