# Manager one-on-one meetings

A manager one-on-one (often written 1:1) is a deliberate, live conversation between a manager and a single direct report, held for work purposes and centred on what the direct report needs.[^s1] Meeting science treats it as distinct from email, chat and team meetings because it is synchronous and individual: the manager can respond to this one person's priorities, problems and mood in real time.[^s1] In practice it is the setting where much day-to-day feedback, coaching, recognition and expectation-setting actually happens.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Regular manager check-ins are an old management habit, long recommended in practitioner books and HR guidance, but they received little formal study until recently.[^s1] Organisational psychologist Steven G. Rogelberg of UNC Charlotte, who earlier wrote *The Surprising Science of Meetings* (2019), turned the meeting-science lens on them: with Jonathan Flinchum and colleagues he called 1:1s a new opportunity for meeting science in *Organizational Psychology Review* (2022), and he later published *Glad We Met: The Art and Science of 1:1 Meetings*.[^s5] Flinchum's 2022 dissertation, chaired by Rogelberg, defined the manager 1:1 as an intentional, synchronous meeting focused primarily on the direct report's work-related needs, and tested whether it drives engagement.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Flinchum surveyed 303 U.S. full-time employees at two points a week apart; 94% said they had 1:1s with their manager, and just over half met weekly or more.[^s1] What happened in the meeting mattered more than how often it happened: relations-oriented behaviour by the manager - listening, letting the person voice opinions, showing concern, giving deserved recognition - was the strongest route to engagement, working through employees' sense of autonomy, competence and relatedness.[^s1] Frequency still counted, but mainly as a floor of roughly monthly, and 1:1s added to engagement beyond what other manager contact provided.[^s1] Gallup reports a similar pattern from its survey of nearly 15,000 employees: those who had received meaningful feedback in the past week were far more likely to be engaged, yet only 16% called their last manager conversation extremely meaningful.[^s3] Rogelberg argues that 1:1s held too rarely or run badly leave people disconnected both in their work and emotionally.[^s2]

## What works here

Let the direct report own the agenda. Flinchum's review recommends that direct reports help build the agenda and that their items come first, with the manager's topics saved for the end; Rogelberg likewise says the best 1:1s are dominated by topics that matter to the direct report.[^s1][^s2] Keep the manager talking less: one university's guidance suggests aiming for the employee to do roughly 80% of the talking and starting with their agenda, since people take feedback better once they feel heard.[^s4]

Cover both the work and the relationship. Task-oriented moves (clarifying priorities, resolving issues, removing roadblocks) help, but the relations-oriented moves did more for engagement in Flinchum's data.[^s1] In Gallup's analysis the topics employees found most meaningful were recognition for recent work, collaboration and relationships, and current goals and priorities, while dwelling on weaknesses ranked lowest; regular 15-30 minute conversations beat longer ones.[^s3] Bring development in from time to time - roughly monthly - rather than every meeting.[^s1]

Protect the slot and agree the record. Book a recurring time, treat it as protected time in both diaries, and meet more often during onboarding, role changes or when there are concerns.[^s1][^s4] Keep proportionate notes of agreed actions, share them, and let the employee correct them.[^s4]

A workplace before/after: a team lead used her weekly 30 minutes to walk through her own project tracker line by line, and her analysts said little. She switched to a shared document where each analyst adds items before the meeting; she opens with "What's on your list?", ends with her own two items, and closes by reading back the agreed actions. The meeting now surfaces blockers she would not have asked about, because the agenda follows the analyst's priorities rather than hers.[^s1][^s2]

## Pitfalls

The commonest failure is turning the 1:1 into a status report the manager could have read in an update, which uses the only individual time on the manager's own priorities.[^s2] Others are letting meetings lapse below the roughly monthly floor, skipping relationship talk in favour of tasks, and filling the time with a manager monologue.[^s1][^s2] Rogelberg cautions that no single format suits everyone, so templates should flex to the person and the season.[^s2] Remote 1:1s keep the synchronous benefit only if both people are actually present on video or phone; swapping the meeting for chat loses what made it distinctive.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence base is young. Flinchum's study is a self-report survey drawn mostly from an online panel, with recall of meeting frequency over four weeks and a very small organisational sample, so it shows associations rather than causation.[^s1] Gallup's figures come from proprietary surveys that are not peer reviewed.[^s3] Taken together the findings are consistent - quality beats quantity, and the meeting should exist at least monthly - but effect sizes and the best cadence for different roles remain open, so the status here is mixed.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

1:1s are **supported by Flinchum (2022)** (`cm:flinchum-2022`), which linked their quality and regularity to **employee engagement** (`cm:employee-engagement`) and **explained** that link through **self-determination theory** (`cm:self-determination-theory`) - satisfying needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness.[^s1] The same study **contrasts** them with **team meetings** (`cm:team-meetings`) and written contact, showing a separate contribution.[^s1] They **complement feedback** (`cm:feedback`): Gallup's weekly meaningful-feedback finding describes exactly what a good 1:1 delivers.[^s3] They sit within **workplace meetings** and **interpersonal communication**, and draw on **active listening**, **humble inquiry** and **open-ended questions** for the relations-oriented behaviour the research favours, on **feedforward** for development talk, and on **psychological safety** for candour; from the report's side they are the main venue for **managing up**, and they feed the formal **performance review**.[^s1][^s2][^s4]
