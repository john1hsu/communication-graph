steps:
  - Pick one recurring conversation (for example, raising a missed deadline) and write four to six learning points as rules that say what to do and why.
  - Show a model handling it, ideally more than one, including a version that goes wrong, and discuss the learning points before or after the demonstration rather than over it.
  - Have each learner picture themselves doing it, then role-play scenarios drawn from their own job, swapping roles.
  - Give specific feedback and praise on each learning point, then run the practice again.
  - End with a concrete goal for using the skill at work in the next two weeks, brief the learners' managers, and check back on it.
example:
  situation: A new team lead has to raise a late report with an experienced analyst. Before training she had only read an article on feedback; afterwards she had practised the conversation in a modeling session.
  before: "You missed the deadline again. This really can't keep happening, OK?"
  after: "The board report was due Tuesday and came in Thursday, so I had to delay the pack. What got in the way? ... OK, so let's agree that you'll flag a slip by Monday noon. Can you do that?"
  why: She had watched the steps modeled, rehearsed them twice with feedback and set a goal to use them, so under pressure she named the specific behaviour and its impact, asked before judging and closed on an agreed action.

# Behavior modeling training

Behavior modeling training (BMT) teaches a skill by showing it and then having people do it. A typical session has five parts: a short set of clearly defined behaviours to learn, one or more models demonstrating them, practice, feedback and social reinforcement, and steps to carry the behaviour back to the job.[^s2] It is used for interpersonal skills such as conflict management, interviewing, assertive communication and cross-cultural management, as well as for technical skills.[^s2] For communicators its value is simple: reading about a good feedback conversation and being able to hold one under pressure are different things, and BMT is built to close that gap.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The method grew from Albert Bandura's social learning theory, which holds that people acquire much of their behaviour by watching others and seeing what happens to them, rather than by being persuaded with arguments.[^s3][^s4] Arnold Goldstein and Melvin Sorcher turned this into a four-step training model (modeling, role-play, social reinforcement, transfer) in 1974, and it quickly spread through industrial supervisory training.[^s3] A 1980 review for the US Bureau of Mines described the commercial "interaction modeling" version: small groups watched a roughly five-minute film of a supervisor handling a problem well, rehearsed it, were praised as their role-play approached the model, and went back to managers trained to support them.[^s4] Practitioner accounts date the approach to 1974 and say it had been delivered to millions of people by the mid-1990s; that figure comes from a vendor that sells it.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Communication advice is easy to agree with and hard to perform. A 2005 meta-analysis of 117 BMT studies (Taylor, Russ-Eft and Chan) found the largest gains in knowledge of what to do and how to do it (effect sizes around d = 1.0) but a much smaller average change in on-the-job behaviour (d = 0.27), with wide variation between programmes.[^s1] In other words, BMT reliably builds skill in the room, and whether that skill shows up at work depends on design and on the workplace.[^s1][^s2] An earlier 2003 meta-analysis of workplace training found that programmes combining cognitive and interpersonal skills were among the most effective.[^s1]

## How to apply it

The steps above follow the moderators identified in the meta-analysis:[^s2]

- **Write learning points as rules, not scripts.** Points that state the underlying rule ("describe the behaviour, not the person") transfer better than word-for-word lines that fit only one case.[^s2]
- **Time the commentary.** Presenting learning points during the model hurts learning; give them before or after.[^s2]
- **Use mixed models for flexible skills.** Showing both effective and flawed versions helps people use an adaptable interpersonal skill on the job, though it makes the rules a little harder to learn; when a procedure must be copied exactly, show only the correct version.[^s2]
- **Prepare and personalise practice.** Picturing yourself doing the behaviour improves skill gains, and when learners know the job well, scenarios they write themselves transfer better than trainer-written ones.[^s2]
- **Plan for transfer.** Specific on-the-job goals, rewards or consequences for using the skill (for example through appraisals) and training the learners' own managers all improve transfer.[^s2]

You can run a lightweight version without a course: a colleague models the conversation, you rehearse it twice with them, and they give feedback on each learning point.[^s3][^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping at the demonstration.** Watching without practice, feedback and praise drops the steps the model depends on.[^s3][^s4]
- **Only perfect models.** For skills that must adapt to people, a single polished video gives learners nothing to compare against.[^s2]
- **No transfer plan.** Without goals and a supportive manager, the large classroom gains shrink to modest change at work.[^s1][^s2]
- **Over-trusting vendor results.** Training providers report strong results, but they sell the method; check independent reviews.[^s1][^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The status is `mixed`. Gains in knowledge and skill are large and well replicated across more than a hundred studies, but effects on attitudes and job behaviour are modest and vary a lot by design, so the method is not a guarantee of changed behaviour.[^s1][^s2] Much of the evidence comes from supervisory and technical training rather than from specific communication tasks, and the strongest summaries rest on a single meta-analysis.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections
