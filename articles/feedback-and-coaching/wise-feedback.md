steps:
  - Make sure the standard is real and the work actually falls short of it; wise framing cannot rescue vague or unfair criticism.
  - Write specific, rigorous comments that say where the work misses the mark and what would fix it.
  - Add one or two sentences saying why you are giving this feedback - the bar is high because the work matters - and that you are confident this person can reach it.
  - Give a concrete chance to act on it, such as a revision, a second draft or a follow-up review date.
  - When the improved version arrives, review it against the same standard and say what changed, so your stated belief is backed by follow-through.

# Wise feedback

Wise feedback is critical feedback that comes with a stated reason: the giver holds high standards and is confident the recipient can meet them.[^s6] The point is not to soften the criticism but to remove doubt about why it is being given, so the recipient can treat it as useful information instead of a possible sign of bias or low regard.[^s1]

## Origin

The idea comes from Geoffrey Cohen, Claude Steele and Lee Ross, who in 1999 named the "mentor's dilemma": how to criticise someone's work in a way that improves it without damaging their motivation.[^s2] In two studies, Black students who received unbuffered criticism saw the evaluator as more biased and were less motivated than White students; when the criticism was paired with both an emphasis on high standards and assurance that the student could meet them, that gap disappeared.[^s2] The two-part framing beat cushioning criticism with praise and beat stating high standards alone.[^s2]

David Yeager, Cohen and colleagues tested the approach in classrooms and published the results in 2014. Seventh-graders got teacher-marked essays with either a neutral note or a short note saying the comments reflected the teacher's high expectations and belief that the student could reach them.[^s1] Stanford's SPARQ centre now summarises the result as advice to pair every critique with a stated reason built on high expectations.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Criticism is ambiguous: the recipient has to guess whether it reflects the work, the giver's view of their ability, or prejudice. Yeager and colleagues argue that this attributional ambiguity is strongest when someone has reason to expect bias, and that mistrust leads people to discount critical feedback rather than act on it.[^s1] The authors frame the dilemma as one shared by every mentor, coach and manager, which is why the idea has travelled into workplace feedback advice.[^s1] In the original studies the benefit was concentrated among those with the most reason for mistrust, so it matters most when giving feedback across differences of group, status or tenure.[^s1][^s6]

## How to apply it

University teaching guidance breaks the practice into three parts: restate the high standard and why it matters, express belief that the person can meet it, and give rigorous, actionable comments on where the work falls short.[^s5] Add a real opportunity to act, because Yeager and colleagues found that stating standards and assurance did not improve outcomes unless people also had a genuine chance to improve.[^s1] The same guidance recommends treating it as an ongoing pattern across many rounds of feedback rather than a one-off line.[^s5]

By channel: in writing, the framing sentence goes at the top of the markup, as in the original note on the essay.[^s1] Live, say it before the first critical point and repeat it when you agree next steps.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Using it as a magic phrase.** The authors expect the note to fail, or backfire, where feedback is sparse and bias pervasive; the words only work when the standards and the help are real.[^s1]
- **Praise instead of assurance.** Padding criticism with compliments did worse than the high-standards-plus-assurance framing in the original studies.[^s2]
- **High standards without belief.** Stating the bar alone was also weaker than pairing it with confidence in the person.[^s2]
- **No follow-up.** Feedback with no chance to revise leaves the assurance untested and, in the authors' view, may not convey respect.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The original findings are striking but rest on small samples. In Yeager's first study (44 students), about 71% of Black students revised their essay after the wise note versus 17% after the neutral one; effects for White students were positive but not significant, and the authors themselves flag low power.[^s1] A third study with 76 high-school students, which used a related attribution-retraining exercise, closed about 39% of a racial grade gap over a semester.[^s1]

Replications have been weaker. A 2024 conceptual replication with 94 university students found no effect of a wise feedback message and reported that students' trust was already high.[^s3] A 2025 working paper on teacher training in wise feedback found gains in teachers' awareness and confidence, but no detectable effect on students' AP exam scores in two randomised trials, and its main pilot had no control group.[^s4] No study opened here tested wise feedback with adult employees, so treat it as a sound, low-cost habit with a contested evidence base rather than a proven workplace intervention.[^s1][^s3][^s4]

## Connections

Wise feedback **addresses** attributional ambiguity (`cm:attributional-ambiguity`), the uncertainty about whether criticism reflects the work or bias that Yeager and colleagues place at the centre of the problem.[^s1] It is an **alternative to** the feedback sandwich (`cm:feedback-sandwich`), since Cohen, Steele and Ross found that cushioning with praise worked less well.[^s2] It was **introduced by** Geoffrey L. Cohen (`cm:geoffrey-l-cohen`) and **demonstrated by** Cohen, Steele and Ross's 1999 studies (`cm:cohen-steele-ross-1999`)[^s2] and the 2014 field experiments of Yeager and colleagues (`cm:yeager-et-al-2014`).[^s1] It is **challenged by** the 2024 conceptual replication of Troy, Moua and Van Boekel (`cm:troy-moua-van-boekel-2024`)[^s3] and is **studied in** social psychology (`cm:social-psychology`).[^s1] Like the receiver-side self-affirmation technique, it targets the threat that makes criticism hard to hear, but from the giver's side.[^s1]
