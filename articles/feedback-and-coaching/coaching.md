# Coaching

Coaching is helping someone get better at their work by drawing out their own thinking rather than handing them answers. In the version most relevant to managers, the coach asks more than tells, supports rather than judges, and enables development rather than dictating what to do; Herminia Ibarra and Anne Scoular describe it as an everyday part of managing, done by people inside the organisation rather than only by hired consultants.[^s4] Research on workplace coaching more broadly covers both external professional coaches and internal ones, typically in a series of one-to-one conversations aimed at the coachee's goals.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Workplace coaching spread from sport and humanistic psychology into business from the 1980s. Ibarra and Scoular trace the manager-as-coach idea to Sir John Whitmore, who framed coaching as unlocking people's potential to maximise their own performance, and to the GROW model he and colleagues developed in that decade.[^s4] Academic study followed the practice: Theeboom and colleagues noted in 2014 that coaching had become a popular management tool well before research on its effectiveness had caught up.[^s1]

## Why it matters

When work changes quickly, a manager cannot hold every answer, and command-and-control direction scales poorly; coaching builds people who can solve problems themselves.[^s4] A 2014 meta-analysis found significant positive effects of coaching in organisations on all five outcome groups it examined - performance and skills, well-being, coping, work attitudes and goal-directed self-regulation - with effect sizes (Hedges' g) from 0.43 for coping to 0.74 for self-regulation.[^s1] A 2016 meta-analysis of 17 workplace studies found an overall positive effect (δ = 0.36), smallest for skills, larger for attitudes and largest for individual results.[^s2]

## What good looks like

Ibarra and Scoular distinguish three styles. Directive coaching is telling - sharing expertise, much like mentoring - which transfers knowledge but can drain the other person's energy. Nondirective coaching is listening, asking and withholding judgment, which energises people but feels unnatural to many managers. Situational coaching moves between the two as the moment requires, and they advise learning the nondirective style first.[^s4] Good coaches also recognise when not to coach: if someone is working productively, leaving them alone is the right call.[^s4]

In practice, most coaching happens in short exchanges rather than formal sessions. A request for help can be met with one open question - what have you already considered? - before any advice is given.[^s4] Structured conversations often follow GROW: agree the goal for this conversation, establish the facts with what/when/where/who questions (avoiding "why", which tends to provoke self-justification), widen the options, then confirm the plan and check commitment.[^s4]

## How to practise it

- **Hold back the first answer.** When someone brings you a problem, ask one open question before saying anything else, and let the silence work.[^s4]
- **Check your neutrality.** Notice when you have already decided the answer and are using questions to lead them there; Ibarra and Scoular call this "tell and sell" and warn that leading questions make people defensive.[^s4]
- **End with their commitment.** Ask them to rate on a 1-10 scale how likely they are to act; the authors treat 8 or above as a likely follow-through and anything lower as a signal to revisit the options.[^s4]
- **Get outside ratings of yourself.** In one assessment of 3,761 executives cited by the authors, 24% rated their coaching skill well above how colleagues rated it, so ask your team rather than trusting your own impression.[^s4]
- **Build their confidence, not just their plan.** Self-efficacy is the mechanism with the most consistent support in the coaching literature, so help people notice what they did that worked.[^s3]

**Before/after (one-on-one).** A team member says a stakeholder keeps rejecting her designs. *Before:* "Send them to me first and I'll tell you what they'll object to." *After:* "What feedback have they given on the last two rounds? ... What do you think is really driving it? ... What could you try before the next review? ... How confident are you, one to ten, that you'll do that?" *Why:* the first answer fixes this week's problem but makes the manager the bottleneck; the second builds her own diagnosis and leaves her owning the next step, while the manager can still add an idea if she gets stuck.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The overall picture is positive but less certain than the headline effects suggest, so its status here is mixed. A 2016 systematic review of 52 studies concluded coaching is an effective development tool, yet found many small samples, heavy reliance on self-report (20 studies used it alone), only three studies with objective outcomes and few randomised controlled trials.[^s3] Jones and colleagues found that neither format (face-to-face, blended or online) nor number of sessions changed results, and that internal coaches produced larger effects than external ones - a finding the review authors caution rests on few studies.[^s2][^s3] Importantly for managers, the 2016 review excluded manager-as-coach studies because a supervisory relationship changes the dynamic, so most of this evidence comes from coaches without authority over the coachee.[^s3] Advice on how line managers should coach therefore rests more on practitioner experience than on direct trials.[^s4]

## Connections

Coaching is a form of [[interpersonal-communication]] and depends on [[active-listening]]: Ibarra and Scoular place listening and good questions at its core, and it pairs naturally with [[open-ended-questions]].[^s4] Its effects are supported by the meta-analyses of Theeboom and colleagues (2014) and Jones and colleagues (2016).[^s1][^s2] Grover and Furnham's coaching-psychology review points to self-efficacy as a key mechanism, with goal setting, the coaching relationship and openness to feedback also implicated.[^s3] The [[grow-model]] gives it a conversational structure; [[one-on-one-meetings]] are its most common setting; and it differs from [[feedback]] in that the coachee does most of the diagnosis. Ibarra and Scoular warn that treating a [[performance-review]] as a judgment session encourages people to hide mistakes, which links coaching to [[psychological-safety]].[^s4]
