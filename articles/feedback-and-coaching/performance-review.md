# Performance review

A performance review (also called a performance appraisal or appraisal interview) is the formal conversation in which a manager and an employee review the past period's work against agreed expectations, discuss the assessment that goes on record, and agree what comes next.[^s1][^s4] It is usually annual: U.S. federal rules, for example, set a 12-month appraisal period by default so that each employee gets a yearly rating of record.[^s4] It is a hard conversation because it tries to evaluate, justify a rating that may affect pay, give feedback and plan development at once - jobs that pull against each other.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

Formal appraisal is a long-standing fixture of organisational life, and industrial-organisational (I-O) psychologists have spent decades trying to improve it, with limited success; Pulakos and colleagues note that managers and employees still widely see the process as burdensome and low in value.[^s2] The U.S. Merit Systems Protection Board, which has reported on federal performance management since 1981, argued in 2015 that an annual rating had come to stand in for performance management itself, even though the rules already expected ongoing appraisal throughout the year.[^s4] In the mid-2010s several large employers began dropping numerical ratings, and the 2015 conference of the Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology staged a formal debate on whether that was wise; the debate was published in 2016.[^s3]

## Why it matters

The review is often the only scheduled moment when a manager's overall judgement of someone's work is said out loud and written down, so how it is communicated shapes whether the employee leaves understanding the assessment, accepting it and knowing what to do next.[^s1][^s4] A close study of 48 audiotaped annual appraisal interviews, coded into more than 32,000 behavioural events, found that the interaction itself predicted success: when supervisors made relation-oriented statements, employees contributed more actively, which in turn drew further relation-oriented responses, and interviews with more of this back-and-forth were rated more successful by both parties.[^s1] Frequency of feedback around the review matters too: in MSPB's 2007 federal survey, employees who got feedback weekly were far more likely to call it very helpful than those who got it less than once a year (about 57% versus 3%).[^s4]

## What works here

**Make it a dialogue, not a verdict.** The Meinecke study suggests the supervisor's job is to draw the employee in - asking for their view, acknowledging it, showing support - because active employee contribution was the route to a successful interview.[^s1] Open with the employee's own account of the period and use open questions before you share your assessment.[^s1][^s4]

**Prepare for disagreement.** Disagreement was the main way employees actively contributed in the recorded interviews, and it was linked to lower perceived supervisor support - so expect it, and respond by exploring the reasons rather than defending the rating.[^s1]

**Remove surprises.** Hold regular performance discussions during the year so the formal review summarises conversations already had; MSPB found that more frequent discussions went with employees believing their supervisor actually understood their work.[^s4] OPM's playbook suggests check-ins as often as every two weeks, immediate feedback after notable work, and a start-stop-continue structure for progress reviews.[^s5]

**Separate judging from developing.** MSPB stresses that rating performance is not the same as managing it; spend real time on forward-looking guidance - what to try next, drawing on what went well - rather than only documenting the past.[^s4]

**Write down what was agreed.** Record the key points of the discussion so both people share the same account of the outcome.[^s5]

A workplace before/after: a manager used to open each annual review by reading the rating and then listing three areas for improvement; employees went quiet or argued about one example. She now asks each person to summarise their year and the work they are proudest of, reflects back what she heard, then gives her assessment with specific examples, and ends with two goals they draft together. The rating is unchanged, but the employee now speaks first and is heard - the pattern the research links to success.[^s1][^s4]

## Pitfalls

Treating the review as the year's only feedback is the most common failure: surprises in the review usually mean the ongoing conversations did not happen.[^s4] Others include a one-way monologue in which the employee has little room to contribute, using the meeting mainly to document mistakes for possible formal action (MSPB notes this serves the large majority who meet expectations poorly), and letting the rating dominate the hour so development gets squeezed out.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The process-level evidence is fairly consistent - employee participation and relation-oriented supervisor behaviour go with better appraisal outcomes - but much of it is observational, from single organisations or self-report surveys, so it shows association more than cause.[^s1][^s4] The value of the rating itself is actively disputed. In the 2016 SIOP debate, critics argued that raters often disagree, ratings track actual performance weakly and serve conflicting purposes, while defenders replied that people are evaluated anyway, that differentiation has real benefits and that rating-free alternatives may be worse.[^s3] Pulakos and colleagues argue the fix lies less in redesigning forms than in building everyday behaviours - setting expectations, giving real-time feedback - that the annual event cannot replace.[^s2] Hence the status here is mixed.

## Connections

The review's communication dynamics are **demonstrated by Meinecke, Lehmann-Willenbrock and Kauffeld (2017)** (`cm:meinecke-lehmann-willenbrock-kauffeld-2017`), which showed how supervisor relation-oriented talk and employee contributions reinforce each other.[^s1] It **complements feedforward** (`cm:feedforward`), which MSPB recommends for the forward-looking part of performance conversations, and **contrasts with manager one-on-one meetings** (`cm:one-on-one-meetings`), the regular discussions MSPB says should carry most feedback rather than the annual event.[^s4] It is **challenged by Adler et al. (2016)** (`cm:adler-et-al-2016`), the published debate on whether to drop performance ratings.[^s3] OPM's guidance pairs ongoing progress reviews with a **start-stop-continue** structure (`cm:start-stop-continue`).[^s5] The review sits within **performance management** and **workplace meetings**, builds on the concept of **feedback**, and often becomes a **difficult conversation**, where **active listening**, **open-ended questions**, **psychological safety**, SBI-style specific examples and awareness of **defensiveness** do much of the work.[^s1][^s2][^s4]
