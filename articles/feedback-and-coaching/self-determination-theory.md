# Self-determination theory

Self-determination theory (SDT) is a broad theory of human motivation developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan. It claims that people have three basic psychological needs - autonomy (acting with a sense of choice), competence (feeling effective) and relatedness (feeling connected to others) - and that settings which satisfy them produce more durable motivation, better performance and greater well-being, while settings that thwart them do the opposite.[^s1][^s2] For managers, the theory turns everyday communication into a motivational lever: the same request or piece of feedback can feel like support or like pressure depending on how it is framed.[^s1]

## Origin

SDT grew out of Deci and Ryan's laboratory research on intrinsic and extrinsic motivation at the University of Rochester, set out in their 1985 book and summarised for a general psychology audience in a 2000 *American Psychologist* article.[^s1][^s2] It draws its needs from earlier work: competence from White (1959), autonomy from de Charms (1968) and relatedness from Baumeister and Leary (1995).[^s1] The theory was later extended from classrooms and clinics to work organisations, where it is now a mainstream framework in organisational psychology.[^s1][^s2]

A central idea is that motivation differs in quality, not just quantity. Extrinsic motivation runs along a continuum from external regulation (working for a reward or to avoid punishment), through introjected regulation (working out of guilt or pride), to identified and integrated regulation (working because you value the goal). The last two, together with intrinsic interest, form autonomous motivation; the first two form controlled motivation.[^s1]

## Why it matters

SDT explains why the way something is said at work matters as much as what is said. Deci and colleagues describe autonomy-supportive managers as those who acknowledge employees' perspectives, offer choices, explain the rationale for requests, give meaningful feedback and encourage initiative.[^s1] In a field experiment at a Fortune 500 company, managers trained in this style had employees who later reported higher job satisfaction and more trust in top management.[^s1]

The theory also draws a line between informational and controlling feedback. In Deci, Koestner and Ryan's meta-analysis, positive verbal feedback increased intrinsic motivation among adults, but praise worded to pressure people toward a standard, rather than to inform them, undermined it.[^s5] Expected tangible rewards tied to doing a task lowered later free-choice interest, and rewards that only top performers could win had the largest negative effect.[^s5]

A workplace illustration: a team lead needs a new analyst to adopt a revised reporting template. A controlling version says, "Use the new template from Monday - no exceptions." An SDT-informed version says, "From Monday we're moving to the new template so finance can consolidate figures in one pass. The structure is fixed, but you choose how to migrate your old reports; tell me if anything in it doesn't fit your accounts." The request is just as clear, but it gives a reason (supporting internalisation), real choice (autonomy) and an invitation to raise concerns (relatedness).[^s1][^s4]

## How to use it in feedback and coaching

The theory suggests a few practical checks, drawn from its authors' workplace recommendations:[^s1][^s4][^s5]

- Explain why a task or standard matters before saying what to do, so people can come to value it rather than merely comply.[^s1]
- Separate what is truly fixed from what the person can decide, and offer the real choices that remain.[^s1][^s4]
- Make feedback informational: describe the work and how to improve it, rather than praising or criticising in a way that signals you are judging or controlling.[^s5]
- Set tasks that stretch without overwhelming, and say what good progress looks like, to support competence.[^s1]
- Be cautious with contingent, competitive rewards for work people already find interesting.[^s5]

## Evidence

Workplace evidence for the needs is extensive but largely correlational. Van den Broeck and colleagues' 2016 meta-analysis of 99 studies (119 samples) examined autonomy, competence and relatedness satisfaction at work. As summarised by Deci and colleagues, need satisfaction predicted intrinsic motivation and well-being, but predicted negative outcomes less well.[^s3][^s1] Slemp and colleagues' 2018 meta-analysis of 72 studies (N = 32,870) found leader autonomy support correlated with autonomy need satisfaction (rho = .57), autonomous motivation (.38), job satisfaction (.56) and lower turnover intentions (-.40). It was unrelated to controlled motivation, and its link with performance was modest (.25), weaker when performance was rated by others (.15) than by employees themselves (.35).[^s4] The authors caution that most studies were cross-sectional and self-reported, so causal direction is uncertain and shared-method bias may inflate effects.[^s4]

The theory's claims about rewards are disputed. Cameron and Pierce (1994) and Eisenberger and Cameron (1996) argued that the reward-undermining effect was largely a myth. Deci, Koestner and Ryan reanalysed 128 experiments and argued those critiques rested on flawed procedures, finding undermining for expected tangible rewards (d = -0.36 on free-choice behaviour).[^s5] Gerhart and Fang (2015) argued that support for warnings about pay for performance is lacking in real workplaces, and that SDT itself has moved toward recognising positive roles for extrinsic rewards.[^s6] SDT's authors reject that critique and call for more longitudinal designs and objective measures.[^s1] Hence the rating of mixed: the link between need support and motivation and well-being is consistent, while causal claims at work and the reward findings remain contested.[^s1][^s4][^s6]

## Connections

SDT was **introduced by Edward L. Deci** (`cm:edward-l-deci`) and **Richard M. Ryan** (`cm:richard-m-ryan`), and is **studied in industrial-organizational psychology** (`cm:industrial-organizational-psychology`), where Deci, Olafsen and Ryan review its workplace research.[^s1] It is **supported by** Slemp and colleagues' 2018 meta-analysis of leader autonomy support (`cm:slemp-et-al-2018`), by Van den Broeck and colleagues' 2016 meta-analysis of need satisfaction at work (`cm:van-den-broeck-et-al-2016`), and by Deci, Koestner and Ryan's 1999 reward meta-analysis (`cm:deci-koestner-ryan-1999`).[^s4][^s3][^s5] It is **challenged by** Eisenberger and Cameron's 1996 critique of the undermining effect (`cm:eisenberger-cameron-1996`) and by Gerhart and Fang's 2015 review of pay for performance (`cm:gerhart-fang-2015`).[^s5][^s6] It sits under **work motivation** and explains **autonomy-supportive language**, why regular **one-on-one meetings** relate to **employee engagement**, and why informational **praise** helps while controlling praise can backfire - a point that parallels **feedback intervention theory**.[^s1][^s5]
