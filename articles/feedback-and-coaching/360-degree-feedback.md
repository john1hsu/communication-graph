steps:
  - Decide and announce the purpose before anyone rates - development only, or input to appraisal - and say who will see the report.
  - Agree a set of raters with the recipient and their manager, choosing people who see the work often enough to give examples; aim for several per group rather than two or three.
  - Use a short questionnaire of observable behaviours, and brief raters on what to rate and on common rating errors.
  - Keep peer and direct-report ratings anonymous and aggregated, and give the recipient the report with a trained coach or facilitator rather than by email.
  - Help the recipient pick one or two development areas - especially where their self-rating is well above others' - and write a specific action plan with dates.
  - Have the recipient thank raters, share the chosen focus and ask for suggestions, then check progress every few months and re-survey after about a year.
example:
  situation: A team lead receives her first 360 report, which shows that her direct reports rate her much lower than she rates herself on "involves the team in decisions".
  before: "Thanks all for doing my 360. I saw some low scores on decision-making - I think people didn't realise how many of those calls came from above me. Anyway, it was useful to see."
  after: "Thanks for the honest input on my 360. The clearest message was that you'd like more say in decisions that affect your work. For the next six months I'll bring open decisions to our Monday meeting before I settle them. If you have one idea for how I could do that well, I'd like to hear it - and I'll check back with you in March."
  why: The first reply explains the gap away, which tells raters their input will be argued with and discourages candour next time. The rewrite names one behaviour, commits to a visible change with a date, and invites suggestions and follow-up, which is what the research links to improvement.

  - Have I told my raters what I am working on and when I will check back with them?

# 360-degree feedback

360-degree feedback (also called multisource or multi-rater feedback) collects ratings of one person's behaviour from the people who work around them - usually their manager, peers and direct reports, sometimes customers - on the same questionnaire, and sets those ratings beside the person's own self-assessment.[^s2][^s5] Its value for communication is that it shows a gap a single manager's view cannot: how your listening, decision-making or clarity actually land with different audiences. Bracken, Rose and Church define a proper 360 as a questionnaire-based, behaviour-focused process that allows comparison across rater groups and over time and aims at lasting behaviour change; interviews or self-ratings alone do not count.[^s2]

## Origin

Ratings from several sources have a long history in personnel research, but the "360°" label is credited to consultants Mark Edwards and Ann Ewen, who coined it in the mid-1980s as an alternative to "multirater" and set out the first systematic treatment in their 1996 book.[^s2] According to a Center for Creative Leadership review, the practice went from almost unknown in the 1980s to widespread by 2000.[^s4] From the start the field split over purpose: the Center for Creative Leadership held that the data belong to the recipient and should be used only for development, while Edwards and Ewen favoured using it in appraisal; Bracken and colleagues report that use for performance management rose from 27% of surveyed companies in 2003 to 48% in 2013.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Most workplace feedback comes from one person, usually the boss, who sees only part of how someone communicates. Peers and direct reports see the meetings, emails and one-on-ones the manager never attends, so a 360 can reveal blind spots - for example a leader who believes she consults widely while her team experiences decisions handed down.[^s4][^s5] The process is also a large investment of many people's time, and done badly it can lower morale or performance, so how it is run matters as much as whether it is run.[^s3][^s5]

## How to apply it

Set expectations before the survey: tell recipients and raters what the process is for, who will see the results, and what will happen afterwards.[^s4] Let the recipient choose raters, ideally agreed with their manager, from people who see the work regularly and can cite examples; one practitioner-researcher recommends eight to ten raters, because two or three give an incomplete picture.[^s4][^s5] Brief raters on the behaviours being rated and on errors such as halo, keep peer and direct-report ratings anonymous and aggregated, and accept that the manager's own rating is usually identified.[^s4]

Deliver the report through a qualified coach or facilitator who helps the person interpret it and choose one or two development areas, then turn those into a specific, dated action plan.[^s4][^s5] The critical part comes afterwards: CCL's review notes that follow-through is where most efforts fail, and it recommends check-ins every few months with raters, the manager or a coach.[^s4] Going back to raters, sharing what you are working on and asking for suggestions, is the step most often linked to perceived change.[^s2][^s4]

## Common mistakes

The most damaging mistake is ambiguity about purpose: calling a process "development only" when results reach the manager or inform promotion decisions undermines trust in the data, and Bracken and colleagues urge organisations to state their real purpose at the outset.[^s2] Other common failures are too few raters, a report handed over with no coaching, and no follow-up, all of which can make a 360 backfire.[^s5] Recipients often argue with or explain away low scores; the research suggests the ability to accept and interpret critical feedback constructively is a precondition for change.[^s1][^s4] Finally, DeNisi and Kluger argue that feedback does not always improve performance and can even harm it, and that typical 360 systems have design features that make them less effective than they could be; they call for organisations to evaluate their programmes systematically rather than assume they work.[^s3]

## Evidence

The evidence supports modest, conditional benefits, so its status here is mixed. Smither, London and Reilly's 2005 meta-analysis of 24 longitudinal studies found that improvement in manager, peer and direct-report ratings after multisource feedback was generally small; they proposed that change is more likely when the feedback signals a clear need to change, the recipient reacts positively, believes change is feasible and sets goals.[^s1] In a one-year study of 5,335 managers at a global firm, sharing results with raters and asking for suggestions explained only a very small, though statistically significant, share of improvement.[^s6] CCL's review reports that the largest gains came from managers who initially received the most negative feedback or who rated themselves above their co-workers' view.[^s4] Bracken and colleagues caution that the studies vary so much in design that pooled results are hard to interpret, and that Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 finding - that about a third of feedback interventions lowered performance - is often cited about 360s even though it reviewed feedback in general.[^s2][^s5]

## Connections

The "360-degree" label and its first systematic treatment came from Mark Edwards and Ann Ewen, and the process is now widely applied in performance reviews as well as in development programmes.[^s2] It is supported, with small average effects, by Smither, London and Reilly's meta-analysis,[^s1] and challenged by DeNisi and Kluger's critique of how typical 360 systems are designed.[^s3] It complements coaching, because a skilled feedback giver helps the recipient interpret the report and build a plan.[^s4] It sits under feedback generally, draws on feedback intervention theory and the Kluger-DeNisi meta-analysis, and requires the skill of receiving feedback without defending against it.[^s1][^s3] It pairs with feedforward - asking raters for future-focused suggestions is a natural follow-up - and with one-on-one meetings, where progress on the chosen behaviours can be reviewed.[^s2][^s4]
