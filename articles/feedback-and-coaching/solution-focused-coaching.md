steps:
  - Let the person describe the problem briefly and acknowledge it; do not cut off problem talk they need, but do not dig into its history either.
  - "Ask what they want instead, in concrete terms: \"If this were sorted, what would you notice on a normal Tuesday?\" (a toned-down miracle question)."
  - "Ask for exceptions and past successes: \"When has this gone a bit better?\" and \"What did you do differently that time?\""
  - Help them turn what worked into one or two small, specific next steps they could take this week.
  - Close by checking commitment and agreeing when you will review progress, so the good feeling the questions create is converted into action.
example:
  situation: In a one-on-one, an analyst says stakeholder meetings keep running over and ending without decisions, and she is frustrated.
  before: "How long has this been happening? Why do you think they keep going off track? Who's the main culprit?"
  after: "That sounds draining. Picture a meeting next month that goes well - what's different about it? ... Has any recent meeting come close? ... What did you do in that one? ... Which of those could you repeat on Thursday?"
  why: The problem-focused questions keep her rehearsing causes and blame; the solution-focused version gets her to picture the outcome, find an exception she already produced, and leave with a step she owns.

# Solution-focused coaching

Solution-focused coaching helps someone make progress by asking about the future they want and the times things already work, rather than by analysing the origins and consequences of their problem. Where a problem-focused coach asks how long something has been a problem and how it started, a solution-focused coach asks what would be different if it were solved and when it has gone better before.[^s4] Its signature moves are the miracle question (imagine the problem has gone - what do you notice?), success questions about past occasions when the person handled something similar, and questions about exceptions, when the problem could have happened but did not.[^s4]

## Origin

The approach is adapted from solution-focused brief therapy, which Steve de Shazer described in the late 1980s, and which shifted attention from explaining a problem to building on what already works; its rule of thumb is that if something works, you do more of it.[^s4] Coaching psychologist Anthony Grant became one of its main researchers in workplace coaching, testing solution-focused against problem-focused coaching questions from 2010 onwards and writing a stepwise process for teaching and practising the principles.[^s2][^s1]

## Why it matters

Managers and technical experts tend to diagnose problems in detail, which feels rigorous but can leave the other person more discouraged than when they started. Grant notes that the principles are simple to state yet hard to apply consistently, and that people with deep technical expertise often struggle to shift from expert manager to leader-as-coach; a structured solution-focused process gives them something concrete to learn and practise.[^s1] In Grant and O'Connor's 2010 pilot (39 people received a problem-focused session, 35 a matched solution-focused one), both improved goal approach, but the solution-focused group improved significantly more and also gained positive affect and insight into the problem, which the problem-focused questions did not deliver.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Start by letting the person describe the problem; Solms and colleagues stress that problem talk can be cathartic and that coaches should not counter it with a rigid insistence on solutions.[^s4] Then move to the preferred future, exceptions and past successes, and finish by turning those into specific, attainable steps - feeling better is not the goal in itself.[^s3][^s4] In practice most coaching blends solution- and problem-focused questions, using the solution-focused ones deliberately at the stage where optimism and momentum are needed.[^s4] The approach sits comfortably inside a GROW conversation: the Goal and Options stages are natural places for solution-focused questions.

## Common mistakes

- **Forced positivity.** Jumping to "what's going well?" before the person has been heard reads as dismissive; acknowledge the problem first.[^s4]
- **Stopping at the good feeling.** A vivid picture of success without a concrete plan can substitute for action rather than fuel it; always close with a next step.[^s4][^s3]
- **Using it on problems people cannot control.** Solms and colleagues suggest benefits shrink when problems are complex, systemic or outside the person's control - those need escalation or a process change, not just better questions.[^s4]

## Evidence

The short-term effects are well replicated; the behavioural effects are not, so its status here is mixed. In a 2018 randomised study of 512 participants, problem-focused questions performed worst on every measure, solution-focused questions matched a positive-mood induction on affect, self-efficacy, goal approach and action steps, and combining the two did best - which the authors read as support for broaden-and-build theory but also as a warning that making people feel good is not enough.[^s3] A 2022 experiment with 183 Dutch medical residents and MD/PhD students found solution-focused questions raised positive affect and approach goal orientation, but did not replicate earlier gains in self-efficacy or action planning, and produced no difference in goal attainment or in actually visiting a recommended resource over the following days.[^s4] Most studies use single sessions or written self-coaching exercises rather than live, repeated coaching by a manager, so how far the effects carry into real workplace relationships is still uncertain.[^s4]

## Connections

Solution-focused coaching is one way to practise and develop [[coaching]]: Grant's stepwise process is written for leaders, managers and HR professionals learning to coach.[^s1] It is a form of [[questioning]] that builds on [[open-ended-questions]], and it grew out of solution-focused brief therapy.[^s4] It contrasts with problem-focused questioning, which the 2010 and 2018 Grant and O'Connor studies used as the comparison condition.[^s2][^s3] Broaden-and-build theory is the mechanism its 2018 test points to, while the 2022 Solms study tempers its claims about behaviour change.[^s3][^s4] It pairs with the [[grow-model]] and fits [[one-on-one-meetings]]; like [[feedforward]], it directs attention to the future rather than to past mistakes.
