# Feedback intervention theory

Feedback intervention theory (FIT) is Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi's account of why the same act, telling someone how they did, can raise performance in one case and lower it in another. Its central claim is that feedback works by moving the receiver's attention, and that it helps when it keeps attention on the work and hurts as it draws attention toward the person's self-image.[^s1] For anyone who gives feedback at work, it turns a vague rule ("be constructive") into a testable question: after hearing this, what will the other person be thinking about?[^s2]

## Origin

Kluger (Hebrew University of Jerusalem) and DeNisi (Rutgers) proposed the theory in their 1996 *Psychological Bulletin* review. Their meta-analysis of 607 effects found an average gain from feedback of d = 0.41, yet more than a third of interventions made performance worse, and neither sampling error, the sign of the feedback nor existing theories explained the spread.[^s1] They called FIT "preliminary" and built it from control theory, in which people regulate behaviour by comparing feedback with a goal and acting on the gap.[^s2]

The theory rests on five assumptions: behaviour is regulated by comparing feedback with standards; those standards are arranged in a hierarchy; only the gaps that get attention drive behaviour; attention normally sits in the middle of the hierarchy; and feedback shifts where attention goes.[^s6] The hierarchy has three levels: task learning (the details and strategies of the task), task motivation (the overall effort to meet the standard), and meta-task processes (thoughts about the self, such as "what does this say about me?").[^s1][^s6]

## Why it matters

FIT predicts that feedback cues pulling attention upward to the self drain the mental resources the task needs and trigger emotional reactions that interfere with it. That is why, in the 1996 data, feedback carrying praise or discouraging criticism had weaker effects than feedback without such self-cues, and feedback given in self-threatening settings did worse.[^s2] Hattie and Timperley's widely used education model draws directly on this work, citing Kluger and DeNisi's finding that praise had almost no effect (0.09) and that self-threatening feedback (0.08) did far worse than low-threat feedback (0.47); their model accordingly ranks feedback about the self as the least effective of its four levels.[^s3]

The theory also explains why pairing feedback with a clear goal helps: a goal anchors attention to the task gap rather than to the receiver's standing.[^s2] It predicts larger benefits on familiar or simple tasks, where some self-focus costs little, and smaller or negative effects on complex, novel tasks that need every bit of working memory.[^s2]

A workplace illustration: a manager reviewing a junior analyst's first client model says, "Honestly, I expected more from you - this isn't senior-level work." The comment is about the analyst, so FIT predicts attention jumps to the meta-task level (worry, impression management) and the next draft may not improve. A FIT-informed version would say, "The revenue tab double-counts renewals in rows 40-55; split new and renewal bookings, and let's aim to have it reconciled by Thursday." It names a task detail, offers a strategy and sets a goal, keeping attention low in the hierarchy.[^s1][^s2]

## How to use it when giving feedback

FIT is a theory, not a script, but it yields practical checks drawn from its authors' recommendations:[^s2][^s5]

- Agree the goal or standard before giving feedback, so the gap you describe is about the work.[^s2]
- Describe the task and a next step; leave out ratings against colleagues and verdicts on the person.[^s1][^s3]
- Expect self-threat to cost more on new or complex tasks, and keep feedback narrower there.[^s2]
- Treat any feedback system, such as a review or 360 process, as an intervention to evaluate, not one that helps by default.[^s2][^s5]

## Evidence

The pattern FIT was built to explain has held up. A 2020 meta-analysis of 435 educational studies (994 effects) again found a positive average (d = 0.48 after removing outliers), very high heterogeneity and 17% negative effects; feedback rich in task and process information (d = 0.99) far outperformed rewards or punishments alone (d = 0.24), consistent with FIT's task-versus-self logic.[^s4] That meta-analysis did not, however, test the attention mechanism itself.[^s4]

Direct tests are scarcer and mixed. In a 1998 Virginia Tech experiment with 144 students on a typing task, person-delivered and negative feedback did raise self-reported self-focused (meta-task) thinking as predicted, but the predicted task-motivation effect was not found, and performance effects depended on typing ability: experimenter praise was linked to slower typing for the more able typists but to the best results for the least able.[^s6] The original moderator analyses were correlational, and the authors themselves noted that task types were poorly theorised and that how direction and size of the feedback gap combine needed more work.[^s2]

Later work has refined rather than replaced the theory. Kluger and Van Dijk argued that positive and negative feedback have similar effects on average because their impact depends on regulatory focus: positive feedback helps people pursuing gains (promotion focus) and negative feedback helps those guarding against losses (prevention focus). They also proposed the feedforward interview as an alternative where feedback is likely to threaten the self.[^s5] Hence the rating of mixed: the outcome pattern is robust, but the specific attention hierarchy is only partly confirmed.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

FIT was **introduced by Avraham N. Kluger** (`cm:avraham-n-kluger`) and **Angelo DeNisi** (`cm:angelo-denisi`) and is **demonstrated by** their 1996 meta-analysis (`cm:kluger-denisi-1996`), whose unexplained negative effects it was proposed to account for.[^s1] It is **influenced by control theory** (`cm:control-theory`), from which it takes the feedback-standard comparison.[^s2] It is **supported by Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie's 2020 meta-analysis** (`cm:wisniewski-zierer-hattie-2020`), which found information-rich feedback far more effective than rewards or punishment.[^s4] It **complements feedforward** (`cm:feedforward`), which Kluger and Van Dijk offered as a lower-threat alternative, and **regulatory focus theory** (`cm:regulatory-focus-theory`), which they used to explain when positive versus negative feedback helps.[^s5] It sits under **feedback**, explains why the **feedback sandwich** and generic **praise** add little task information, and underlies Hattie and Timperley's model and task-focused techniques such as **Situation-Behavior-Impact**, all of which matter in **performance reviews** and for **receiving feedback** well.[^s3][^s2]
