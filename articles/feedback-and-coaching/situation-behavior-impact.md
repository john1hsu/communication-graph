steps:
  - Pin down the situation - the specific meeting, document or moment, with a time and place ("in Tuesday's 10 a.m. client call"), not "lately" or "last week".
  - Describe the behaviour as something a camera could have recorded - words said, actions taken - and strip out labels such as "rude", "lazy" or "great attitude".
  - State the impact the behaviour had on you, the team or the work, in your own voice, naming a concrete consequence and, if useful, your reaction (frustrated, relieved, proud).
  - For redirecting feedback, stop and ask about intent ("What were you aiming for there?") and listen before proposing any fix.
  - Agree together on what to keep doing or what to try next time, and give the feedback close to the event while it is still fresh.
  - Use the same three parts for positive feedback, so the person knows exactly which behaviour to repeat.
example:
  situation: A team lead gives feedback to an analyst who presented the quarterly numbers at a leadership review.
  before: "You came across as really unprepared today and kind of defensive. You need to step up your game before the next one."
  after: "In this morning's quarterly review with the VPs, when Priya asked where the churn figure came from, you said 'I'd have to check' and moved on. The VPs then questioned two other numbers and we lost ten minutes of the agenda, and I was worried they'd discount the whole forecast. What was going on for you at that point?"

# Situation-Behavior-Impact

Situation-Behavior-Impact (SBI) is a short script for giving feedback: say where and when it happened, describe what the person did, and explain the effect it had on you.[^s2] Its purpose is to replace personal attacks, sweeping judgements and vague comments with direct, objective remarks about actions, so the recipient knows what to keep doing or what to change.[^s6] A later extension, SBII, adds a fourth step - asking about the person's intent - which turns a one-way message into a two-way conversation.[^s1]

## Origin

SBI was developed by the Center for Creative Leadership (CCL), a leadership-development non-profit that says it teaches the technique to thousands of leaders a year.[^s6] It is set out in CCL's short guidebook *Feedback That Works* by Sloan R. Weitzel, which builds effective feedback around three ideas: anchor it in the situation, describe the behaviour you observed, and name the impact on you.[^s2] CCL now presents the intent step as a way to begin coaching, well before any formal review or disciplinary conversation, crediting research by its staff psychologists Bill Gentry and Stephen Young.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Feedback is powerful but unreliable. A 1996 meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes found that feedback improved performance on average (d = 0.41), yet more than a third of feedback interventions made performance worse; the authors argued that feedback loses power as it draws attention away from the task and toward the self.[^s4] A 2020 meta-analysis of 435 education studies reached a compatible conclusion: feedback was more effective the more information it carried, with high-information feedback (d = 0.99) far outperforming bare rewards or punishments (d = 0.24).[^s5] SBI is a practical way to keep workplace feedback on that side of the line - specific, informative and about the task rather than the person's character - and CCL reports that using it lowers the giver's anxiety and the receiver's defensiveness.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Name the situation precisely: "this morning at the 11 a.m. team meeting" works, while "last week" invites confusion about which moment you mean.[^s1] Describe the behaviour as facts - "you interrupted me while I was presenting the budget" rather than "you were rude" - because labels provoke defensiveness and observable actions can be discussed.[^s1] State the impact in your own terms, using words such as frustrated or worried for problems and proud or relieved for good work; then, if the behaviour needs to change, ask what the person was trying to achieve and listen before suggesting a fix.[^s1] Give redirecting feedback soon after the event, while both of you can recall it accurately.[^s3] The same structure works for praise: telling someone exactly which action helped is more useful than a generic "great job".[^s2][^s3]

In writing - a chat message or email - the three parts keep feedback concrete, but intent cannot be explored easily, so it is usually better to give redirecting SBI feedback live and use text for brief, positive SBI notes. With senior stakeholders, the impact step carries the weight: tie it to an outcome they care about (time, risk, a client relationship) rather than to your own feelings alone.[^s1][^s3]

## Common mistakes

CCL's list of feedback mistakes reads like a checklist of what SBI guards against: judging the person instead of the action, vague or clichéd praise and criticism, words like "always" and "never", guessing at motives, piling on examples, sarcasm, and dressing feedback up as a question.[^s3] CCL also advises against the feedback sandwich - wrapping criticism between two compliments - because recipients learn to tune the praise out.[^s3] A subtler mistake is treating the impact step as a verdict on intent: the behaviour may have had a bad effect for reasons you have not considered, which is why the intent question exists.[^s1]

## Evidence

SBI itself has little independent testing: CCL's claims about reduced anxiety and defensiveness are reported without published data or methods, so its status here is practitioner.[^s1] Its core ingredients are, however, consistent with the broader research: feedback aimed at the task rather than the self, and feedback rich in specific information, tend to work better.[^s4][^s5] Those meta-analyses cover many settings (the 2020 one is mostly classroom learning) and show large variation between studies, so they support the logic of SBI rather than prove its effect in workplaces.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

SBI addresses defensiveness by replacing judgements with observable facts, and complements active listening, which the intent step depends on.[^s1] CCL offers it as an alternative to the feedback sandwich, and it is applied in performance reviews and the everyday coaching conversations that should precede them.[^s1][^s3] It was set out in CCL's guidebook *Feedback That Works*.[^s2] It is supported, indirectly, by Kluger and DeNisi's (1996) feedback-intervention meta-analysis and by Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie's (2020) educational feedback meta-analysis.[^s4][^s5] It sits under feedback in general and alongside I-statements, nonviolent communication and the choice between direct and indirect feedback, all of which ask the speaker to describe behaviour and its effects instead of judging the person.
