# Receiving feedback

Receiving feedback is the skill of taking in someone's view of your work, understanding what they actually mean, and then choosing what to act on.[^s2] It covers both the reactive part (hearing criticism without shutting down) and the proactive part that researchers call feedback-seeking behavior: deliberately asking for, or watching for, information about how you are doing.[^s1] Effective feedback depends on the receiver as well as the giver, so this skill gives the receiver a real share of responsibility for whether the exchange produces learning.[^s5]

## Origin

The research thread began in organizational psychology. Susan Ashford and Cummings's 1983 paper framed feedback as a resource that employees actively create rather than wait for, and distinguished two ways of getting it: inquiry (asking directly) and monitoring (reading cues from others' behavior).[^s1] Three decades later, Frederik Anseel and colleagues pooled this literature in a 2015 meta-analysis and found strong support for its central idea, that people seek feedback when the perceived benefits outweigh the costs, such as looking incompetent.[^s1]

The practitioner version was popularized by Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen, Harvard Law School lecturers, in their 2014 book *Thanks for the Feedback*.[^s2] Their contribution was to name why receivers reject useful input: three "triggers" that fire before the content has been understood.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Feedback only helps if the receiver understands it and decides to use it, and the receiver is the one person present in every feedback conversation.[^s5] Stone and Heen describe a tension between two needs: wanting to learn and grow, and wanting to be accepted as you are now. Feedback threatens the second, so people push away information that would serve the first.[^s2] The cost of seeking also shapes behavior: in Anseel and colleagues' meta-analysis, perceived cost was negatively related to directly asking for feedback, and longer-tenured and older employees sought less of it.[^s1] One implication is that people tend to ask less as they become more established, so seeking input has to become a deliberate habit.[^s1]

## What good looks like

A good receiver recognizes which trigger is firing. Stone and Heen identify truth triggers (the feedback seems wrong or unfair), relationship triggers (the reaction is really about who is giving it) and identity triggers (it clashes with how you see yourself, and people differ in how strongly they react).[^s2] Each calls for a different move: understand before evaluating the truth of it, separate the "what" from the "who", and keep the stakes for your identity in proportion.[^s5]

A good receiver also turns vague labels into specifics. Feedback such as "be more proactive" or "work on your people skills" can mean many things; the skilled response is to ask what the person observed and what, concretely, they would like to see instead.[^s2] Finally, a good receiver asks well. In a preregistered field experiment covering 27,432 comments and two lab studies with 806 participants, Blunden and colleagues found that asking for "advice" rather than "feedback" produced more concrete, actionable input, because it pushed reviewers to think about the future.[^s3] The effect was strongest for improvement suggestions and weaker for comments on strengths.[^s4]

A workplace before and after: after a project review, an analyst hears "the deck felt unfocused." Before, she replies that the client asked for all twelve sections and moves on, a truth trigger rejecting the comment. After, she asks which slides lost the thread for him and what he would cut next time; she learns that the recommendation did not appear until slide nine, a fix she can make. The second response keeps the conversation open long enough to find the usable part.[^s2][^s5]

## How to practise it

- **Ask for one thing.** Instead of "any feedback?", ask "what's one thing I could do better on the next one?", which invites a concrete answer.[^s2]
- **Frame requests as advice.** In 360 reviews, self-reviews and project debriefs, ask colleagues what they would suggest you do differently going forward.[^s3][^s4]
- **Locate where it came from and where it leads.** Ask what they saw, then what specifically they recommend.[^s2]
- **Name the trigger to yourself.** Notice whether you are reacting to the content, the giver or your self-image before you reply.[^s5]
- **Structure the conversation.** Open by saying what kind of input you want, listen and acknowledge in the middle, and close by confirming any next step or follow-up.[^s5]
- **Seek it out regularly.** Use one-on-ones and after-action moments to ask, rather than waiting for the annual review, especially as you gain seniority.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but modest. Anseel and colleagues' 2015 meta-analysis, drawing on 69 articles, found the overall link between feedback seeking and job performance was small (a corrected correlation of about .07 across 11 samples), though directly asking was more positively related to performance than monitoring.[^s1] Seeking feedback is thus a means, not a guarantee: what you do with the answer matters. The advice-framing studies measured the quality of input received, not whether employees later adopted it or improved, and the authors note cultural differences may change how such requests land.[^s4] Stone and Heen's trigger model is practitioner guidance drawn from negotiation and coaching experience rather than a tested theory.[^s2]

## Connections

Receiving feedback is the receiver's half of feedback in general, and its main job is to **address** defensiveness (`cm:defensiveness`), the reflexive rejection that Stone and Heen's truth, relationship and identity triggers describe.[^s2] The skill was **introduced by** Sheila Heen (`cm:sheila-heen`) and Douglas Stone (`cm:douglas-stone`) as a practitioner framework, and is **influenced by** Susan Ashford (`cm:susan-j-ashford`), whose 1983 work with Cummings launched feedback-seeking research.[^s1][^s2] Anseel and colleagues' meta-analysis (`cm:anseel-et-al-2015`) **demonstrates** its antecedents and its small link with performance, and is part of **industrial-organizational psychology** (`cm:industrial-organizational-psychology`).[^s1] It is **supported by** Blunden and colleagues' advice-versus-feedback experiments (`cm:blunden-et-al-2025`) and **applied in** performance reviews (`cm:performance-review`), where the 360 field experiment took place.[^s3][^s4] The future focus of advice framing echoes feedforward, and the questioning moves draw on active listening and open-ended questions.[^s3][^s2]
