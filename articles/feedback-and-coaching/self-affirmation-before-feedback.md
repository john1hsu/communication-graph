steps:
  - Before a review, debrief or round of comments, set aside five minutes somewhere you will not be interrupted.
  - Pick one personal value that has nothing to do with the work being judged, such as family, friendship, craft outside work or community.
  - Write for about five minutes about why that value matters to you and a recent time you acted on it; do not write about the project.
  - Go into the conversation and, when the critical point lands, ask what they observed and what they suggest before you defend or explain.
  - Afterwards, list the one or two changes you will try, separating them from the points you still disagree with.
example:
  situation: A product manager is about to hear a director's comments on a launch that slipped two weeks.

  after: "Beforehand she writes for five minutes about why mentoring two junior colleagues matters to her. When the director says the risk log was stale, she asks which risks were missed and agrees to a weekly review, noting the vendor issue separately."

# Self-affirmation before feedback

Self-affirmation before feedback is a brief, deliberate pause in which you reflect on a core personal value just before you hear criticism or look at your own mistakes. It borrows a standard social-psychology intervention, usually a short essay about why a chosen value matters, and uses it to make threatening input easier to absorb.[^s1] The aim is not to feel good about the work under review but to remind yourself that your worth rests on more than this one piece of it.[^s4]

## Origin

The exercise comes from self-affirmation research, which Geoffrey Cohen and David Sherman reviewed in the *Annual Review of Psychology* in 2014. Their account starts from the idea that people need to see themselves as adequate; threats to that view trigger stress and self-protective defenses, and affirming values can blunt both.[^s1] Clayton Critcher and David Dunning later argued, in a 2015 paper, that affirmations work by widening the self-concept: under threat people develop a kind of tunnel vision, and affirming an unrelated value restores the wider picture.[^s4]

The feedback application is clearest in a 2012 *Psychological Science* study by Lisa Legault, Timour Al-Khindi and Michael Inzlicht. Thirty-eight students either wrote for five minutes about their top-ranked value or about why it was unimportant, then did a speeded task that flashed negative feedback after each error.[^s2] Affirmed students made fewer errors and showed a larger brain response to their mistakes, which the authors read as greater receptiveness to error feedback rather than avoidance of it.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Much of what makes feedback hard to hear is the threat it seems to pose to who you are, which is the core of defensiveness.[^s1] When that threat narrows your attention, your sense of worth temporarily hinges on the domain being judged, so a comment about one deck can feel like a verdict on your competence.[^s4] Reducing that threat gives the content of the feedback a better chance of getting through, which is the receiver's half of any feedback conversation.[^s2] It is also something a receiver can do alone, without waiting for the giver to phrase things well.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Use it before predictable moments of threat: a performance review, a design critique, a post-incident review or reading comments on a draft.[^s1] Choose a value outside the domain being judged; the broadening account predicts, and its authors report confirming, that out-of-domain affirmations work better than reassurance about the threatened skill.[^s4] Keep it short and concrete, as in the lab version, which was a ranking of values followed by five minutes of writing.[^s2] Then pair it with good receiving behaviour: ask what was observed and what is suggested before you explain.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

- **Affirming the work instead of yourself.** Telling yourself "my plan was solid" is defending the threatened domain, not broadening the self.[^s4]
- **Treating it as a magic fix.** Critcher compares affirmations to stepping back to see the whole table rather than removing the stain; the feedback still has to be dealt with.[^s4]
- **Doing it after the blow lands.** The interventions reviewed are designed to be timely, placed before the threatening encounter.[^s1]
- **Making it a public ritual.** Most evidence comes from private writing exercises, so asking a team to affirm aloud before a review goes beyond what has been tested.[^s1]
- **Using it to excuse unfair feedback.** It lowers your defensiveness; it does not make vague or biased criticism accurate.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but modest and uneven. A 2015 meta-analysis of 144 experimental tests in health communication by Tracy Epton and colleagues found small but reliable positive effects on accepting threatening messages, intentions and later behaviour.[^s3] The random-effects estimates were about d = .17 for message acceptance, .14 for intentions and .32 for behaviour.[^s6] Cohen and Sherman report that well-timed affirmations have sometimes had effects lasting months, but they tie this to settings where an early boost starts a self-reinforcing cycle.[^s1]

Replications have not always succeeded. A 2021 replication at the University of Illinois, closely following a values-affirmation study that had narrowed a gender gap in introductory physics, did not reproduce the positive results; its authors concluded the exercise is not understood well enough to count as a reliable intervention.[^s5] The direct feedback evidence is thin: the Legault study had 38 participants and measured reactions to task errors, not workplace reviews.[^s2] No study opened here tested the exercise before real performance conversations, so treat it as a low-cost experiment for yourself rather than a proven workplace tool.[^s2][^s5]

## Connections

This technique **develops** receiving feedback (`cm:receiving-feedback`), because the Legault study found affirmed participants more responsive to error feedback,[^s2] and it **addresses** defensiveness (`cm:defensiveness`), the self-protective reaction Cohen and Sherman describe affirmations as offsetting.[^s1] It is **explained by** self-affirmation theory (`cm:self-affirmation-theory`), reviewed in the same paper,[^s1] and **demonstrated by** Legault, Al-Khindi and Inzlicht's 2012 study (`cm:legault-al-khindi-inzlicht-2012`).[^s2] It is **supported by** the 2015 meta-analysis of Epton and colleagues (`cm:epton-et-al-2015`)[^s3] and **challenged by** the 2021 Illinois replication (`cm:values-affirmation-illinois-replication-2021`).[^s5] It is **studied in** social psychology (`cm:social-psychology`), the field of the Critcher and Dunning work.[^s4] Like self-distancing and anxiety reappraisal, it is a self-regulation move a person makes before a threatening conversation.
