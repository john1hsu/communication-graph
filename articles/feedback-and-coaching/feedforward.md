steps:
  - Pick one specific behaviour you want to get better at, phrased as a future goal ("run tighter project kick-offs"), not a past failure.
  - Describe that goal briefly to a colleague who has seen you work or who knows the subject - they do not need to be your manager.
  - Ask for two concrete suggestions for the future, and make clear you are not asking for a review of anything that already happened.
  - Listen and write the suggestions down without judging, explaining or defending - the only permitted reply is "thank you".
  - Offer to return the favour - ask what they want to improve and give them two forward-looking suggestions in turn.
  - Repeat with a few different people, then choose the suggestions you will actually try and tell those colleagues what you changed.
example:
  situation: A project manager whose kick-off meetings regularly overrun wants to improve before the next quarter's projects start.
  before: "Can you give me some feedback on last week's kick-off? I know it ran 40 minutes over, but the client kept raising scope questions, so honestly I'm not sure what I could have done differently."
  after: "I'm working on running kick-offs that finish on time. Could you give me two ideas I could try at the next one? I'll just note them down - no need to go back over last week."
  why: The first version invites a debate about a past meeting and opens with a defence, so the colleague has to argue to be helpful; the rewrite names a future goal, asks for a bounded number of ideas and promises not to argue, which makes it easy to offer useful suggestions.
selfCheck:
  - Have I named one behaviour I want to improve in the future, rather than asking for a verdict on the past?
  - When a suggestion arrived, did I say "thank you" - or did I explain why it would not work?
  - Am I using feedforward to avoid a conversation about a real problem that still needs to be discussed?
  - Which of the suggestions will I actually try, and have I told the people who gave them?

# Feedforward

Feedforward is a way of getting help to improve that looks only forward. You choose one behaviour you want to change, ask a colleague for a couple of suggestions for the future, and accept them with thanks instead of discussing what went wrong last time.[^s1] It is not a replacement for telling someone about a specific problem, but it is a low-threat way to collect ideas, build a development habit, and keep improvement conversations from turning into arguments about the past.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The technique is most closely associated with executive coach Marshall Goldsmith, who describes it as an exercise in his leadership workshops: participants pair up for about two minutes, each names a behaviour they want to change, asks for two suggestions, listens without critique, says thank you, and then swaps roles before moving on to a new partner.[^s1] Goldsmith reports running the exercise with more than 30,000 leaders, though this is his own observational account rather than a controlled study.[^s1]

A separate, more structured method shares the name. In 2010 organisational psychologists Avraham Kluger and Dina Nir proposed the Feedforward Interview, a manager-employee conversation built on appreciative inquiry, which they suggested could complement or replace the traditional performance appraisal.[^s2] In that version the manager asks the employee to recall a recent success, what conditions made it possible, and what could be done to recreate those conditions before the next review.[^s5] The two methods share a forward focus but differ in form: Goldsmith's is a quick peer exchange of suggestions, while the interview is a guided appraisal conversation.

## Why it matters

Conversations about past performance often go badly. In a 2020 set of three studies, managers who received mixed or negative feedback tended to doubt both its accuracy and the giver's qualifications, and disagreement about past performance actually grew over the course of the discussion, driven by self-protective explanations.[^s3] Recipients were more motivated to improve when they saw the conversation as being about future actions rather than past performance.[^s3] Goldsmith makes a similar practical argument: people cannot change the past, they take suggestions less personally than criticism, and they listen better when they are not preparing a rebuttal.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Frame the request around a goal ("I want my status emails to be shorter") rather than a failing, and ask for a small, specific number of ideas so the other person can answer quickly.[^s1] You can ask peers, people in other teams or even people outside your organisation, because a useful suggestion does not depend on having watched your past performance.[^s1] Treat every idea as an offer, not an instruction: write it down, thank the person, and decide later which ones to try.[^s1]

As a manager, you can bring the same forward focus into one-on-ones and reviews by moving quickly from "what happened" to "what will you do next time, and what do you need from me".[^s3][^s5] In the Feedforward Interview version, start with the employee's own success story and ask what enabled it before turning to the gap between their goals and where they are now.[^s4][^s5] Feedforward works well in writing (a short message asking two or three colleagues for one idea each), but the no-debate rule is easier to keep live, where you can simply say thanks.

## Common mistakes

The most common slip is breaking the no-argument rule - answering a suggestion with "we tried that" - which turns the exchange back into a debate.[^s1] A second is using feedforward to dodge a necessary conversation: a missed deadline or a conduct problem still needs specific, direct feedback about what happened, and Kluger and Nir themselves presented their interview as a way to complement appraisal as well as replace it.[^s2] A third, noted by reviewers of the Feedforward Interview, is bolting a positive, future-focused postscript onto an otherwise negative review, which can confuse and irritate staff; they advise that it replace the traditional format rather than decorate it.[^s4] Finally, managers should keep developmental feedforward separate from decisions on pay and promotion, which still need their own administrative conversation.[^s5]

## Evidence

The evidence is encouraging but indirect, so its status here is mixed. Goldsmith's exercise itself has not, as far as the sources reviewed show, been tested in a controlled study; its support rests on workshop observations.[^s1] The closest experimental evidence comes from Gnepp and colleagues (2020), where a perceived future focus was linked to greater motivation to improve - but two of the three studies used role-played performance reviews rather than real ones.[^s3] For the Feedforward Interview, a field test by Budworth, Latham and Manroop found that employees given a feedforward interview performed better four months later than those who had a traditional appraisal.[^s4] That is a single study of a different protocol, partly involving the method's own advocates, and the original authors called for more research on when the approach works best.[^s2][^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Feedforward was popularised by Marshall Goldsmith.[^s1] It addresses defensiveness, because research shows that past-focused discussions provoke self-protective reactions while a future focus raises motivation, and it is applied in performance reviews.[^s3] It is supported by Gnepp et al. (2020) and, more loosely, by Budworth, Latham and Manroop's field test of the related Feedforward Interview.[^s3][^s4] It sits under feedback in general, requires the active listening needed to hear suggestions without rebutting them, and works alongside Situation-Behavior-Impact, which handles the specific, past-focused message that feedforward deliberately leaves out, and one-on-one meetings, where forward-looking questions fit naturally.[^s1][^s5]
