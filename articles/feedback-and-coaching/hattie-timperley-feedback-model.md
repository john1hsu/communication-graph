  - Before the conversation, write the goal in one line - what good looks like for this piece of work - and check the other person would describe it the same way (where am I going?).
  - Compare the work with that goal using specific observations, saying what is already on target and what is not (how am I going?).
  - Name one or two concrete next actions, a stretch or a resource that will close the gap (where to next?).
  - Aim most comments at the process - the approach, method or strategy that produced the result - rather than only at whether this output is right.
  - Where the person is experienced, add a self-regulation prompt that helps them check their own work next time, such as a question they can ask before sending.
  - Drop or separate praise of the person ("you're a star") from the information about the work, so it does not crowd out the useful part.

# Hattie and Timperley's feedback model

Hattie and Timperley's model treats feedback as information that closes the gap between where someone is and where they are meant to be.[^s1] It says useful feedback answers three questions - where am I going (the goal, sometimes called "feed up"), how am I going (progress against it, "feed back") and where to next (the following step, "feed forward") - and that each answer can be pitched at one of four levels: the task, the process used to do it, the person's self-regulation, or the person themselves.[^s1] For a manager, the model works as a checklist: good feedback makes the goal clear, locates the work against it, points forward, and is aimed at how the work is done rather than at who did it.[^s1]

## Origin

John Hattie and Helen Timperley, both then at the University of Auckland, set out the model in "The Power of Feedback", a 2007 review in *Review of Educational Research*.[^s1] They built it from a synthesis of 12 earlier meta-analyses on classroom feedback (196 studies, 6,972 effect sizes) that put feedback's average effect at d = 0.79, roughly double the typical effect of a year of schooling.[^s1] They leaned heavily on Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 meta-analysis and its feedback intervention theory, which found that feedback lost power as it pulled attention away from the task and toward the self.[^s1][^s5] Although written for teachers and students, the model is now used in workplace training settings such as medical residencies.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Feedback does not reliably help. Kluger and DeNisi's analysis of 607 effect sizes found a positive average (d = 0.41) but also that more than a third of feedback interventions made performance worse.[^s5] Hattie and Timperley's contribution is a way to predict which side of that line a given comment will land on.[^s1] In their reading, task-level feedback (right or wrong, what is missing) is powerful for fixing errors but often does not transfer to the next piece of work; process-level feedback (the method or strategy) and self-regulation feedback (how the person checks and corrects their own work) drive deeper improvement; and self-level comments such as "good job, you're great" carry the least information and can distract.[^s1] They cite praise studies with average effects near d = 0.1, though praise tied to effort or strategy did better.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Use the three questions as a structure for any feedback conversation, from a one-on-one to written comments on a draft: confirm the goal, compare the work with it, then name the next step.[^s1] Missing goals are a common reason feedback fails, because comments only help when the person knows what they are aiming at.[^s1] Then aim the comments at the right level. Novices and people making factual errors need task-level correction first; once the basics are right, move up to process ("start from the client's decision, then add the data") and self-regulation ("what check could you run before sending?").[^s1] Keep praise of the person separate from information about the work, or leave it out.[^s1]

Timing depends on level: in studies reviewed by the authors, immediate feedback suited process-level learning while delay sometimes helped at the task level.[^s1] Confidence matters too: feedback has most effect when someone is confident and wrong, and little when they are unsure and wrong, because they are not yet ready to use it.[^s1] Live conversation suits process and self-regulation feedback, where you may need to ask how the person approached the work; tracked changes and short written notes mostly carry task-level corrections.[^s1][^s3]

## Common mistakes

Feedback in real workplaces tends to stay at the bottom of the model. A 2025 study of 1,828 written feedback notes for family-medicine residents in Canada found task-level comments in 60% and process comments in 36%, while self-regulation and self-level comments each appeared in only 2%; no note covered all four levels.[^s3] The authors suggest the model could guide training for the senior clinicians who give that feedback.[^s3] Other pitfalls are giving a verdict without any corrective information, which the authors judge of little use, and confusing whether people *like* feedback with whether it helps them, since reassurance can feel good without changing performance.[^s1] Finally, the model's "feed forward" is simply its third question - where to next - pitched at the same goal as the first two.[^s1] It should not be confused with the separate coaching technique of the same name, which asks colleagues for future-focused suggestions.

## Evidence

The model is a framework rather than a tested intervention, and its parts have mixed support. A 2020 meta-analysis co-authored by Hattie, covering 435 studies, found a lower average effect for feedback (d = 0.48) than the 2007 synthesis and very high variation between studies.[^s2] It supported the core claim that feedback works better the more information it carries - high-information feedback reached d = 0.99 against d = 0.24 for bare rewards or punishments - but it could not code the four levels or test the claim that self-level praise is least effective.[^s2] The method behind the 2007 figure, averaging across many meta-analyses with different designs and outcomes, belongs to Hattie's *Visible Learning* approach, which a statistician has criticised for combining incompatible studies and treating correlational effects as causal.[^s4] Most of the evidence comes from schools and universities, so workplace effects are inferred rather than demonstrated.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

The model was introduced by John Hattie and Helen Timperley and influenced by Kluger and DeNisi's feedback intervention theory, whose warning about self-focused feedback it builds into its fourth level.[^s1][^s5] It is supported, in part, by the 2020 meta-analysis of Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie, which confirmed the value of information-rich feedback.[^s2] It has been applied in workplace-based assessment, where it was used to code clinical supervisors' written feedback.[^s3] It sits under feedback, alongside Situation-Behavior-Impact, which supplies a script for the "how am I going" step, and it relates to Goldsmith's feedforward, one-on-ones and performance reviews as settings where its questions can be asked.[^s1]
