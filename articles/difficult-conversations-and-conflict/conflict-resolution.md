# Conflict resolution

Conflict resolution is the field that asks how disputes can be changed so that they are carried on constructively: with less hostility, with outcomes both sides can accept, and with settlements that last.[^s1] Sociologist Louis Kriesberg describes it less as a tightly bounded discipline than as a general approach, covering the long-term strategies and short-term tactics of the disputing parties themselves as well as the work of mediators and other intermediaries.[^s1] Its subject ranges from two colleagues arguing over a deadline to labour disputes and wars, and its practical offshoots include negotiation, mediation and alternative dispute resolution (ADR).[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Kriesberg traces precursors to the 1930s and 1940s, including Mary Parker Follett's work on labour-management conflict and Kurt Lewin's research on group processes.[^s1] Early institutions followed: the *Journal of Conflict Resolution* began in 1957 and the University of Michigan opened its Center for Research on Conflict Resolution in 1959.[^s1] Landmark ideas of the next decades included Kenneth Boulding's *Conflict and Defense* (1962), Charles Osgood's GRIT proposal for reducing tension through small, announced, reciprocated concessions (1962), and Morton Deutsch's *The Resolution of Conflict* (1973).[^s1] Between roughly 1970 and 1985 a core consensus formed, above all the idea that a conflict can be reframed as a shared problem; the field spread as a social movement, community mediation centres multiplied in the United States, and Fisher and Ury's *Getting to Yes* (1981) carried interest-based negotiation to a wide audience.[^s1] From the late 1980s it diversified by setting and stage and became institutionalised; for example, a 1998 US federal law required federal trial courts to promote ADR.[^s1]

Deutsch's work supplied much of the field's psychological core. His early experiments contrasted groups whose goals were positively linked (cooperation) with groups whose goals were negatively linked (competition): competition brought poor communication, suspicion and deception, while cooperation brought trust and openness.[^s2] He extended this to argue that constructive conflict resolution resembles a cooperative problem-solving process and destructive conflict resembles competition, and that each kind of relationship tends to reproduce the processes that created it, so competitive exchanges escalate while cooperative ones build on themselves.[^s2] He later called treating a conflict as a mutual problem the key idea of the conflict resolution movement.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Workplace conflict is costly when it turns personal. A 2003 meta-analysis by Carsten De Dreu and Laurie Weingart found that relationship conflict was strongly and negatively related to team performance and member satisfaction.[^s4] It also found that task conflict (disagreement about the work itself) was negatively related to both, contradicting the then-common textbook claim that task conflict reliably helps teams.[^s4] A larger 2012 meta-analysis by de Wit, Greer and Jehn, covering 116 studies and 8,880 groups, confirmed stable negative links for relationship and process conflict but found no strong overall negative link for task conflict.[^s5] Task conflict was more positive where it was only weakly tied to relationship conflict, in top management teams, and when outcomes were measured as decision quality or financial performance.[^s5] The practical message from both reviews is that disagreement about substance is not automatically healthy; it helps only when it stays separate from personal friction.[^s4][^s5]

## Using the field at work

The field's central move translates directly into everyday practice: describe the disagreement as a problem both sides need to solve ("we need a launch date that both QA and sales can live with") rather than as a contest over whose position wins.[^s2][^s3] Deutsch's research implies protecting the cooperative features of the exchange, such as open information, honesty and visible concern for the other side's interests, because misleading or coercive moves push a dispute toward a destructive, escalating course.[^s2][^s3] The meta-analytic evidence adds a guardrail: keep debate on the work, and act early when it starts to sound personal, since task conflict is least harmful when it is not tangled up with relationship conflict.[^s5] When two parties cannot get there alone, the field's main tools are negotiation and a neutral third party such as a mediator.[^s1][^s3]

## Limits and critiques

Critics, including many feminist scholars, argue that conflict resolution can overlook power differences: in mediated agreements the weaker party tends to concede more, and a dominant party can use a "resolution" process to keep control.[^s1] Scholars also disagree about whether settling a dispute merely suppresses it when underlying grievances or injustices remain, and Kriesberg acknowledges that the field's methods need more rigorous evaluation.[^s1] At work, this means a resolved-looking conversation between a manager and a report is not proof that the underlying issue has been dealt with.[^s1]

## Connections

Morton Deutsch's theory of cooperation and competition is the main explanatory framework behind the field's view of constructive and destructive conflict.[^s2] The field was influenced by social psychology through Lewin's group research, by Charles Osgood's GRIT strategy for de-escalation, and by *Getting to Yes*, whose interest-based approach underpins principled negotiation and the interests-versus-positions principle.[^s1] In this graph, difficult conversations, the Thomas-Kilmann conflict modes, de-escalation and role reversal are studied within it, and negotiation and mediation are its main practices.[^s1][^s3] The De Dreu-Weingart and de Wit-Greer-Jehn meta-analyses are its key workplace evidence on when team conflict helps or hurts.[^s4][^s5]
