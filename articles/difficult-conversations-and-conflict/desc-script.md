steps:
  - Pick one specific, recent behaviour to raise, and plan a private moment to raise it rather than doing it in front of others.
  - "Describe: write one sentence stating what happened in observable facts - when, what, how often - with no labels or guesses about motive."
  - "Express: add one sentence on how it affects you or the work, starting with \"I\" rather than \"you\"."
  - "Specify: state the concrete change you want, small enough to agree to now; if you are open to options, suggest one and ask for theirs."
  - "Consequences: say what will improve for both of you or for the team's goal if the change happens, and, only if needed, what you will do if it does not."
  - Rehearse the four sentences aloud, then deliver them, stop, and listen to the reply before negotiating the specifics.
example:
  situation: A project manager's colleague has twice changed the client deck the night before a review without telling her, and she has had to rework her talking points in the hour before the meeting.
  before: "You always do this. You clearly don't care whether I look prepared in front of the client."
  after: "On Tuesday and again this morning the deck changed after 9 p.m., and I found out at 8 a.m. (Describe). I end up rewriting my part in a rush, and I'm worried it shows in front of the client (Express). Could we freeze the deck at 5 p.m. the day before, with late changes flagged in our team chat? (Specify) Then we both walk in with the same story and a calmer morning (Consequences). What would make that work for you?"

# DESC script

A DESC script is a short, pre-planned message for asking someone to change a behaviour that is causing you or the team a problem. It has four parts: **Describe** the situation, **Express** how it affects you, **Specify** the change you want, and state the **Consequences** of making it.[^s4] Its purpose is to let you be assertive - honest about what you need while respecting the other person - in a way that keeps them listening instead of defending themselves.[^s4]

## Origin

The script comes from *Asserting Yourself*, a step-by-step self-help programme written by communication trainer Sharon Anthony Bower and Stanford psychologist Gordon H. Bower, which drew on behaviour-change psychology, speech and acting; the publisher still lists the DESC scripts as the book's signature tool (updated edition, 2004).[^s1] Healthcare later adopted a team-oriented version: the U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) teaches DESC in TeamSTEPPS as its tool for resolving conflict, especially interpersonal conflict and serious behaviour such as hostility that puts patients or staff at risk.[^s2] Nursing textbooks now present it from the TeamSTEPPS pocket guide.[^s6]

The two versions differ in a useful way. In the Bowers' line, "S" means *specify* the behaviour you would prefer and the consequences can be positive or negative.[^s4] In TeamSTEPPS, "S" means *suggest* alternatives and seek agreement, and the consequences are framed around shared team goals with consensus as the aim.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Hard conversations go wrong in two predictable ways: people stay silent, or they open with "you" statements that trigger defensiveness, after which the other person stops listening.[^s4] DESC forces the speaker to lead with checkable facts, own their reaction and end with a concrete ask, which is why Yale recommends it to managers for emotional, high-stakes talks.[^s4] Because you write it beforehand, the script also survives the nerves of the moment.[^s4]

## How to apply it

1. **Choose the moment and place.** AHRQ treats DESC as a form of feedback: raise it soon, and in private, so neither side is protecting their reputation in front of an audience.[^s2]
2. **Describe** with concrete data - dates, numbers, what was said or done.[^s2]
3. **Express** the effect in "I" language rather than blame.[^s2][^s4]
4. **Specify or suggest** one change. Use the specific request when the fix is clear; offer alternatives and ask for theirs when you need buy-in.[^s2][^s4]
5. **State consequences** in terms of shared goals - what improves for the team or the customer - and aim for agreement.[^s2]
6. **Write it down and rehearse it** before the conversation.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Judgements dressed as description.** "You were rude" is a verdict; "you cut me off three times in the meeting" is a description.[^s2][^s4]
- **"You" in the Express step.** Sentences that start with "you" tend to put people on the defensive.[^s4]
- **Turning consequences into threats.** TeamSTEPPS frames them around team and patient goals and a solution everyone can accept; focus on what is right, not who is right.[^s2][^s6]
- **Expecting a guarantee.** DESC lowers defensiveness but cannot make the other person agree.[^s4]
- **Using it for an urgent safety stop.** For an immediate risk, TeamSTEPPS uses faster statements of concern and the two-challenge rule; DESC is for disagreements where you want a lasting resolution.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The guides that teach DESC present it as expert advice rather than reporting trials of the script itself, so it is rated **practitioner** here.[^s1][^s2][^s4] Evidence for the wider family it belongs to is stronger: a 2023 meta-analysis of 11 studies found that assertive-communication training improved nurses' speaking-up behaviour, though not their attitudes or confidence, and that structured content, several teaching methods and enough training time mattered most.[^s5] TeamSTEPPS also notes that DESC works best where the culture is committed to safety for patients and staff, so a script cannot substitute for a team where speaking up is safe.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Broader:** assertive communication - DESC is presented as a way to be assertive while reducing defensiveness.[^s4]
- **Develops** assertive communication, by giving a repeatable structure to rehearse.[^s4]
- **Addresses** defensiveness and accusatory "you" statements, by leading with facts and "I" language.[^s4]
- **Applied in** difficult conversations and interpersonal conflict at work.[^s2]
- **Introduced in** *Asserting Yourself* by Sharon Anthony Bower and Gordon H. Bower.[^s1]
- **Complements** graded assertiveness: TeamSTEPPS pairs DESC for resolving disagreements with assertive statements, CUS and the two-challenge rule for urgent concerns.[^s3]
- **Requires** I-statements, which form the Express step; **related** to situation-behaviour-impact feedback and nonviolent communication, which share the observe-then-impact sequence, and to TeamSTEPPS, saying no and conflict resolution.[^s2][^s4][^s6]
