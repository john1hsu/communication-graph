steps:
  - Agree the ground rule before the discussion starts - each person summarises the other's position, and the other confirms or corrects it, before anyone argues back.
  - Let each side state its own position once, briefly and without interruption.
  - Swap - each person presents the other's position in the first person ("As the ops lead, my concern is...") covering their goal, their reasons and what worries them.
  - Ask the original owner to rate the summary and fill in anything missing or distorted; repeat until they say it is fair.
  - Only then argue your own case, starting from the points you now know you share and naming the points that still genuinely conflict.
  - When preparing alone for a hard negotiation, write or rehearse the other party's brief in their voice and test which of your own arguments would actually move them.
example:
  situation: A product manager and an operations lead are stuck on whether to launch a feature before the quarter-end freeze.
  before: "PM - We have to ship on the 28th; sales has promised it. Ops - You always do this, and my team pays for it on call. PM - It's a small change, you're overreacting."
  after: "PM - Let me try your side first. You're worried that launching two days before the freeze leaves no window to roll back, and your on-call team absorbed the last two late launches. Is that right? Ops - Mostly; the bigger issue is we have no runbook yet. Now let me do yours: sales made a commitment to a named customer and slipping costs you credibility. PM - Yes. So can we ship to that one customer behind a flag and do the full launch after the freeze?"
  why: Each side states the other's concern until the owner agrees it is fair, which surfaces the real blocker (no runbook) and a shared fact (one customer matters most), and opens an option neither raised while they were only defending their own positions.

  - Am I using the swap to understand them, or to rehearse a rebuttal?
  - If I argued their side to someone who shares their values, would it sound persuasive or like a caricature?

# Role reversal

Role reversal is a structured way of disagreeing: before you argue your own position, you state the other party's position back - their aim, their reasons, their worries - accurately enough that they accept the summary, and they do the same for you.[^s1] Its purpose is to strip out distortion and misunderstanding, so that whatever disagreement remains is about the real issue.[^s1] In training and preparation the same idea is taken further: you argue a negotiation from the other side's chair to see how the facts look from there.[^s3] It is one of the few practices that turns the skill of perspective-taking - which researchers regard as learnable - into a repeatable routine.[^s5]

## Origin

The technique comes from psychodrama. Jacob L. Moreno reportedly began experimenting with swapping roles while playing with children in Vienna around 1908, developed it in educational and industrial settings after moving to the United States in 1925, and his colleague Zerka Moreno's 1959 paper made it a cornerstone of psychodrama.[^s2] Conflict researchers then tested it in negotiation. In 1966 David W. Johnson, working under Morton Deutsch at Teachers College, Columbia University, ran a controlled study of role reversal between group representatives in a simulated civic dispute, funded by the Office of Naval Research.[^s1] Negotiation teachers now use it as a classroom simulation: the Program on Negotiation's exercise asks each participant to replay a difficult real negotiation from their opponent's side.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Most workplace stand-offs combine a real conflict with a misunderstanding of it. In Johnson and Deutsch's study, representatives who reversed roles understood their opponent's position better than those who only restated and defended their own, though their grasp of their own position did not change.[^s1] The Program on Negotiation lists further aims for the exercise: seeing how the same facts read differently depending on where you sit, exposing partisan perception and framing, and discovering which of your own arguments would actually persuade the other side.[^s3] That makes role reversal useful before a contract talk, a budget fight or a cross-team dispute, where misreading the other side's priorities wastes rounds of argument.[^s3]

## How to apply it

Set the rule up front and keep the order strict: state your own position briefly, swap, have the owner correct the summary until it is fair, and only then argue.[^s1] Speak the other side's case in the first person and include their reasons and fears, not just their demand, because the exercise is meant to build understanding of their viewpoint rather than a list of their asks.[^s3] When you cannot get the other party in the room, rehearse alone: write their brief and argue it aloud, then note which of your own points it defeats.[^s3] Psychodrama practitioners stress that the swap only helps if you return to your own role and reflect on what you learned; without that step people slip straight back into their original positions.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating understanding as agreement.** In Johnson and Deutsch's study, when positions were truly incompatible, role reversal reduced each side's sense of being understood and did no better than plain self-presentation on agreements reached or willingness to compromise.[^s1] Clearer understanding can expose a real clash that vagueness had been hiding.[^s1]
- **Using it in a contest.** Reviews of the research suggest the technique works best when the relationship is cooperative and the parties' starting positions are compatible, and can push apart people whose positions are incompatible.[^s2]
- **Playing a caricature.** Arguing an opponent's case in terms that clash with your own values can backfire; in one Stanford study, taking the opposing side's perspective before writing counter-arguments cancelled the attitude change those arguments otherwise produced.[^s4]
- **Forcing it on people who cannot do it safely.** Psychodrama practitioners advise against reversing roles with someone harmed by the other party, and note that it works better between people with closer backgrounds.[^s2]

## Evidence

The status is `mixed`. The core experiment is small - eight conditions of ten male high-school and college students - and it found a reliable gain in understanding the opponent but benefits for attitude change and perceived similarity only in specific conditions, and none on agreements reached.[^s1] A later review of the psychodrama literature concluded there was not enough evidence that mutual role reversal promotes reconciliation, and that most studies used fictional characters rather than real counterparts.[^s2] Catapano, Tormala and Rucker's 2019 studies, beginning with nearly 500 online participants, add a modern qualification: adopting an opponent's perspective helped only when that opponent shared the participant's underlying values.[^s4] The technique is therefore well suited to diagnosing a dispute and building perspective-taking, but should not be sold as a reliable route to agreement.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a conflict-resolution technique for disputes between individuals or group representatives.[^s1]
- **Develops** perspective-taking, by forcing an accurate, checked account of the other side's view; the 1966 study found better understanding of the opponent's position.[^s1][^s5]
- **Demonstrated by** Johnson and Deutsch's 1966 role-reversal study at Columbia, which also showed the effect depends on whether positions are compatible.[^s1]
- **Introduced by** Jacob L. Moreno, who developed it within psychodrama.[^s2]
- **Applied in** negotiation preparation, where arguing the other side's case tests your own arguments, and in difficult interpersonal conversations.[^s2][^s3]
- **Complements** paraphrasing: role reversal relies on restating the other party's position to remove distortion, then adds arguing it from their side.[^s1]
- **Challenged by** Catapano, Tormala and Rucker (2019), whose studies found that taking an opponent's perspective can blunt persuasion when values differ.[^s4]
- **Related:** perspective-getting (asking rather than imagining), empathy, integrative negotiation and Morton Deutsch's research on cooperation and conflict.[^s1][^s5]
