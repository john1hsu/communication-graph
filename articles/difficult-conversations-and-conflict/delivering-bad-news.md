# Delivering bad news

Delivering bad news is the situation of telling a colleague, report, client or manager something that will disappoint, worry or cost them: the project is cancelled, the promotion went to someone else, the release will slip, the budget is cut, the role is being eliminated. It differs from feedback because the news is usually an outcome rather than a behaviour the listener can change, and from an apology because the messenger may not have caused it. Organizational researchers treat it as a core managerial task in its own right, running through layoffs, performance decisions and change programmes.[^s6]

## Origin

The most widely taught structure comes from medicine. In 2000 Walter Baile and colleagues published SPIKES, a six-step protocol for telling patients they have cancer: set up the conversation, find out what the person already perceives, get their invitation for information, give knowledge, address emotions, and agree a strategy with a summary.[^s1] In management research, a 2013 review in the *Journal of Management* drew on studies of professionals whose jobs involve bad news and organised the task into three linked phases - preparation, delivery and transition.[^s6] University leadership programmes now teach adapted versions for workplace news.[^s4]

## Why it matters

The default is to delay or dilute. Social psychologists call the reluctance to pass on unwelcome information the MUM effect, after Rosen and Tesser's 1970 phrase for keeping mum about undesirable messages.[^s3] In a 2017 study with 309 undergraduates, Dibble found reluctance was highest for bad news whether the channel was face to face, text or email, and that the strongest predictor was fear of upsetting the recipient rather than fear of being blamed, so the urge to postpone often feels kind.[^s3] The sample was students imagining a friend's exam result, so the size of the effect at work is unknown.[^s3]

Order matters too. In lab studies with 121 students working in pairs reported in 2013, Legg and Sweeny found that 78% of recipients wanted the bad news first, while news-givers mostly preferred to start with good news; asking givers to imagine the recipient's feelings shifted them only slightly.[^s5] Recipients who heard the bad news first ended up less worried.[^s5] The authors argue a cushioning "sandwich" mainly serves the speaker.[^s5]

## What works here

Use the three phases as a checklist.[^s6]

**Prepare.** Decide the one-sentence message and the outcome you want, rehearse it, gather the facts and documents, and choose a private setting and a time that lets the person react - not five minutes before their next meeting.[^s4][^s1] If the decision was yours, plan to own it rather than attribute it to "leadership".[^s4]

**Deliver.** Start by asking what they already know, so you can correct misunderstandings and pitch the detail.[^s1] Give a brief warning ("I have some disappointing news about the vendor contract"), then state the news plainly and early, in small chunks and without jargon.[^s1][^s4] Stay hard on the problem and soft on the person: stick to specific, dated facts and avoid speculating about motives.[^s4]

**Respond to the reaction.** Expect emotion and name it before moving on; SPIKES suggests observing the feeling, identifying its cause and making a statement that links the two ("I can see this is frustrating after the weekends you put in").[^s1] Don't water the message down because the reaction is uncomfortable.[^s4]

**Transition.** Close with what happens next: options, decisions still open, support available and a follow-up time, then check what they understood.[^s1][^s6] If the person can act on the news, end on the action; if nothing can be done, the positive context belongs after the news, as help in absorbing it.[^s5]

A workplace example: a product manager has to tell a client that a promised integration will ship in April, not January. Rather than an email opening with recent wins, she books a call, asks what the client has heard about the integration, says plainly that it has slipped to April and why, acknowledges the client's own launch is affected, and ends with two concrete options and a date to decide.[^s1][^s4]

**Channel.** Reluctance is similar across channels, but a live conversation lets you hear the reaction and respond to it, which a one-way message cannot; follow up in writing so the facts and next steps are on record.[^s3][^s1]

## Pitfalls

- **Waiting for a better moment.** Delay feels considerate but lets rumour fill the gap; set yourself a deadline.[^s3][^s4]
- **Burying the news.** Opening with compliments or context keeps the listener anxious and can make the key message easy to miss.[^s5]
- **Hiding behind others.** Blaming "the business" for a call you made damages trust; own your part.[^s4]
- **Talking through the emotion.** Pressing on with logistics while the person is upset means little of it will be heard.[^s1]
- **Ending without a next step.** The transition phase is where people decide whether the messenger can still be trusted.[^s6]

## Evidence

The research base is strongest in medicine. A 2018 meta-analysis of 17 controlled studies (1,322 participants in the main analysis) found that short training, usually role-play with feedback, produced a large improvement in observer-rated news-delivery skill and a moderate rise in confidence; SPIKES-based programmes showed larger effects, which the authors treat as tentative.[^s2] Heterogeneity was high, outcomes were measured only immediately after training, and just one study measured recipients' wellbeing, so evidence that better delivery improves outcomes for the people hearing the news is thin.[^s2] Workplace advice is largely adapted from this clinical work and from practitioner experience.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

- **Broader:** a kind of difficult conversation, where the news is the hard part rather than a disagreement.[^s6]
- **Explained by** the MUM effect - the reluctance to transmit unwelcome information that drives delay and dilution.[^s3]
- **Demonstrated by** Johnson and Panagioti (2018) on trainability, Legg and Sweeny (2013) on news order, and Dibble (2017) on why messengers hesitate.[^s2][^s5][^s3]
- **Studied in** organizational communication, where it is framed as preparation, delivery and transition.[^s6]
- **Requires** active listening, because the perception and emotion steps depend on hearing the other person first.[^s1]
- **Related:** the SPIKES protocol, social accounts (explaining why it happened), apology, the feedback sandwich, bottom line up front, communicating uncertainty and channel selection.[^s1][^s5]
