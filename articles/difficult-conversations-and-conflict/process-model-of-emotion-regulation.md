# Process model of emotion regulation

The process model of emotion regulation is James Gross's framework for classifying the ways people influence their own emotions. It organises strategies by the point at which they act on an emotion as it is generated, rather than by what they feel like from the inside.[^s6] The model names five families: situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change and response modulation (often discussed through its best-known member, expressive suppression).[^s2] For anyone who has to stay clear-headed in a tense meeting, a hard client call or a feedback conversation, it is a map of *where* you can intervene, from choosing the setting to managing what shows on your face.[^s2][^s1]

## Origin

The model was developed by Stanford psychologist James Gross and is widely cited as Gross's process model;[^s4] a 2015 methods review described it as having had enormous influence on emotion-regulation research.[^s6] It rests on a "modal model" of emotion in which a situation is attended to, appraised, and then gives rise to loosely coupled responses, a sequence that can loop and that regulation can target at any stage.[^s5] Gross and colleagues later proposed an **extended process model**, which adds the decisions around regulation itself: identifying that an emotion needs regulating, selecting a strategy, implementing a specific tactic, and monitoring whether to keep going, switch or stop.[^s3][^s4] In this later work, emotion regulation is defined as activating a goal to influence the emotion-generative process.[^s4]

## Why it matters

The model makes one practical point that workplace advice often misses: strategies differ in *timing*, and earlier ones act before a full response has formed.[^s2] In a budget review where you expect pushback, the five families look like this:

- **Situation selection** – asking for the conversation in a one-on-one rather than in front of the whole team.[^s2]
- **Situation modification** – sending the numbers ahead so the meeting starts from shared facts.[^s2]
- **Attentional deployment** – shifting your focus from the critic's tone to the question being asked.[^s2]
- **Cognitive change** – reappraising the challenge as a request for evidence rather than an attack.[^s2]
- **Response modulation** – keeping your voice and expression level while you are still irritated.[^s2]

Because each family acts at a different stage, they have different costs. Doré, Silvers and Ochsner summarise lab findings that reappraisal tends to reduce behavioural, experiential and physiological signs of emotion, whereas suppression mainly changes outward behaviour while raising physiological arousal and impairing memory for what was said.[^s2] For a communicator, that means hiding frustration can leave you calmer-looking but less able to recall the substance of the conversation.[^s2]

## Evidence

The largest test of the model's strategy families is Webb, Miles and Sheeran's 2012 meta-analysis of 306 experimental comparisons.[^s1] It found that cognitive change had a small-to-medium overall effect (d = 0.36), response modulation a small effect (d = 0.16) and attentional deployment no overall effect (d = 0.00).[^s1] The families were not uniform inside: distraction helped (d = 0.27) while concentrating on the emotion did not (d = −0.26), perspective taking was among the strongest forms of cognitive change (d = 0.45), and suppressing emotional expression worked (d = 0.32) while suppressing the felt experience or thoughts about the event did not.[^s1] The authors also reported that effectiveness depended on the emotion being regulated, the purpose of the strategy and study design.[^s1]

These findings support the model's taxonomy as a useful way to organise research, but they also show its limits. Doré and colleagues argue that the basic model is well supported yet has encouraged a search for main effects, when outcomes depend on the person, the situation and the strategy together.[^s2] For example, they report work by Troy, Shallcross and Mauss in which reappraisal ability went with lower depression under uncontrollable stress but higher depression when the stressor was controllable, so reappraising a fixable problem can stand in for fixing it.[^s2] Seeley and colleagues likewise note that context and individual differences cast doubt on labelling any strategy simply adaptive or maladaptive.[^s6] Conceptually, Parkinson's commentary on Gross's 2015 review argues that the model's criteria can both exclude genuine regulation and treat unlike strategies as equivalent, that implicit regulation is hard to test because it requires an unseen emotion goal, and that interpersonal regulation is poorly captured by treating other people as "extrinsic" regulators.[^s5] Much of the evidence comes from laboratory assessment rather than live workplace conversations, so applying it to meetings and negotiations is an extrapolation.[^s6][^s1]

## Connections

The model is the theory behind the broader skill of emotion regulation (`cm:emotion-regulation`), and it locates cognitive reappraisal (`cm:cognitive-reappraisal`) at the cognitive-change stage and expressive suppression (`cm:expressive-suppression`) at the response stage.[^s2] It was **introduced by** James Gross (`cm:james-j-gross`) and is linked to his integrative review (`cm:the-emerging-field-of-emotion-regulation`); its decision-focused successor is the extended process model (`cm:extended-process-model-of-emotion-regulation`).[^s6][^s3] It is **supported by** Webb, Miles and Sheeran's meta-analysis (`cm:webb-miles-sheeran-2012`), which tested strategies derived from it.[^s1] It is **challenged by** Parkinson's commentary on its boundaries (`cm:pushing-and-pulling-the-boundaries-of-emotion-regulation`) and by Troy and colleagues' evidence that reappraisal's value depends on context (`cm:troy-shallcross-mauss-2013`).[^s5][^s2] It is **studied in** psychology (`cm:psychology`).[^s6] Workplace tactics such as self-distancing (`cm:self-distancing`), taking a cooling-off period (`cm:cooling-off-period`) and labeling emotions (`cm:labeling-emotions`) can be read as instances of its families, which is why it is useful preparation for a difficult conversation (`cm:difficult-conversation`) where defensiveness (`cm:defensiveness`) is likely.[^s2][^s1]
