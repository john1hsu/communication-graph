  - Decide in advance which level the situation warrants - a question, a stated concern, a request to pause, or a stop - based on how serious and how imminent the harm is.
  - "Open by getting the person's attention and using their name: \"Priya, can I flag something before this goes out?\""
  - "State your concern in the first person and name the problem as you see it, with the specific fact behind it (\"I'm concerned - the Q3 figure on slide 4 doesn't match the ledger\")."
  - Offer a concrete fix or next step rather than only the objection.
  - Ask for an explicit decision or agreement, and wait for an answer instead of letting the point drop.
  - If you are not acknowledged, say it a second time, more plainly, naming the consequence you expect.
  - If the concern is still unresolved and the risk is serious, escalate in the open - ask for a pause, bring in a colleague, or go to the next level - and say that you are doing so.

# Graded assertiveness

Graded assertiveness is a family of short, rehearsed scripts for raising a concern with someone who outranks you, with a built-in way to turn up the strength of the message if the first attempt is not heard.[^s5] The best-known versions come from the U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's TeamSTEPPS programme: a five-part assertive statement, the escalating CUS phrases ("I am concerned... I am uncomfortable... this is a safety issue") and a two-challenge rule for when you are ignored.[^s1][^s2] Clinical teams also teach it with the PACE mnemonic: probe, alert, challenge, emergency.[^s5]

## Origin

The technique grew out of team-safety training in high-risk, hierarchical work. AHRQ notes that commercial aviation, the military and healthcare have all suffered tragic failures when junior members kept concerns to themselves or leaders would not listen.[^s1] Aviation research keeps finding the same pattern: a 2018 review of 18 studies, summarised by the Flight Safety Foundation, reported that a first officer's lack of assertiveness or corrective action was a significant factor in most of the accident and incident studies it examined, and that status and authority gradients can stop the monitoring pilot from voicing what they see.[^s6] TeamSTEPPS turned that lesson into teachable tools under the heading of "mutual support", and trauma services such as Victoria's state trauma system teach PACE and a five-step advocacy script for moments when a team member disagrees with the leader's plan.[^s2][^s5]

## Why it matters

The aviation review describes why juniors stay quiet even when they have seen a problem: they doubt themselves, presume the senior person is competent, worry about their careers and are sensitive to a senior's dismissive comments or silence.[^s6] Graded assertiveness lowers that cost in two ways. First, a script gives a nervous speaker words that are respectful of authority but still unmistakable, which AHRQ describes as clearly stating the concern and the suggestion without being threatening.[^s1] Second, agreed escalation words work as a shared signal: when a team has learned that "I am uncomfortable" means "stop and listen", saying it is less of a personal confrontation.[^s2] AHRQ's own worked example is not clinical at all - a colleague privately raising a teammate's rude tone with a receptionist - which shows the script carries over to office problems such as a release that should not ship or a client figure that does not add up.[^s1]

## How to apply it

The levels, from mildest to strongest:[^s5][^s2]

1. **Probe** - ask a question that draws attention to the issue ("Do you know the audit numbers changed last night?").
2. **Alert / state the concern** - use the assertive statement: get attention, state the concern, state the problem as you see it, offer a solution, and obtain agreement.[^s1]
3. **Challenge** - ask for the action to pause while a check is made ("I'm uncomfortable sending this; can we hold it for five minutes?").[^s5]
4. **Emergency / stop** - direct an immediate stop when harm is imminent. In TeamSTEPPS this is the "this is a safety issue" line and the right of any team member to stop the line.[^s2]

The two-challenge rule governs persistence. If your first assertive statement is ignored, you voice it a second time; the person challenged is expected to acknowledge it. If the reply still does not resolve the concern, you state the expected danger more plainly and then take stronger action - involving other team members or going up the chain of command.[^s2] AHRQ asks speakers to be persistent and persuasive and to back concerns with evidence or data.[^s1] It also notes that a statement can be appropriate when the senior person has done nothing wrong, for example when new information means an earlier decision should be revisited.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

- **Hinting instead of stating.** Opening with apologies and "probably nothing" lets the listener dismiss the concern; the assertive statement names the problem directly.[^s1]
- **Skipping the solution.** A concern with no proposed next step is harder to act on; the TeamSTEPPS script makes offering a solution a required step.[^s1]
- **Jumping straight to "stop".** Stop-the-line language is powerful because it is reserved for real risk; AHRQ says to use the two-challenge rule carefully, since it halts the process.[^s2]
- **Dropping it after one try.** The point of the two-challenge rule is that one unacknowledged comment is not the end of your responsibility.[^s2]
## Evidence and limits

Evidence that training changes behaviour is mixed. A 2020 systematic review of 14 intervention studies on speaking up and voice found mixed outcomes: some programmes improved speaking up, others did not, most relied on self-report, and the authors concluded that education alone may not shift deeply rooted habits.[^s3] In simulation, Pian-Smith and colleagues' 2009 study of teaching anaesthesia residents the two-challenge rule found improved speaking-up behaviour.[^s3] A later randomised experiment by Raemer and colleagues (2016) found that a speaking-up workshop for practising anaesthesiologists did not produce more speaking up, and the authors suggested culture change needs more intensive interventions.[^s4] Both simulation studies found participants were less likely to speak up to some team members than others, a reminder that the script cannot override the relationships around it.[^s3] Most research is from healthcare and aviation; the transfer to offices is plausible but largely untested.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

Graded assertiveness is a practice that develops the broader skill of upward dissent: AHRQ frames it as a firm, respectful way to question authority with evidence.[^s1] It is a structured form of assertive communication, and its first-person "I am concerned" lines echo I-statements.[^s2] Training in speaking up is meant to counter employee silence, the habit of withholding concerns that the 2020 review targets.[^s3] It complements formal escalation: the two-challenge rule ends by taking the issue up the chain of command once direct attempts fail.[^s2] Whether people use it depends on psychological safety and on the authority gradient in the team, which is why both simulation trials found speaking up varied by who was on the receiving end.[^s3][^s6] Pian-Smith (2009) supports it; Raemer (2016) challenges the idea that a short workshop suffices.[^s3][^s4]
