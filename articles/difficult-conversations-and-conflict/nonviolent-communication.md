steps:
  - Write down what happened as a camera would record it - who did what, when - and strike out every label, motive or "always".
  - Name the feeling it stirs in you using an emotion word (worried, frustrated, relieved), not a thought dressed as a feeling ("I feel ignored", "I feel that you...").
  - Identify the need or value behind the feeling (predictability, fairness, being able to do good work) and keep it separate from your preferred solution.
  - Turn your preferred solution into one concrete, doable request for now, phrased as what you do want, and be ready to hear "no".
  - If they say no or react strongly, listen for their observation, feeling, need and request, and reflect those back before restating yours.
example:
  situation: A team lead's colleague has twice changed a shared project plan the night before a client review without telling anyone.
  before: "You keep going rogue on the plan. It's disrespectful, and if it happens again I'm escalating it."
  after: "The plan changed on Monday night and again last night, and I found out in the client call. I was rattled, because I need to be able to stand behind what we show the client. Would you be willing to post changes in the channel by 5 p.m. the day before a review, starting this week? And what's been pushing those late changes?"

# Nonviolent Communication

Nonviolent Communication (NVC) is a model for raising a problem, and for hearing one, in four moves: an observation free of evaluation, a feeling, the need behind that feeling, and a request.[^s2] The trainers' summary of the model defines each part narrowly: an observation is specific to a time and context, a feeling is an emotion or bodily sensation rather than an opinion, a need is a universal value that names no particular person or action, and a request is concrete, doable now and open to the answer.[^s2] Its author describes it as more than a script: a way of using language to create connection rather than distance, and of sharing power with others rather than holding power over them.[^s3]

## Origin

The American clinical psychologist Marshall B. Rosenberg (1934-2015), who earned his PhD at the University of Wisconsin in 1961, developed the approach while working with civil rights activists in the early 1960s, when he also mediated student protests and helped desegregate public schools.[^s1] He founded the Center for Nonviolent Communication in 1984 and went on to train educators, health workers, lawyers, police, prisoners and officials in more than 60 countries.[^s1] The model was set out in his book *Nonviolent Communication: A Language of Life*, an edition of which was published in September 2015.[^s3]

## Why it matters

At work, conflicts escalate when one person's interpretation ("you went rogue") is delivered as fact and the other person defends their character instead of discussing the problem. NVC's core discipline is separating what happened from your evaluation of it, and separating a need (being able to stand behind a client deliverable) from the one strategy you prefer for meeting it, which leaves room for other solutions.[^s2] Its distinction between requests and demands also matters: a demand carries an implied threat or reward, so the listener hears coercion even when the words are polite.[^s2] A 2024 scoping review of NVC in health-care teams reported benefits for empathy, teamwork, communication and leadership, and less anger and interpersonal conflict, though from a small and uneven evidence base.[^s4]

## How to apply it

Draft the four components before a tense conversation, then test each one: would the other person accept the observation, is the feeling an emotion rather than a thought, is the need free of any reference to them, and is the request something they could do this week?[^s2] Requests come in three kinds - for clarity ("what did you hear me say matters to me?"), for the other person's reaction, and for an action - so you can check understanding before asking for change.[^s2] The listening half matters as much as the speaking half: when the other person reacts, guess at their feelings and needs and reflect them before you restate yours, since the model treats empathy as its central skill.[^s3][^s5] In email or chat, keep the observation and request explicit and short; in person, you can let the conversation carry the feelings and needs.

## Common mistakes

The commonest failure is the disguised judgement: "I feel disrespected" or "I feel you don't care" names what the speaker thinks the other person did, not an emotion, which the model distinguishes from feelings.[^s2] A second is a demand phrased as a request - if a "no" will be punished, it was a demand.[^s2] A third is using the formula mechanically or in a soft, therapeutic register that sounds out of place with an executive or client; Rosenberg frames NVC as a consciousness of compassion and authenticity as much as a set of words, and short trainings may not change habits: one study's authors concluded 10 hours was not enough for significant change.[^s3][^s6]

## Evidence

The evidence is encouraging but thin, so its status here is mixed rather than robust. In a 2014 single-group study of nursing students, empathy scores on the Interpersonal Reactivity Index rose modestly after NVC training (69.1 to 71.4), with focus groups describing more empathy for self and others; it used a single-group pre/post design without a comparison group.[^s5] The 2024 scoping review found only seven studies on NVC in health work, just two of them experimental, with heterogeneous methods and small samples, and called for larger, more rigorous trials.[^s4] A 2024 qualitative study of 118 young people found self-reported reductions in judgemental language and better recognition of feelings and needs, but it had no control group or pre/post measure, analysed written answers from 54 participants, and a co-author was the NVC trainer.[^s6] No large randomised trials in ordinary workplaces turned up in this review, so claims that NVC reliably resolves workplace conflict outrun the data.[^s4]

## Connections

NVC was introduced by Marshall Rosenberg, who first used it to mediate between protesters and school administrators, and it was codified in *Nonviolent Communication: A Language of Life*.[^s1][^s3] It aims to develop empathy, the outcome measured in the nursing-student study.[^s5] It is supported, weakly, by Nosek, Gifford and Kober (2014), the Adriani et al. (2024) scoping review and the Batūraitė-Bunka et al. (2024) youth study.[^s4][^s5][^s6] It sits under conflict resolution and alongside I-statements and situation-behaviour-impact feedback, which likewise ask the speaker to describe behaviour and its effect instead of judging the person; it relies on active listening, is used in difficult conversations and de-escalation, and targets the defensiveness that judgemental language provokes.[^s2]
