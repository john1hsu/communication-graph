steps:
  - Work out what the breach actually cost the other person (hours, money, a missed opportunity, an awkward moment with their own boss) before you decide what to offer.
  - Acknowledge the breach in the first person and name that cost, so the amends reads as repair rather than a bribe to move on.
  - Offer something concrete that at least restores their position - honour the original commitment, return the time, take on the rework - and make it cost you something real.
  - Ask what would put things right for them, framing the question so the responsibility stays with you, and treat their answer as a guide rather than an opening bid.
  - Deliver the amends promptly and completely; then keep your next commitments to them visibly, because consistent behaviour is what finishes the repair.
example:
  situation: A team lead promised a senior analyst she would lead the client workshop she had prepared for, then gave the slot to a newer colleague without telling her.
  before: "Sorry about the workshop mix-up - things moved fast and I didn't think it through. I'll make sure you get the next one."
  after: "I promised you the Henley workshop and gave it away without telling you, and that's on me - you lost the client exposure you had worked for. I've already asked Henley's sponsor to add you as co-lead for the follow-up session on the 28th, and I'll introduce you as the owner of the analysis. Is there anything else that would make this right for you? From now on any change to a role I've promised goes to the person first."
  why: The vague apology plus a future maybe costs the lead nothing, so it reads as cheap talk; the rewrite names the loss, restores a comparable opportunity with a date, invites her view of what is fair while keeping responsibility with the lead, and adds a change that makes a repeat less likely.

  - Will I keep my next few commitments to this person visibly, so the amends is not a one-off gesture?

# Penance and restitution after a breach

Penance, or restitution, is the part of repairing a breach where you give something back: you honour the promise you broke, return the time you cost someone, or absorb the extra work your mistake created. It turns "I'm sorry" from words into a costly signal that you value the relationship.[^s1] In trust-repair research it sits beside apologies, explanations and denials as one of the main responses a violator can choose, and it is usually the strongest of them.[^s5]

## Origin

The idea is old, but the workplace research traces to a 2002 study by Bottom, Gibson, Daniels and Murnighan, who asked whether "talk" after a defection rebuilds cooperation as well as substantive penance does.[^s1] In a repeated prisoner's dilemma with 225 undergraduate and MBA students, a programmed partner broke cooperation and then offered either a verbal promise to cooperate, a small or large payoff to the participant, or an open offer asking what would restore cooperation.[^s1] Later researchers in Rotterdam and Ghent, notably Desmet, De Cremer, Haesevoets and colleagues, tested how much compensation to give and how it interacts with apologies.[^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

Words are cheap after a breach, so people discount them. In the 2002 study, apologies and explanations helped, but any substantive penance produced more cooperation and more positive feelings than talk alone.[^s1] In two 2020 scenario experiments with 918 students and working adults, an organization breaking a promised raise or promotion recovered trust with every tactic except denial, and full penance, making the employee whole, worked best.[^s5] At work this means that when you cost a colleague time, credit or an opportunity, putting the loss right is often what convinces them you will not do it again.

## How to apply it

**Size it to the harm, then stop.** In the 2002 study, going from a small to a large payoff added nothing; the gap was between substantive and non-substantive penance.[^s1] A 2013 dictator-game study found undercompensation preserved the relationship less well than equal compensation or overcompensation.[^s3] Aim to restore their position fully rather than to impress.

**Ask, but keep the responsibility.** An open offer that invited the victim to name the remedy worked best when it clearly accepted responsibility; when the wording shifted the burden to the victim, it was less effective. People who were asked usually requested less than their actual loss.[^s1]

**Pair it with an apology.** Haesevoets and colleagues found apologies helped preserve relationships beyond what money did, especially when the compensation fell short.[^s3] Penance answers "what will you do about it"; the apology answers "do you understand what you did".

**Make your intent clear.** Desmet, De Cremer and van Dijk found that a slight overcompensation rebuilt more trust than an exact or partial payment only when the violator's bad intent was ambiguous. Once harm looked deliberate, extra money added nothing, and attributions of intent explained the pattern.[^s2]

## Common mistakes

**Overcompensating to buy forgiveness.** In a 2014 Ghent experiment with 111 students, victims trusted an allocator less after a large overcompensation than after an equal one, while observers rated the two the same.[^s4] Lavish amends can read as buying your way out.

**Using amends instead of acknowledgement.** Money without an apology works least well precisely when it is incomplete,[^s3] and after a clearly deliberate breach compensation alone cannot remove the suspicion about your intentions.[^s2]

**Making the victim do the work.** An open-ended "tell me what you want" that leaves the responsibility with them was less effective than one that kept it with the violator.[^s1]

**Promising future amends.** A vague "I'll make it up to you" is the mere talk that underperformed concrete penance.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is consistent that substantive amends outperform words alone, but it is mixed on details such as how much to give, and almost all of it comes from lab games or hypothetical scenarios with students and online adults.[^s1][^s5] Bottom and colleagues noted short, anonymous, low-stakes interactions and did not measure trust directly;[^s1] the Rotterdam and Ghent studies used small student samples and money-based harms.[^s2][^s4] How these effects hold in long working relationships, or after harms to reputation rather than pay, is less tested.

## Connections

Penance is **applied in** **trust repair** as its concrete, costly component, as shown in Bottom and colleagues' 2002 study (`cm:bottom-et-al-2002`), which **demonstrates** that substantive penance beats mere talk.[^s1] It is also **demonstrated by** Henderson, Welsh and O'Leary-Kelly's 2020 experiments, where full penance was the most effective response to a broken employment promise.[^s5] It **complements** an **apology**: each adds something the other lacks.[^s3] Its effect is **explained by** **attribution theory**, because compensation works differently depending on whether the victim sees the harm as intended.[^s2] **Service recovery** applies the same logic to clients, **social accounts** supply the explanation that usually accompanies amends, and the fairness of the remedy, which Haesevoets and colleagues tied to the equality norm, links it to **organizational justice**.[^s3]
