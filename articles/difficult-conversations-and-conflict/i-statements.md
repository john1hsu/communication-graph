steps:
  - Before speaking, name one specific, observable behaviour (what was said or done, and when) instead of a trait or a pattern word like "always" or "never".
  - Work out the concrete, tangible effect that behaviour has on you or your work - time lost, rework, a missed commitment.
  - Identify the feeling underneath, and check whether anger is masking something more precise such as worry or frustration.
  - Say it in the first person in roughly this order - behaviour, effect, feeling - and, if useful, a request for what you would like instead.
  - Add one sentence that shows you see the other person's side (their constraints, intentions or pressures) before or after your own view.
  - Stop and listen; expect some defensiveness, reflect back what you hear, and send a clearer second I-statement only if the first one was not understood.
example:
  situation: A project lead raises a recurring problem with a colleague who sends status updates late on the morning of the weekly client call.
  before: "You never get your updates in on time. You're making the whole team look unprepared in front of the client."
  after: "I know Mondays are packed for you with the support queue. When the status update reaches me after 9 a.m. on call days, I can't check the numbers before we present them, and I get anxious that we'll give the client something wrong. Could we agree on Friday afternoon as the cut-off?"
  why: The rewrite replaces a character verdict ("never", "you're making") with one observable behaviour, its concrete effect and the speaker's own feeling, acknowledges the colleague's constraint, and ends with a specific request - the combination the 2018 rating study found least likely to provoke defensiveness.
selfCheck:
  - Have I described a specific behaviour I could point to, or slipped in a label, a motive or an "always"?
  - Is my "I feel…" a real feeling, or a disguised accusation such as "I feel that you don't care"?
  - Have I said anything that shows I understand the other person's side of the situation?
  - After I speak, am I ready to listen to their reaction rather than repeat my point louder?

# I-statements

An I-statement raises a problem by describing what the other person did, how it affects you and how you feel about it, in your own voice, instead of delivering a judgement about them. A university conflict-management guide frames it as stating your feeling, the specific situation, its effect on you and what you want, and contrasts it with "you" statements that leave the listener feeling blamed or criticised.[^s4] In the original version the confronting message has three parts - the behaviour, its tangible effect, and your feeling - and deliberately explains why the behaviour is a problem rather than dictating a fix.[^s5]

## Origin

The technique comes from the American clinical psychologist Thomas Gordon, who founded Gordon Training International and whose Parent Effectiveness Training course, launched in 1962, taught "I-Messages" alongside active listening and the "communication roadblocks".[^s6] Gordon's organisation later carried the same three-part "confrontive I-message" into leader and workplace training, where it is presented as a way to confront a co-worker's behaviour while keeping the relationship intact.[^s5] Mediation and campus conflict programmes popularised a looser four-part template ("I feel… when… because… and I want…"), which adds an explicit request and puts the feeling first.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Most workplace conflict escalates not over the issue but over how it is raised: an accusation invites the other person to defend their character instead of solving the problem.[^s4] In a 2018 study, 253 participants rated opening lines for six conflicts (including two between work colleagues); statements in I-language were rated much less likely to make the recipient defensive than equivalent you-language statements, a large effect.[^s1] Correlational work points the same way: in a 2005 study of 59 couples discussing a problem, more second-person "you" pronouns went with more negative behaviour, while more first-person "I" pronouns went with greater satisfaction.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Start from evidence, not interpretation: name one behaviour you could point to, then the concrete cost to you, then the feeling, because people need a real reason to change and Gordon's materials warn that a message without its real effect rarely works.[^s5] Check whether anger is covering a more precise feeling such as worry, and expect the other person to be upset; respond by listening before sending a second, clearer message.[^s5] Add a line that acknowledges the other person's view: in the 2018 study, the opening rated least likely to provoke defensiveness combined I-language with both the speaker's and the other person's perspective, and I-language helped most when some perspective was communicated.[^s1] In email or chat, where the reader cannot hear your tone, the wording carries even more weight; the authors note their written-rating task may generalise best to text-based messages.[^s1]

## Common mistakes

The most common failure is a disguised accusation: "I feel that you don't respect my time" is a verdict with "I feel" in front of it, and the Iowa guide stresses starting with an emotion rather than a thought.[^s4] Other mistakes are leaving out the concrete effect, prescribing the solution instead of explaining the problem, and expecting every I-message to work first time.[^s5] Using the formula mechanically can also backfire: in recorded everyday arguments, partners often met feelings talk with objective counter-claims that deflected blame back to the speaker.[^s3]

## Evidence

The evidence is supportive but narrower than the popularity of the advice suggests, so its status here is mixed. The strongest experimental support (Rogers et al., 2018) comes from university students rating hypothetical opening lines on a within-participants design, which measures predicted defensiveness rather than what happens in a live exchange, and the authors acknowledge effects may be smaller in other designs.[^s1] The pronoun findings of Simmons and colleagues are correlational and come from couples in which one spouse had an anxiety disorder, so they show association, not that switching pronouns causes better outcomes.[^s2] A 2020 discourse analysis of about 140 hours of couples' everyday conversations found that I-language on its own rarely de-escalated arguments; what helped was speakers showing awareness of the partner's feelings or of the relationship dynamic.[^s3] Taken together, the studies suggest the active ingredient is less the pronoun than a non-blaming description paired with visible perspective-taking.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

I-statements address defensiveness, the reaction they were shown to reduce in rated conflict openings, and the accusatory you-statements that conflict guides warn escalate disputes.[^s1][^s4] They are applied in difficult conversations - confronting a behaviour or raising a grievance - and complement active listening, which Gordon paired with I-messages so the speaker shifts to listening once the other person reacts.[^s4][^s5] The technique was introduced by Thomas Gordon.[^s6] It is supported by Rogers et al. (2018) and, more weakly, by the correlational pronoun study of Simmons, Gordon and Chambless (2005), and challenged by Korobov's (2020) analysis of naturally occurring arguments.[^s1][^s2][^s3] It sits under assertive communication and alongside related approaches such as nonviolent communication, situation-behaviour-impact feedback and the choice between direct and indirect feedback, which likewise ask the speaker to describe observable behaviour rather than judge the person.
