# Cognitive reappraisal

Cognitive reappraisal means changing how you interpret a situation so that the emotion it produces changes. Instead of gritting your teeth through a provocative comment, you revisit what the comment means - why it was said, what the other person is under, what could come of it - and let the feeling follow the new reading.[^s1] In a difficult conversation it is the difference between replying to the attack you perceived and replying to the problem actually on the table.

## Origin

Reappraisal comes from James Gross's process model of emotion regulation at Stanford. In a 2002 review in *Psychophysiology*, Gross contrasted it with expressive suppression: reappraisal acts early, while the emotion is still forming, whereas suppression acts late, by holding back the outward signs of a feeling already under way.[^s1] Organizational researchers later mapped the same distinction onto customer-facing work: "deep acting", trying to actually feel the emotion the job requires, is treated as antecedent-focused regulation that works partly through reappraisal, while "surface acting" resembles suppression.[^s3] Eli Finkel, Gross and colleagues turned reappraisal into a brief conflict intervention in 2013, asking people to view a recent disagreement as a neutral third party who wants the best for everyone would.[^s4]

## Why it matters

In the laboratory work Gross reviewed, reappraisal reduced both felt emotion and its visible expression without harming memory, while suppression reduced only the expression, impaired memory for the conversation, and raised physiological arousal in the suppressor and in their partner.[^s1] At work, the emotional-labor meta-analysis of 95 studies found that surface acting was strongly linked to poorer well-being and job attitudes, whereas deep acting had only weak links to strain and a positive link to emotional performance and customer satisfaction.[^s3] The practical message is that hiding anger keeps it in the room; reinterpreting the trigger can take some of it out.

## How to apply it

Use reappraisal when a message or remark lands as a threat. Name the story you are telling yourself, then generate at least one alternative that is still accurate: a different cause, a neutral observer's view, or a possible upside.[^s1][^s4] Finkel's prompt is a usable template: how would a fair-minded third party see this disagreement, what gets in the way of you seeing it that way, and how will you take that view next time?[^s4] Prepare the reframe before predictable flashpoints, such as a standing meeting with a combative stakeholder, and say your reply from the new reading rather than the first one.[^s4]

First ask whether you can change the situation. In Troy, Shallcross and Mauss's 2013 study of 170 adults who had recently faced a stressful event, better reappraisal ability went with less depression when the stressor was uncontrollable but with more depression when it was controllable, such as work trouble caused by one's own performance; the authors suggest reframing can sap the motivation to fix things.[^s2] At work, reappraise the feeling so you can act - not instead of acting.

## Common mistakes

- **Spin instead of reinterpretation.** Telling yourself an implausibly rosy story rarely sticks; look for a reading that fits the facts.[^s1]
- **Reappraising problems you should fix.** A missed handoff caused by your own process is a cue to change the process, not just your mood.[^s2]
- **Calling suppression reappraisal.** If the anger is unchanged and you are only keeping a neutral face, you are surface acting, with its costs to you and your listener.[^s1][^s3]
- **Expecting a one-off to last.** The marital trial repeated the seven-minute exercise three times over a year with reminders; a condensed three-month version produced no reliable benefit.[^s4][^s5]

## Evidence

The basic effect - that reappraisal changes felt emotion more effectively and with fewer social costs than suppression - is well established in laboratory studies.[^s1] Applied evidence is encouraging but narrower. In Finkel's two-year randomized trial with 120 married couples, three 7-minute reappraisal writing tasks in the second year stopped the decline in marital quality seen in controls, partly by reducing conflict-related distress; it did not report fewer conflicts.[^s4] A 2016 dissertation that compressed the intervention into about three months found no reliable benefit, suggesting dose and time matter.[^s5] Benefits also depend on context and person: reappraisal ability helped with uncontrollable stress but not controllable stress, and a three-study 2017 follow-up (N = 429) found it went with less depression for people of lower socioeconomic status but not for those of higher status.[^s2][^s6] The workplace evidence on deep acting is largely correlational and does not test reappraisal directly.[^s3] Hence `mixed`: a real effect whose value depends on the situation.

## Connections

- **Broader:** emotion regulation, the umbrella skill; reappraisal is its cognitive-change strategy.[^s1]
- **Explained by** Gross's process model, which places reappraisal early in the emotion sequence.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with** expressive suppression, which hides a feeling without changing it.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Finkel and colleagues' 2013 conflict-reappraisal trial.[^s4]
- **Applied in** difficult conversations, the disagreements the 2013 trial targeted,[^s4] and client communication, where deep acting is linked to customer satisfaction.[^s3]
- **Challenged by** Troy, Shallcross and Mauss 2013, which showed reappraisal can hurt when the stressor is controllable,[^s2] and by McManaman's 2016 non-replication of a shortened intervention.[^s5]
- **Related:** self-distancing and perspective-taking supply alternative viewpoints; labeling emotions names the feeling first; perception checking tests whether your reading is right; receiving feedback and defensiveness are where reappraisal most often pays off.[^s1][^s4]
