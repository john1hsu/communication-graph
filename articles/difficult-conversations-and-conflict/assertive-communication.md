# Assertive communication

Assertive communication is saying what you think, feel, want or will not do - directly, openly and honestly - without trampling on the other person's right to do the same.[^s5] It sits between two failure modes. The passive style withholds the concern, so colleagues cannot understand it or respond to it; the aggressive style demands, blames or belittles, so they feel attacked and get defensive.[^s5] Assertiveness is not a polite word for aggression, and it is not a fixed personality trait: it is a set of behaviours that can be learned and rehearsed.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

The idea came out of behaviour therapy. Salter's 1949 *Conditioned Reflex Therapy* urged inhibited clients to voice their feelings, including through "I" statements; Wolpe (1958) used self-assertion to reduce anxiety and drew the line between assertion and aggression; and Lazarus (1971) described it as social competence made of four abilities - expressing wants, saying no, expressing feelings, and starting, sustaining and ending conversations.[^s1] Alberti and Emmons's 1970 *Your Perfect Right* took it to a general audience, and Goldfried and Davison (1976) added a cognitive angle: people often stay quiet because they overestimate what speaking up will cost them.[^s1] Many of these tools now live inside larger programmes under other names, such as "interpersonal effectiveness" in dialectical behaviour therapy.[^s1]

## Why it matters

At work, both extremes have a price, and they are different prices. In three studies, Ames and Flynn found an inverted-U: leaders seen as very low or very high in assertiveness were rated less effective, and observers often described colleagues as having too much or too little of it.[^s2] Too little cost *instrumental* outcomes (goals not reached); too much cost *social* outcomes (damaged relationships).[^s2] Skilled assertive communication is the attempt to get the task result without paying the relationship cost.

## What good looks like

A clear assertive message usually has four moves, often taught as DESC: **Describe** the situation, **Express** how it affects you, offer a **Specific** request or compromise, and keep the **Conversation** going (or name the consequence).[^s5]

- **Before (passive):** In a planning meeting, an analyst is handed a third urgent report this week. "Sure, no problem, I'll try to fit it in." She works until midnight and the quarterly forecast slips.
- **Before (aggressive):** "This is ridiculous - you always dump things on me at the last minute."
- **After (assertive):** "This is the third urgent request this week, and I can't do it and still finish the forecast by Friday. Which should come first? If it's the report, I'll move the forecast to Tuesday."
- **Why it works:** it names the facts and the trade-off, asks for a decision, offers a way forward and blames no one, so the manager can respond to the problem rather than defend themselves.[^s5]

Good assertive communicators also acknowledge the other person's point before restating their own, ask questions instead of assuming, request time to think instead of agreeing on the spot, and keep voice, eye contact and posture steady rather than loud or apologetic.[^s1][^s5]

## How to practise it

1. **Write the one-sentence ask first.** Before a tense meeting, finish "What I need is..." or "I can't..." so the core message survives nerves.[^s5]
2. **Draft it in DESC order** and strip out "always", "never" and character labels.[^s5]
3. **Rehearse aloud or role-play** with a colleague; behavioural rehearsal and modelling are the core of assertiveness training, and the practice doubles as exposure to the situations you dread.[^s1]
4. **Build a ladder.** Start with low-stakes cases (sending back a wrong order, asking a question in a small meeting) and move up to pushing back on a senior stakeholder.[^s1][^s5]
5. **Check your calibration.** After a negotiation or pushback, ask a trusted participant how you came across instead of trusting your own read.[^s3]

## Evidence

The training evidence is real but dated. Speed, Goldstein and Goldfried's 2018 review found that assertiveness training reduced social anxiety and depressive symptoms and improved self-esteem and couples' satisfaction across many small trials, and meta-analyses that included a handful of assertiveness studies found social-skills training beat wait-list controls and matched other therapies; most of that work is clinical, and research output roughly halved after 2000.[^s1]

Self-perception is a weak guide. In Ames and Wazlawek's four studies (2014), including mock negotiations between MBA students, more than half of the people counterparts saw as under-assertive thought they had been fine or too pushy, and a similar share of those seen as over-assertive thought they had been fine or too soft.[^s3] Many who were seen as appropriately assertive believed they had crossed a line - a "line-crossing illusion" - and then made costly concessions to repair a relationship that was not damaged.[^s3]

Context changes the reception. Gender stereotypes mean explicitly dominant behaviour from women can cost likability; Williams and Tiedens's *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis found the backlash weakens when dominance is expressed implicitly (voice, posture, negotiating style), and women face less penalty when advocating for their team than for themselves.[^s4] The authors present these as partial workarounds, not a fix for an unfair standard.[^s4] Norms for directness and eye contact also vary by culture, so the "right" level is set by the setting as well as the speaker.[^s1][^s5] Overall, treat the evidence as **mixed**: assertive skills can be trained and the passive and aggressive extremes are costly, but the optimum is moderate, culture- and gender-dependent, and hard to judge from the inside.[^s1][^s2][^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** communication competence - Lazarus framed assertiveness as a component of social competence.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Ames and Flynn (2007), the curvilinear leadership studies, and Ames and Wazlawek (2014), the line-crossing illusion in negotiation.[^s2][^s3]
- **Supported by** Speed, Goldstein and Goldfried's 2018 review of assertiveness training outcomes.[^s1]
- **Contrasts with** aggressive communication, which pursues the same goals through demands, blame and put-downs.[^s5]
- **Influenced by** Joseph Wolpe's behaviour therapy, which first separated assertion from aggression.[^s1]
- **Applied in** negotiation and difficult conversations, where under- and over-assertiveness both cost outcomes.[^s3][^s5]
- **Related:** I-statements, saying no and graded assertiveness are specific assertive techniques; the Thomas-Kilmann model uses assertiveness as one of its two axes; assertiveness is the underlying disposition; employee silence is the passive failure at organisational scale; behaviour modeling training is one way to build the skill; the illusion of transparency is a cousin of the line-crossing illusion.[^s1][^s3][^s5]
