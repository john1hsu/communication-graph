steps:
  - Before you answer, check the facts; deny only what you are confident did not happen, and acknowledge any part that did.
  - Decide what the accusation is about - your honesty or intentions, or your skill - because a denial helps most against integrity charges and an apology usually serves competence lapses better.
  - State the denial early, in one plain first-person sentence that names the specific allegation, rather than staying silent or saying "no comment".
  - Give the evidence or the route to it (records, timestamps, who can confirm) and invite the other person to check it.
  - Keep the tone calm and avoid attacking the accuser; acknowledge their concern and the effect it had on them.
  - If new evidence shows you were wrong, correct the record at once and switch to an apology.
example:
  situation: A team lead hears from her director that a peer has said she took credit in a steering meeting for analysis the peer's team produced.
  before: "I really don't want to get into he-said-she-said. I'm sure there was some misunderstanding, and I'm sorry if anyone felt sidelined."
  after: "I didn't present that analysis as mine. The slide footer credits Marco's team, and the meeting notes record me naming them when I introduced it - I've attached both. I can see why it would be upsetting if he heard otherwise, and I'm happy to talk with him directly so we clear it up."
  why: The vague non-answer and conditional apology read as an admission of a dishonesty charge, which the research suggests is the costliest kind; the rewrite names the allegation, denies it in the first person, points to checkable evidence and offers a constructive next step without attacking the colleague.

# Denial

Denial is the response of stating that you did not do what you are accused of. In trust-repair research it is one of the main verbal responses to an allegation, alongside apology (admitting fault and signalling change) and reticence (declining to confirm or deny).[^s1][^s2] It lowers how guilty you appear but, unlike an apology, offers no promise that anything will change, so it only pays off when the accusation is false and you can show it.[^s1] This article covers truthful denial; denying something that did happen is deception, and the evidence shows it tends to fail as well.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

The workplace evidence comes from a 2004 *Journal of Applied Psychology* paper by Peter Kim, Donald Ferrin, Cecily Cooper and Kurt Dirks, who tested apology against denial in two hiring-scenario experiments with 200 and 444 participants.[^s1] Ferrin and colleagues added "no comment" as a third option in 2007.[^s2] In parallel, crisis-communication scholars such as Timothy Coombs listed denial, together with attacking the accuser, as the most defensive organizational response, suited mainly to rumours.[^s3][^s5]

## Why it matters

How you answer an accusation can matter as much as the facts. In Kim and colleagues' studies, an unproven allegation alone lowered trust in a job candidate.[^s1] When the accusation concerned integrity (deliberately filing a wrong tax return), denial restored trust better than apology; when it concerned competence (not knowing the tax rules), apology worked better.[^s1] The authors explain this through how people judge character: one dishonest act is taken as strong evidence of low integrity, so admitting it confirms the worst, whereas one poor performance is easily treated as a one-off.[^s1] Staying silent is no safe middle path: reticence did no better than the weaker of the two responses in either case.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Start with the facts, not the tactic. Response and evidence interact: when later evidence showed guilt, trust fell further after a denial than after an apology, and the mismatch hurt most for integrity charges.[^s1] So deny only what you are sure of, say it early and plainly, and point to evidence others can check.[^s1][^s2] Separate the honesty question from any real mistake: you might deny that you hid a defect while owning that you missed it in testing.[^s1] In an organizational crisis, keep denial for rumours and false claims; when the organization caused the harm, more accommodating responses such as corrective action or a full apology are expected.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Denying something that happened.** In two vignette studies with 918 students and working adults about a broken promise of a 5% raise, every repair tactic improved trust and eased negative feelings except an HR reply denying the promise was ever made.[^s4]
- **Hiding behind "no comment".** Observers read reticence negatively even though it only asks them to wait.[^s2]
- **Attacking the accuser.** Coombs and Holladay class this alongside denial as a defensive posture; confronting the person rarely helps a colleague relationship.[^s3]
- **Apologizing by reflex for an honesty charge you believe is false.** An apology in that case confirms the trait you most need to dispel.[^s1]

## Evidence

The competence-versus-integrity pattern appeared in both 2004 experiments and again across two 2007 studies in the United States and Singapore, but all used hypothetical scenarios with students, so field evidence is thin.[^s1][^s2] Crisis findings are weaker: in a Ghent University experiment, deny responses scored only marginally below rebuild responses (p = .087), and the predicted match between deny strategies and victim crises was not supported; people with an external locus of control judged denying companies less harshly.[^s5] Hence the `mixed` status: denial works under narrow conditions, not as a general defence.

## Connections

Denial **contrasts with apology**, its main alternative after an accusation, and the trade-off between them is **demonstrated by** Kim et al. (2004).[^s1] Ferrin et al. (2007) **demonstrated** that **reticence** is an **alternative** that usually performs worse.[^s2] It is **applied in crisis communication** as the most defensive response option.[^s3] It **contrasts with social accounts** such as excuses, which admit the event but explain it, and which did repair trust where denial did not.[^s4] It is **explained by attribution theory**: people infer character from behaviour, and weigh negative integrity evidence heavily.[^s1] It sits within **trust repair** and connects to **defensiveness**, **credibility**, **face and facework**, and **delivering bad news**.[^s1][^s3]
