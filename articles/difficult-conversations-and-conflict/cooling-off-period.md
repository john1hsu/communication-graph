steps:
  - Notice the trigger - a racing pulse, clenched jaw, or an urge to reply right now - and rate your anger from 1 to 10.
  - Say or send a short holding line ("Thanks, I want to give this proper thought and will reply by 3 pm") instead of your full reaction.
  - If you must write, draft the reply with the recipient field empty, or save it unsent.
  - Spend the gap on an unrelated task, a walk or other work, not on rereading the message or rehearsing comebacks to colleagues.
  - Before you return, ask what outcome you actually want from this exchange and what the other person may have meant.
  - Come back when your rating has dropped to a 3 or 4, reread the original and your draft, then cut anything written to wound and send or say the considered version.
example:
  situation: A project lead gets a terse message from a peer in another department, copied to both their managers, saying her team "missed the handover again" when the delay was caused by the peer's late inputs.

  after: "Reply to the peer only: 'Got it - I'll come back to you by 2 pm.' After lunch and an hour on other work: 'Hi Sam, I want to fix the handover too. On our side, we received the inputs on Tuesday rather than Friday, which pushed us back. Could we agree a cut-off date for inputs and a quick check-in the day before? Happy to talk it through.'"

# Cooling-off period

A cooling-off period is a deliberate gap between a provocation and your response: you acknowledge the message, step away, and reply or decide only once the anger has subsided.[^s6] The point is not to forget the grievance but to plan a considered response instead of a reactive one.[^s6] At work, the classic case is the email that makes your face hot; the cooling-off version is a short holding reply now and the real answer later.[^s7] Experiments suggest that what you do during the pause matters as much as the pause itself.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Cooling-off periods are an old tool of dispute resolution, used in labour disputes, public-policy conflicts and family disputes, and sometimes imposed by law or a third party; mediators use private caucuses for the same purpose when talks overheat.[^s6] Negotiation author William Ury describes the personal version as "going to the balcony", mentally stepping back from a heated exchange.[^s6] Behavioural economists began testing the idea in the lab around 2008-2011, using bargaining games in which people often turn down unfair offers out of anger even though it costs them money.[^s1][^s4]

## Why it matters

Hot emotional states distort decisions, and people in a hot state are poor at predicting what they would choose once calm; George Loewenstein's work on hot-cold empathy gaps argues that a cooler state supports better long-run choices.[^s5] Angry people are more likely to make decisions they later regret, such as retaliating, conceding or walking away.[^s6] In Grimm and Mengel's ultimatum-game study (168 students), responders deciding at once accepted the lowest offers rarely or never, but after a delay of about ten minutes 60-80% of low offers were accepted.[^s1] Across five experiments, Wang and colleagues (2011) found that a time delay reduced costly punishment of non-cooperators, largely by lowering negative emotion.[^s2]

## How to apply it

Name the state before acting: one practical routine is to rate your anger from 1 to 10 and wait until it falls to about 3 or 4 before making any significant move.[^s7] Buy the time openly with a holding line and a reply-by time, and draft offline if writing helps you think; the aim is to plan a considered response, not to drop the issue.[^s6] Spend the gap on something absorbing and unrelated: people who did a distraction task during a delay punished less, while those who spent it ruminating on their anger punished more.[^s2] Using the gap to reappraise the situation left punishment unchanged.[^s2] In negotiations, dyads that used a break for an unrelated task reached better agreements than those who mulled over the negotiation, and if you do think about the dispute, think cooperatively about joint gains rather than about winning.[^s3] When you return, reread both the original and your draft and decide what outcome you want.[^s7]

## Common mistakes

- **Stewing instead of cooling.** A pause filled with replaying the slight or venting to colleagues can backfire and leave you angrier than before.[^s2][^s7]
- **Planning competitive moves during a break.** Competitive thinking during a negotiation break produced worse agreements than cooperative thinking.[^s3]
- **Treating the pause as a cure-all.** In a large online study, a 24-hour cooling-off period barely changed rejection of unfair offers when stakes were low; it helped only when stakes felt high.[^s4]
- **A pause that never ends.** Cooling-off only works if it is honoured and followed by a real response; the grievance is postponed, not forgotten, and cooling-off arrangements are hard to make stick in practice.[^s6]

## Evidence

The evidence is consistent in direction but limited, so the status here is `mixed`. Grimm and Mengel's effect was large but came from one student sample, and emotion was inferred rather than measured.[^s1] Wang and colleagues measured emotion and showed that delay helps mainly through distraction, not rumination.[^s2] Harinck and De Dreu's two experiments (140 and 76 participants) found that the mindset during a break decides whether it helps.[^s3] Oechssler, Roider and Schmitz's study of about 1,250 online participants found no meaningful cooling-off effect with ordinary cash stakes, a finding that challenges how general the benefit is.[^s4] Most studies use economic games rather than real workplace messages, so effects on email and meetings are extrapolated.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

A cooling-off period is a form of emotion regulation used within conflict resolution.[^s2][^s6] It addresses the hot side of the hot-cold empathy gap by moving the decision into a calmer state.[^s5] It was demonstrated by Grimm and Mengel (2011), supported by Wang and colleagues (2011) and Harinck and De Dreu (2008), and challenged by Oechssler, Roider and Schmitz (2008).[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s4] It is applied in negotiation and in heated, difficult conversations, and pairs naturally with de-escalation, which calms the other person, and self-distancing and labeling emotions, which calm you.[^s3][^s6]
