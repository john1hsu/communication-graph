# Difficult conversation

A difficult conversation is any exchange you would rather put off because something important feels at risk: the working relationship, your standing, the other person's sense of competence, or emotions you are not sure you can manage. At work it covers telling a peer their part of a deliverable is late again, pushing back on a manager's decision, giving a client bad news, or clearing the air after a meeting went wrong. Treating it as a recognisable situation, rather than a one-off ordeal, lets you prepare for it the same way each time.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

The best-known practical model comes from *Difficult Conversations: How to Discuss What Matters Most* by Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton and Sheila Heen, which PON dates to a 2000 edition.[^s2] Patton co-founded the Harvard Negotiation Project, Heen is its deputy director and Stone lectures at Harvard Law School; Roger Fisher wrote the foreword, and a third edition in 2023 added material on race, gender, power and technology-mediated communication.[^s5] The authors argue that every such conversation is really three: a "what happened?" conversation about facts, intentions and blame; a feelings conversation; and an identity conversation about what the situation says about each person.[^s1] Their firm's preparation worksheet turns that model into a set of questions to work through beforehand.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Avoidance is the default, and it is costly. In interviews with 40 professionals, Milliken, Morrison and Hewlin (2003) found that 34 had at some point felt unable to raise an important concern with their boss; the most common reasons were fear of being labelled a complainer or troublemaker and fear of damaging a valued relationship.[^s4] The issues withheld - a colleague's performance, flawed processes, disagreement with decisions - are exactly the ones managers most need to hear.[^s4] The authors note the study is small, retrospective and drawn from part-time MBA students, so the figures are illustrative rather than population estimates.[^s4]

People also misjudge how these conversations will feel. In a 2018 field experiment and laboratory studies, Levine and Cohen found that people predicted honest conversations would be less enjoyable, less connecting and more harmful to relationships than participants who actually had them reported; the forecasting error came largely from overestimating how negatively the other person would react.[^s3] A separate series of 12 experiments (Kardas, Kumar and Epley, 2021, over 1,800 participants) found people expected deeper conversations with strangers to be more awkward than they were.[^s6] Both lines of work used mostly student or online samples and did not test workplace disputes directly, so they show a general bias toward overestimating discomfort rather than a guarantee that a given talk will go well.[^s3][^s6]

## What works here

Prepare by sorting the three conversations before you speak. On "what happened", treat your account as one story among two, map what each of you contributed instead of who is to blame, and separate the other person's intent from the impact on you - people tend to judge others by impact and themselves by intent.[^s1] On feelings, name your own and imagine theirs; you do not have to voice them, but if you do, express a feeling rather than a judgment.[^s1] On identity, notice what the conversation threatens about how you see yourself (competent, fair, a good colleague) so one bad exchange does not knock you off balance.[^s1]

Open from the "third story": describe the difference between your view and theirs the way a neutral observer would, then invite them to explore it with you.[^s2] PON's example of two sales representatives shows the shift - instead of accusing a colleague of missing report deadlines, one rep says they seem to see deadlines differently, explains why the deadline matters to him, and asks how his colleague sees it, which surfaces an interest (chasing a hot lead) the two can then trade around.[^s2]

A workplace version: rather than "You keep ignoring the review process," a project lead might say, "I think we see the design review differently - I'd like to understand how you see it, and explain why it matters to me before release." Then listen first, speak for yourself second, and problem-solve only once both stories are on the table - the order the worksheet recommends.[^s1][^s2]

## Pitfalls

- **Arguing the facts first.** Leading with your version of events invites a counter-version; the worksheet asks you to explore each other's stories before judging them.[^s1]
- **Equating intent with impact.** Assuming a colleague meant the harm you felt, or defending your good intentions while ignoring your impact, keeps the "what happened" conversation stuck.[^s1]
- **Waiting for a perfect moment.** Because people overestimate how badly honest talk will land, delay tends to feel safer than it is, while the unspoken issue persists.[^s3][^s4]
- **Skipping the identity question.** If the talk threatens how you see yourself, you are more likely to over-defend or capitulate; naming that threat beforehand helps you stay steady.[^s1]
- **Ignoring the channel.** The 2023 edition added guidance on technology-mediated conversations, and the worksheet's listen-first sequence is hard to follow in a one-way message; choose a channel that lets you hear their story.[^s5][^s1]

## Connections

- **Explained by** the three-conversations framework from Triad Consulting's worksheet: what happened, feelings and identity.[^s1]
- **Introduced in** *Difficult Conversations* by Stone, Patton and Heen, which popularised the term as a workplace and personal skill area.[^s5]
- **Demonstrated by** Levine and Cohen (2018), Milliken, Morrison and Hewlin (2003) and, more indirectly, Kardas, Kumar and Epley (2021), which show that people avoid hard conversations and overestimate their costs.[^s3][^s4][^s6]
- **Studied in** conflict resolution: PON treats the third story as a mediator's move any party can borrow.[^s2]
- **Requires** active listening, because the learning stance depends on hearing the other story before telling yours.[^s1] Other articles in this graph apply I-statements and paraphrasing here; the topic also relates to crucial conversations, nonviolent communication, delivering bad news, feedback, defensiveness and psychological safety, the team climate that makes these conversations less risky.[^s1][^s4]
