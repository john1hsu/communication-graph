# Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument

The Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument (TKI) is both a model and a short self-assessment of how people handle conflict, meaning any situation where two people's concerns seem incompatible.[^s1] It places behaviour on two independent dimensions - assertiveness, how far you pursue your own concerns, and cooperativeness, how far you pursue the other person's - and names five modes: competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding and accommodating.[^s1][^s5] Its practical message is that each mode fits some situations and that most people over-rely on one or two by habit.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Kenneth W. Thomas and Ralph H. Kilmann met at UCLA in the early 1970s and built the instrument from Robert Blake and Jane Mouton's 1964 five-mode conflict scheme, part of their managerial grid.[^s1] Thomas recast that scheme on the assertiveness and cooperativeness axes, and the instrument was published as a booklet by Xicom in 1974; CPP (now The Myers-Briggs Company) acquired it in 1998 and moved it online in 2002.[^s1] Their key design change was forced choice: earlier instruments let people rate themselves on obviously "good" statements, so over 90% of managers ranked collaborating highest and avoiding lowest, whereas the TKI's 30 paired statements were matched for social desirability.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

Conflict at work is unavoidable, and how it is handled shapes individual well-being, team performance and organisational effectiveness.[^s4] The TKI gives teams a shared, non-judgmental vocabulary: instead of "you're difficult", people can say "you tend to compete and I tend to avoid".[^s1][^s5] It also explains clashes in negotiation, where people tend to assume the other side shares their style; a cooperative negotiator who shares information can be read as naive by a competitor and exploited, which breeds anger and mistrust.[^s5]

## How to apply it

Diagnose before you act, then choose the mode deliberately.[^s1][^s5]

- **Competing** (assertive, uncooperative) - treating the conflict as a contest and claiming as much as possible for yourself; it can protect your concerns, but as a habit it turns every disagreement into win-lose.[^s5]
- **Collaborating** (assertive, cooperative) - digging beneath positions to the interests underneath and looking for an option that gives each side more of what it values; it tends to work well for the conflict and for long-term relationships.[^s5]
- **Compromising** (middle on both) - splitting the difference to move the discussion forward, at the risk of leaving underlying interests unmet and the agreement fragile.[^s5]
- **Avoiding** (unassertive, uncooperative) - postponing or sidestepping; it can help in the short term, but avoided issues often come back, sometimes more intensely.[^s5]
- **Accommodating** (unassertive, cooperative) - yielding to the other person's concerns; it looks agreeable, but neglecting your own interests again and again breeds frustration and resentment.[^s5]

In practice: name the mode you are drifting toward, weigh how much your own concerns and the other person's matter on this issue (the two dimensions), and pick the mode that fits the situation and the phase of the conflict; if collaboration stalls, move to a stated fallback such as a time-boxed compromise.[^s1][^s5] In negotiations, test the other side on small issues first - do they reciprocate concessions and share information? - and adapt your approach rather than trying to convert their style.[^s5] With a senior stakeholder, competing carries more risk and avoiding is more tempting; in email, collaboration is harder because interests are easier to explore live.

## Common mistakes

Treating the result as a fixed personality type is the biggest misuse: the instrument describes habitual preferences, and the authors' own materials stress knowing when each mode is most useful.[^s1] A second is treating collaborating as always best - the very bias the forced-choice format was designed to remove - when different modes suit different phases and types of conflict.[^s1][^s2][^s5] A third is reading high and low scores as absolute amounts; because each answer trades one mode against another, scores show relative preference only.[^s3]

## Evidence

In the 1977 validation study with 86 graduate students, social desirability explained about 17% of the variance in TKI mode scores versus over 90% for three older instruments, though the TKI did not stop individuals who answer in a flattering way.[^s2] Reliability was moderate: average internal consistency of .60 (low for accommodating) and four-week test-retest of .64, better than the alternatives but short of the levels usually wanted for decisions about individuals.[^s2] A 2014 technical brief from the publisher reported two-week test-retest of .61 to .68 for the North American English version and lower figures for some translations, falling as low as .15 for one Chinese-language scale, with small validation samples.[^s3] Independent researchers using dual-concern measures such as the Dutch Test for Conflict Handling have found that self, opponent and observer ratings converge for problem solving, forcing and yielding but not for avoiding.[^s4] The TKI is a sound conversation starter, best used for self-reflection and team discussion rather than selection or performance decisions.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

The model was introduced by Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann and grew out of Blake and Mouton's managerial grid; its 1977 validation study is its main empirical support.[^s1][^s2] The Dutch Test for Conflict Handling is an alternative, theory-based workplace measure of the same family of strategies.[^s4] It is applied in difficult conversations and negotiation, where collaborating corresponds to integrative negotiation and interest-based bargaining, and competing to distributive negotiation.[^s5] It sits under interpersonal conflict and the field of conflict resolution.[^s4]
