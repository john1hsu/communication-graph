steps:
  - Check safety first - yours and others' - and if a threat or hostile act looks imminent, step away and call security or emergency services instead of talking.
  - Regulate yourself before you speak - slow your breathing, lower your volume and pace, and keep a neutral, attentive face.
  - Give physical room - stay about two arm's lengths away, do not block the exit, and keep your hands visible, open and low.
  - Have one person do the talking; introduce yourself and say plainly that you want to help sort it out.
  - Keep sentences short and simple, and repeat the key message calmly rather than escalating your wording.
  - Ask what they want and how they feel, listen closely, and name the feeling or need you hear before you explain anything.
  - Agree with whatever you honestly can (the facts, the principle, that it is frustrating), or agree to disagree.
  - State any limit matter-of-factly as a boundary on behaviour, not a threat, and offer realistic choices about what happens next.
  - Afterwards, write down what was said and done factually, report through the proper channel, and debrief with the person and your team.
example:
  situation: A supplier's account manager arrives unannounced at a reception desk, raising their voice because an invoice is 60 days overdue, and a facilities coordinator is first to respond.
  before: "Sir, you need to calm down right now. Shouting won't get you paid any faster, and if you keep this up I'll have security walk you out."
  after: "I'm Sam, I coordinate this site. Sixty days unpaid is a real problem, and I want to help get it moving. Would you sit with me over here so I can take down the invoice number? I can't change payments myself, but I can get it to someone who can by this afternoon."

# De-escalation

De-escalation is the use of communication - words, tone, pace, posture and distance - to stabilise a situation in which someone is becoming agitated or aggressive, so that it can be resolved without physical force or with as little as possible.[^s1] A 2017 concept analysis of the healthcare literature found it is not one move but a bundle of interwoven parts: communication, self-regulation, assessment, concrete actions and maintaining safety.[^s3] In ordinary workplaces it is the skill you need when a customer, supplier or colleague is shouting, pacing or making threats, and the usual problem-solving conversation has become impossible.

## Origin

The approach comes mainly from psychiatric nursing, emergency medicine and policing, where staff routinely face agitated people and coercion (restraint, seclusion, force) carries real harm.[^s3] An influential version is the 2012 consensus statement of the American Association for Emergency Psychiatry's Project BETA workgroup, led by Janet Richmond, which set out ten domains: respect personal space, do not be provocative, establish verbal contact, be concise, identify wants and feelings, listen closely, agree or agree to disagree, set clear limits, offer choices and optimism, and debrief.[^s2] The U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) later adapted the idea for non-clinical staff at offices, venues and public sites as a four-phase cycle: recognise, assess, de-escalate, report.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Agitation is contagious and quickly makes reasoning impossible on both sides; commands, sarcasm and humiliation tend to raise it further, and the Project BETA authors treat humiliating someone as itself an aggressive act.[^s2] The consensus authors report that verbal de-escalation frequently works in under five minutes, which is far faster and cheaper - in injuries, complaints and lost relationships - than calling security or using force.[^s2] Even when it fails, a calm attempt plus a factual report gives the organisation what it needs to act on a potential threat.[^s1]

## How to apply it

Safety comes first: CISA tells untrained staff to step away and report rather than engage if violence looks imminent, and to know their limits.[^s1] Then manage yourself - a relaxed but alert stance, hands open and visible, slow movements, and a voice whose volume and pace you deliberately lower.[^s1] Respect personal space (about two arm's lengths, with a clear exit for both of you) and have only one person speak.[^s2]

The talking itself is short and repetitive by design: brief sentences, simple words, the same calm message repeated rather than escalated.[^s2] Ask what the person wants, listen for feelings and needs, and find something you can honestly agree with - the facts, the principle, or simply that the situation is frustrating.[^s2] Limits are stated as a matter of fact about behaviour, paired with realistic choices and honest optimism; never promise what you cannot deliver.[^s2] Afterwards, report observed behaviour and words rather than assumptions or a person's characteristics, and debrief the people involved.[^s1][^s2]

## Common mistakes

- **Telling someone to calm down.** It challenges them and signals you are not listening; the consensus guidance warns against provocative, challenging language and staring.[^s2]
- **Crowding or cornering.** Standing too close or blocking a doorway can feel threatening, especially to someone with past trauma.[^s2]
- **A chorus of responders.** Several people talking at once overloads an agitated listener; one voice should lead.[^s2]
- **Threats dressed as limits.** "Do that again and you're out" escalates; a consequence that is clearly tied to the behaviour and stated calmly does not.[^s2]
- **Heroics.** Trying to talk down a person who is about to become violent when you are untrained or alone ignores the first rule: safety and getting help.[^s1]

## Evidence

The evidence is thinner than the confidence with which de-escalation is recommended. The Project BETA domains rest on expert consensus and a limited literature, and its authors note that few studies describe specific techniques or test their efficacy.[^s2] Hallett and Dickens found most de-escalation research is qualitative with small samples, that none of the proposed theories of how it works had empirical support, and that no agreed gold-standard practice exists.[^s3] A 2015 systematic review of 38 studies of staff training found gains in knowledge, confidence and performance in training scenarios, but low-quality, conflicting evidence on assaults and injuries in practice.[^s4] More encouraging, a University of Cincinnati-led evaluation of police de-escalation training in Louisville, Kentucky, reported fewer use-of-force incidents and fewer injuries to residents and officers after the training, though the authors stress that training alone is not enough without supportive policy and supervision.[^s5] The fair reading is `mixed`: the principles are plausible and low-risk, but the size of their effect in real workplaces is not well measured.

## Connections

De-escalation sits under conflict resolution and is a tool for the most heated end of a difficult conversation, the setting CISA's guide is written for.[^s1] It addresses verbal aggression, the antecedent the healthcare literature organises the practice around.[^s3] It complements labeling emotions - Project BETA's "identify wants and feelings" and "listen closely" domains are the same move of naming what you hear - and it requires the basics of active listening.[^s2] It is related to defensiveness, which threatening talk provokes, and it leans on paralanguage and proxemics, since tone, pace and distance carry much of the message.[^s1][^s2] Its evidence base is challenged by the Price et al. (2015) training review and partly supported by the Louisville police training evaluation.[^s4][^s5]
