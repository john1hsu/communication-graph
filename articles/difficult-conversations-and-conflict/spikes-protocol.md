# SPIKES protocol

SPIKES is a six-step structure for telling someone news that will change how they see their future: **S**etting up the conversation, finding out the person's **P**erception, getting an **I**nvitation to share information, giving **K**nowledge, responding to **E**motions, and agreeing a **S**trategy with a summary.[^s1] It was written for doctors telling patients they have cancer, but its sequence - prepare, ask before telling, tell plainly, make room for the reaction, end with a plan - transfers to workplace news such as layoffs, reorganisations, cancelled projects and rejected bids.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

The protocol was published in *The Oncologist* in 2000 by Walter Baile, Robert Buckman and colleagues at a U.S. cancer centre.[^s6] They framed it around four goals: learn what the patient already knows, convey the medical facts, support the patient emotionally, and involve them in planning what comes next.[^s6] They noted that clinicians often feel very uncomfortable in these conversations, fearing they will destroy hope or be unprepared for the reaction, and reported that oncologists, trainees and students who learned the protocol felt more confident afterwards.[^s1][^s6] By 2019 it was the dominant teaching model: ten of twelve responding English-curriculum Canadian medical schools taught it.[^s3] Management writers have since adapted it for managers who must tell employees their jobs are ending.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Bad news delivered badly does lasting damage: a hurried hallway remark, a wall of jargon, or reassurance that arrives before the listener has absorbed the blow. SPIKES gives the messenger a checklist for exactly the moments people tend to skip under stress - checking what the other person already believes, asking how much they want to hear, and staying with their reaction instead of escaping into detail.[^s1] For managers it offers a planned, defensible way to handle layoffs that respects the person while still delivering the decision.[^s5]

## How to apply it

**Set up.** Rehearse the key sentence, choose a private space, sit down, silence your phone and state any time limit at the start.[^s1] Offer the chance to have a support person present where that fits the situation.[^s1][^s4]

**Ask before you tell.** Open questions ("What have you heard about the restructure?") reveal misunderstandings, rumours or unrealistic hopes you will need to address.[^s1][^s4] Then ask how much detail they want now; some people want the headline today and the specifics tomorrow.[^s1]

**Give the news plainly.** A short warning ("I'm afraid I have difficult news") followed by one clear sentence, then small chunks, checking understanding between them.[^s1] Avoid both euphemism and bluntness, and don't say "there's nothing we can do" when there are still things you can do.[^s1][^s4]

**Respond to the emotion first.** Observe the reaction, identify what triggered it, and make a statement that connects the two ("I can see this is a shock after two years on Atlas") before offering reassurance or logistics.[^s1] Allow silence and let the other person set the pace.[^s4]

**Close with a plan.** Once they are ready, summarise, agree next steps, schedule a follow-up and tell them how to reach you.[^s1][^s4] Patients surveyed in 2019 also wanted timely follow-up, written information and contacts for further support - elements the original steps leave implicit.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Reciting it as a script.** Recipients differ in how much detail they want and at what pace, and the steps carry no stronger backing than limited evidence and expert consensus, so treat them as a guide to adapt rather than a fixed sequence; plan for follow-up conversations rather than one.[^s4]
- **Skipping Perception.** Telling before asking means you can't correct rumours or pitch the detail to what the person already knows.[^s1]
- **Reassuring too early.** Jumping to "it's not that bad" before acknowledging the feeling tends to land as dismissal.[^s1][^s4]
- **Assuming everyone wants a warning or touch.** Not all recipients want a warning shot, and most patients in a large survey did not value empathetic touch; some found it uncomfortable.[^s3][^s4] In a workplace, physical contact is best avoided.
- **Ignoring culture.** Preferences for detail and for family or group involvement vary with age, education and cultural background.[^s4]

## Evidence

The evidence is mixed: training in SPIKES clearly improves how people deliver news, but whether it improves outcomes for recipients is largely untested. A 2018 meta-analysis of 17 controlled studies (19 comparisons, 1,322 participants) found that short training produced a large gain in observer-rated news-delivery skill (SMD 0.74) and a moderate gain in confidence (SMD 0.60); SPIKES-based programmes showed a larger skill effect (SMD 1.14) than others, which the authors called tentative.[^s2] Heterogeneity was high, only immediate post-training outcomes were measured, and just one study assessed recipients' wellbeing.[^s2] A 2018 clinical review rated recommendations in this area as based on limited evidence or expert consensus and stated that existing bad-news algorithms are not regarded as evidence-based.[^s4] On the recipient side, a 2019 survey of 1,337 patients with six different life-changing diagnoses found their preferences largely matched SPIKES - especially empathy, taking time, explaining implications and checking understanding - with the touch exception and the five gaps noted above.[^s3] Workplace use rests on adaptation and argument rather than direct trials.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Broader:** SPIKES is a specific technique for the wider situation of delivering bad news, and is applied in layoff and termination conversations.[^s1][^s5]
- **Introduced by** Walter Baile and Robert Buckman.[^s6]
- **Demonstrated by** the Johnson and Panagioti meta-analysis of bad-news training, and **supported by** the Mirza et al. patient survey.[^s2][^s3]
- **Complements** checking understanding (built into the Knowledge step) and labeling emotions (the core of the Emotions step).[^s1]
- **Related:** open-ended questions and perception checking (the Perception step), teach-back, social accounts for explaining why the news happened, and the feedback sandwich, which SPIKES's plain-statement step avoids.[^s1][^s4]
- **Requires** active listening, since the Perception, Invitation and Emotions steps depend on hearing the answer.[^s1]
