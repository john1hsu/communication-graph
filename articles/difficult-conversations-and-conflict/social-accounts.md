steps:
  - Give the explanation at the same moment as the bad news or the admission, before people fill the silence with their own story.
  - State the real reason - the constraint, trade-off or goal that drove the outcome - in concrete terms rather than "business reasons".
  - Separate honestly what was outside your control from what was your call, and own your part; never invent a mitigating factor.
  - Say how the decision will be carried out and how the people affected will be treated, not only why it was made.
  - Make the detail proportional to the loss, then ask what still does not make sense and answer it.
  - If you caused the harm, put the account inside an apology - responsibility and repair first, explanation kept short.
example:
  situation: A team lead has to tell five analysts that the conference places she had promised them this year have been cancelled.
  before: "Hi all, due to business reasons the conference budget has been cut for this year. Thanks for understanding."
  after: "I have to take back the conference places I promised you. Q3 revenue came in 12% under plan, and finance has frozen all non-customer travel across the division until March - I argued to keep two places and lost that argument. Registrations will be refunded by Friday, so none of you are out of pocket, and I've booked 15 minutes with each of you this week to agree what we do instead for your development goals. If any part of that still doesn't add up, ask me directly."
  why: The vague original gives no reason anyone could judge, so it invites cynicism; the rewrite names a specific, checkable cause outside the lead's control, owns the lead's own role, explains how people will be treated, and invites the questions that test whether the account was adequate.

# Social accounts

A social account is the explanation you give people for a mistake, a transgression or an unwelcome decision: why it happened and, ideally, how it will be handled.[^s1][^s4] Research on organizational justice treats it as a core part of fair treatment, because people judge a bad outcome partly by whether anyone bothered to give them an adequate reason for it.[^s2][^s5] Accounts come in two classic forms: an excuse, which points to causes that reduce your responsibility, and a justification, which argues the act was right given a higher goal.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Workplace research on accounts draws on the sociologists Scott and Lyman (1968), whose distinction between excuses and justifications is still used to classify explanations.[^s3] In 1987 Robert Bies and Debra Shapiro ran three studies showing that a causal account for a manager's improper action or an unfavourable outcome raised people's judgements of fairness and their support for the decision maker.[^s2] Their work helped establish explanations as part of what is now called interactional or informational justice.[^s4][^s5]

## Why it matters

A 2003 meta-analysis by Shaw, Wild and Colquitt pooled 54 independent samples and found strong effects of explanations on perceived justice and on responses such as cooperation, retaliation and withdrawal.[^s1] Explanations helped most after unfavourable outcomes, and excuses tended to work better than justifications.[^s1] This fits Joel Brockner and Batia Wiesenfeld's 1996 review of 45 samples, which found that how a decision is handled matters most when the outcome itself is bad; they note that a good explanation makes it harder for people to imagine a fairer alternative.[^s5] In practice, a layoff, a cancelled project or a missed deadline with no adequate account tends to be read as arbitrary or careless.[^s2][^s5]

## How to apply it

Bies and Shapiro found that the perceived adequacy of the account, not the bare fact of giving one, carried much of its effect.[^s2] Later work describes effective accounts as clear, thorough, detailed and personalized, and finds they work better when they cite genuine factors beyond the giver's control or unselfish motives rather than self-serving ones.[^s4] So say what actually drove the outcome, in concrete terms, and own the part that was your decision.[^s1][^s4]

Cover the "how" as well as the "why". In two online experiments with 328 and 373 adults reading about a layoff, Ashli Carter, Ramona Bobocel and Brockner found that a "why" account persuaded abstract thinkers, while concrete thinkers responded to a "how" account describing respectful treatment of the employees; an account describing only implementation steps did little.[^s4] They suggest giving both kinds of information to reach a mixed audience.[^s4]

For your own slips, the account and the apology do different jobs. Joseph Mroz and Joseph Allen showed 558 online participants a video of a colleague arriving ten minutes late to a meeting: a brief excuse raised ratings of her expected performance and others' willingness to help her (d = 0.35 for performance), while a bare apology did no better than saying nothing.[^s3] In real lateness incidents reported by 223 people, excuses and apologies usually came together.[^s3] For larger harms, use the account as short context inside an apology rather than a substitute for one.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

The commonest mistake is the empty account - "business reasons", "circumstances beyond our control" - which gives nothing to judge and may be read as no explanation at all, since adequacy drives the effect.[^s2][^s4] Others are explaining only the strategic why to people whose main question is what happens to them next, and delivering the explanation days after the news.[^s4][^s5] The ethical line matters: an excuse helps because it shifts responsibility, so it must be true; inventing a mitigating cause is deception, and the benefits reported come from accounts that cite genuine factors beyond the giver's control.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence

The core finding rests on a peer-reviewed meta-analysis and a larger justice literature, so explanations reliably improve fairness judgements.[^s1][^s5] Effects are moderated by outcome severity, the content of the account and the audience's mindset, and some individual studies have found only partial or reversed effects.[^s4][^s5] Many studies use vignettes and online samples, and in the workplace lateness study explanation type accounted for only about 3% of the variance, so the status is mixed.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

Social accounts are part of trust repair. They are applied in an apology, where Mroz and Allen show an explanation adds something a bare apology does not, and in delivering bad news, the unfavourable-outcome setting of Bies and Shapiro's field study.[^s2][^s3] The technique is demonstrated by Bies and Shapiro (1987) and by Shaw, Wild and Colquitt's (2003) meta-analysis, which uses fairness theory to explain why adequate accounts work.[^s1][^s2] It is supported by Brockner and Wiesenfeld's (1996) framework showing that fair treatment matters most when outcomes are unfavourable.[^s5] Accounts are a regular move within a difficult conversation, they can lower defensiveness when the other side feels treated arbitrarily, and tailoring why versus how information is a form of audience analysis.[^s4]
