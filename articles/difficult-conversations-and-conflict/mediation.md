# Mediation

Mediation is a way of handling a dispute in which a neutral third party helps the people involved communicate constructively and negotiate an agreement they can all accept. The mediator runs the process but does not judge the merits or impose a settlement, which is what separates it from a grievance hearing or arbitration.[^s1] In workplace use it is typically voluntary and confidential: what is said stays inside the process if no agreement is reached, and any agreement is normally not legally binding.[^s7] For most professionals the situation shows up in two forms: being one of the parties in a formally mediated dispute, or being the manager or peer asked to sit between two colleagues who have stopped working well together.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Mediation is old, but workplace mediation as an organised practice grew in the 1990s and 2000s. Large U.S. programmes run by the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission and the U.S. Postal Service (the REDRESS programme, which by one later review had involved some 66,000 complainants) were key milestones, and the European Commission promoted workplace mediation in 2002 and 2004.[^s2] Bollen and Euwema link the growth partly to the shift from collective to individual employment relationships as union membership declined.[^s2] Researchers now study it inside the wider conflict-resolution field, with Kressel's typology of facilitative, evaluative, settlement-oriented and transformative mediator styles as a common reference point.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Unresolved conflicts between colleagues tend to feed the next round of conflict, and formal procedures are slow, costly and adversarial.[^s1] Mediation is generally seen as faster and cheaper, and a 2018 study by Saundry and colleagues, reviewed by Munduate and colleagues, found it improved employees' access to a voice and reduced power gaps inside the mediation room, though with less evidence of change across the wider organisation.[^s1] Acas recommends using it early, before positions harden, and describes typical uses as communication breakdowns, personality clashes and repairing relationships after a grievance.[^s7]

## What works here

A widely used sequence, set out by both Acas and Harvard's Program on Negotiation, is:[^s5][^s6]

1. **Meet each party separately first.** Hear each account, explain the process, and confirm the person actually wants to take part.[^s5] A large study of 1,381 family and labour mediations found pre-mediation caucuses were linked to better settlement quality.[^s2]
2. **Open the joint meeting with ground rules.** Agree how people will behave and explain that the mediator will not decide who is right.[^s5][^s6]
3. **Let each person speak without interruption, then summarise.** The mediator acts like a translator, restating each side and asking for clarification, then names the points of agreement and disagreement.[^s6][^s5]
4. **Shift from past to future.** Move the discussion from who did what to how the two will work together, generating options that meet each side's core interests.[^s5][^s6]
5. **Check the agreement is workable and write it down.** Spell out who does what and how it will be reviewed, and share it only with those involved.[^s5][^s7]

*Workplace example.* Two team leads keep clashing over shared engineers. A manager asked to mediate first meets each separately and learns that one is worried about a client deadline and the other about burnout on their team. In the joint session the manager summarises both concerns neutrally, then asks what a fair allocation for the next sprint would look like. The leads agree a weekly 15-minute capacity check and a rule for escalating clashes, which the manager writes up and reviews a month later. Focusing on interests rather than rights matters: interest-based workplace conflicts reach agreement roughly 20 percentage points more often than rights-based ones (about 40%).[^s1]

## Pitfalls

- **Ignoring hierarchy.** In supervisor–subordinate disputes, supervisors report more satisfaction and subordinates are more likely to see the conflict as unresolved; a manager mediating between their own reports holds power the parties cannot ignore.[^s2][^s3]
- **Using it for the wrong cases.** Acas generally does not use mediation for disputes about pay, dismissal or conduct, and some scholars argue it is unsuitable for bullying and harassment because its future focus offers no way to address past behaviour.[^s7][^s1]
- **Rushing to a deal.** A quick problem-solving agreement may not last where relationships are badly damaged; mediators often need to shift style during the process.[^s1]
- **Breaking confidentiality.** Promise only what you can keep, and agree up front what may be shared outside the room.[^s7][^s6]

## Evidence and limits

The evidence is real but thin. Bollen and Euwema found only thirteen empirical studies of workplace mediation meeting their criteria, mostly post-mediation satisfaction surveys with few comparison groups or observations.[^s2] Wall and Dunne's broader review likewise found too few studies comparing specific mediator strategies or observing real sessions.[^s4] Where outcomes have been tracked, a one-year follow-up of Dutch hierarchical mediations found short- and long-term outcomes were positively related, with no difference between mediations where the employee stayed and those where they left; supervisors, however, reported more compliance with the agreement than subordinates did.[^s3] Satisfaction figures are high in programme surveys (for example, 90% of participants in one city-government sample would use mediation again), but these are self-reports.[^s2]

## Connections

Mediation is a narrower, third-party-assisted form of `cm:negotiation` and is **studied in** `cm:conflict-resolution`, the field that frames it as a constructive conflict-management tool.[^s1] It **complements** `cm:interests-vs-positions`, since interest-based disputes settle more often than rights-based ones,[^s1] and it **complements** `cm:paraphrasing`, the mediator's core move of restating each side.[^s6] Its long-term value is **supported by** Kalter, Bollen and Euwema's one-year follow-up (`cm:kalter-bollen-euwema-2018`),[^s3] while the strength of the evidence base is **challenged by** Bollen and Euwema's review (`cm:bollen-euwema-2013`), which calls workplace mediation an underdeveloped research area.[^s2] Reading `cm:active-listening` first helps, because both the mediator and the parties rely on it.[^s6]
