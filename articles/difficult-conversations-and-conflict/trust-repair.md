# Trust repair

Trust repair is what you do after a breach: a missed commitment, a mistake that cost someone, a broken promise or a discovered lie. The aim is not to make the other person feel better for a moment but to restore their willingness to rely on you again.[^s4] Research groups the moves into short-term verbal and financial responses (excuses, apologies, denials, paying back a loss) and longer-term changes to the relationship (contracts, monitoring, reframing, forgiveness, and sometimes silence).[^s4]

## Origin

Organizational researchers turned trust repair into a distinct field in the 2000s, after earlier work had focused mostly on how trust is built.[^s4] Most early studies looked at one violator and one victim in the lab; Nicole Gillespie and Graham Dietz argued in 2009 that repairing employees' trust after an organization-level failure needs a multilevel, systemic approach instead.[^s2] Reinhard Bachmann, Gillespie and Richard Priem later widened this to institutions, proposing six repair mechanisms and concluding that none works alone.[^s3]

## Why it matters

At work, trust is what lets a manager delegate without checking, a client accept your estimate, or a peer share a half-formed idea. A breach makes people protect themselves: they monitor, escalate, or stop telling you things.[^s4] How you respond decides whether the damage is temporary. In a 2006 trust-game experiment with 262 participants, trust damaged by untrustworthy actions alone recovered once the other player behaved well, but trust damaged by deception plus those actions never fully recovered.[^s1]

## What works here

**Acknowledge fast, with facts.** For organization-level failures, Gillespie and Dietz's first stage is a timely, accurate, transparent acknowledgement with regret and a commitment to investigate, plus urgent containment of any ongoing harm.[^s2]

**Diagnose before you prescribe.** Their second stage is a genuine investigation. They propose that both a rushed and a slow diagnosis hurt trust.[^s2] Bachmann and colleagues call this sense-making: a shared, credible account of what went wrong.[^s3]

**Match the fix to the breach.** Gillespie and Dietz propose that after an ability failure (a competence lapse), demonstrating trustworthiness through apology, reparation and consistent good performance works better; after an integrity or benevolence failure, people also need distrust regulation: controls, monitoring or sanctions that make a repeat harder.[^s2] Treat this as a theoretical proposition, not a tested rule.

**Back words with something concrete.** In two scenario experiments with 918 students and working adults, every repair tactic except denial improved trust after a broken promise about pay or promotion, and full penance (making the person whole) worked best.[^s5] In the 2006 trust game, a promise to change sped up early recovery, while a bare apology with no explanation or restitution had no measurable effect.[^s1]

**Then let behaviour carry it.** Long-term recovery in the trust game came from consistent trustworthy actions; the promise's advantage faded once those were in place.[^s1]

A workplace example: a team lead misses a vendor deadline that delays a colleague's launch. A weak response is "Sorry for the hold-up, things were hectic." A stronger one names the miss and its cost, explains the cause briefly, offers to cover the extra review work this week, and commits to a shared deadline tracker with a check-in every Friday, then actually holds those check-ins, combining an account, a concrete repair and a structural change.[^s4][^s5]

## Pitfalls

**Denying what you did.** Denial was the only tactic that failed to improve trust in the 2020 experiments.[^s5]

**Lying to smooth things over.** Prior deception weakened the early effect of later promises and left a lasting trust deficit.[^s1]

**Fixing one thing in isolation.** Gillespie and Dietz's congruence principle says repair signals from leadership, culture, strategy and processes must line up; an apology from the top while incentives stay the same reads as hollow.[^s2]

**Relying on rules alone.** Formal controls can stifle flexibility and may not fix integrity-based failures; transparency can be selective and accountability can create perverse incentives.[^s3]

**Overgeneralising the evidence.** Much of the research uses lab games or written scenarios with strangers, one-way messages and short time frames, so effect sizes in long work relationships are uncertain.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

Trust repair is a kind of **difficult conversation** and often starts with **delivering bad news** you caused; apology, social accounts and service recovery are its narrower forms.[^s4] It **is demonstrated by** Schweitzer, Hershey and Bradlow's 2006 promises-and-lies study, where prior deception left a lasting deficit,[^s1] and by Henderson, Welsh and O'Leary-Kelly's 2020 experiments, which **contrast** effective tactics with **denial**.[^s5] It is **explained by** **attribution theory**: whether people blame the breach on your ability, your intentions or the situation shapes which repair works.[^s4] Restoring trust also rebuilds **credibility** and the **trusted-advisor** relationship. Gillespie and Dietz's split between ability failures and integrity failures is the same distinction tested in Kim and colleagues' 2004 apology-versus-denial study (`cm:kim-et-al-2004`).[^s2]
