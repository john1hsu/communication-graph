# Saying no

Saying no is the skill of turning down a request, invitation or extra assignment clearly enough that the other person can plan around it, and respectfully enough that the relationship survives. The best-known practitioner method is William Ury's "positive no": you root the refusal in something you are protecting, say the no plainly and point toward an alternative that still meets some of the other person's interests.[^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Ury, a co-founder of Harvard's Program on Negotiation and co-author of *Getting to Yes*, set out the method in *The Power of a Positive No* (Bantam, 2007).[^s4][^s5] He argued that people usually fall into one of three traps when they want to refuse: accommodating (saying yes when they mean no), attacking (saying no in a hurtful way) or avoiding (saying nothing and hoping the request goes away), and often cycle between them.[^s5] His alternative is a Yes-No-Yes structure: a yes to your own interests, a clear no, and a yes that invites a different agreement.[^s5] PON frames the positive no as a negotiation skill, delivered in a neutral, matter-of-fact tone and paired with an offer such as "I can't do A, but perhaps I could do B".[^s4]

## Why it matters

Refusing feels expensive, so people agree to more than they should. In six studies, Flynn and Lake (now Bohns) found that people asking for help underestimated how many others would agree, by as much as half, because they overlooked how socially awkward it is for the person asked to say no.[^s1] The same pressure shows up on the decliner's side: in a 2023 set of five experiments with more than 2,000 participants, Givi and Kirk found that 77% of pilot respondents had accepted an invitation they did not want, and that decliners consistently overestimated how angry or disappointed the inviter would be.[^s2] In short, the cost of a polite no is usually lower than it feels.

At work the stakes are uneven. In lab experiments and a field study of 3,271 faculty, Babcock, Recalde, Vesterlund and Weingart (2017) found that women were asked to take on low-promotability tasks more often than men, agreed more often, and reported more worry about the consequences of declining; female faculty were 2.7 times more likely than male faculty to volunteer for a committee.[^s3] The authors argue this can slow women's advancement and recommend assigning such work through a fair system rather than volunteering.[^s3] Their later book, *The No Club*, reports that in one firm the median woman spent about 200 more hours a year than the median man on such tasks.[^s6]

## How to apply it

Prepare before you answer. Ury's preparation stage is to uncover your yes (the interest or commitment behind the refusal), empower your no with a Plan B (what you will do if the other side does not accept it), and plan to listen and acknowledge their point of view.[^s5] The *No Club* authors add practical moves for routine requests: ask for time instead of answering on the spot, gather the full details, do not let flattery decide for you, and enlist a colleague to help you word a refusal.[^s6]

Then deliver it in three beats. Acknowledge the request and briefly state your yes; assert the no in plain words, without overstating it (which sounds like an attack) or understating it (which sounds like a maybe); and propose a concrete alternative or say what you can do.[^s5] If the person pushes back, Ury advises neither giving in nor counter-attacking: acknowledge their reaction, repeat the no, and fall back on your Plan B only if needed.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **The soft yes.** Hedged, apologetic replies ("I'll try") are heard as agreement, which is the accommodating trap Ury describes.[^s5]
- **Over-explaining.** A long list of excuses invites the requester to solve each one; Ury's advice is a simple, natural no that follows from your stated yes.[^s5]
- **A fake alternative.** The second yes should be something you will deliver; PON stresses looking for a solution that still meets some of the other side's interests.[^s4]
- **Treating it as only a personal skill.** When the same people are always asked, the fix is also organisational: managers should distribute routine work fairly.[^s3][^s6]

## Evidence

The research supports the premise of the technique rather than the technique itself. Studies show that targets find refusing socially costly and that decliners overestimate the backlash, at least for social invitations; several Givi and Kirk experiments used imagined scenarios, so workplace generalisation is an inference.[^s1][^s2] Babcock and colleagues show who is asked and who agrees, not which way of declining works best.[^s3] We found no controlled test of the Yes-No-Yes structure, so its specific benefits rest on practitioner experience, and it is rated `practitioner` here.[^s4][^s5]
