# Emotion regulation

Emotion regulation is the set of processes people use to shape which emotions they have, when they have them, and how they experience and express them; the processes can be deliberate or automatic.[^s1] At work it is the skill that lets you feel irritated by a missed deadline, anxious before a pushback, or stung by criticism, and still say something useful. It is not the same as hiding feelings: suppressing the outward signs is only one strategy, and usually one of the weaker ones.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Stanford psychologist James Gross drew the stress-and-coping and ego-defence traditions together with several branches of psychology into one field in a 1998 review in *Review of General Psychology*.[^s1] His **process model** says an emotion unfolds as a sequence - a situation, attention to it, an appraisal of what it means, and a response - and that you can intervene at five points: choosing the situation, modifying it, deploying attention (for example distraction), changing how you think about it (cognitive change, including reappraisal), and modulating the response once it has fired.[^s1] The first four act before the emotion is fully generated ("antecedent-focused"); the last acts afterwards ("response-focused").[^s1] Gross illustrated situation selection with taking a different route to avoid a neighbour whose jokes offend you.[^s1]

## Why it matters

How you regulate shapes the other person's experience, not just your own. In Gross and John's five studies, people who habitually reappraised showed more positive and less negative emotion and had better social functioning, while habitual suppressors felt more negative emotion and fared worse socially.[^s4] In Butler and colleagues' experiments, pairs of strangers discussed an upsetting topic: when one was told to suppress, the conversation was disrupted, the partner's blood pressure rose, and rapport and liking dropped.[^s3] So the "poker face" many people use in tense meetings can make the other side more stressed and less connected, which is the opposite of what a difficult conversation needs.[^s3]

## What good looks like

A skilled regulator picks the strategy that fits the moment rather than defaulting to grit.[^s1][^s2]

- **Before the conversation (situation selection and modification):** schedule the hard talk for a private room and a time when neither side is rushed, or put the contentious item first on the agenda instead of letting it ambush you at 5 pm.[^s1]
- **In the moment (cognitive change):** reread the trigger. A product manager who hears "this spec is unusable" can treat it as an attack, or as a sign that the engineer cares about shipping something that works and needs specifics. The second reading lowers the heat and points to a question ("Which parts block you?").[^s1][^s2]
- **When the feeling is already up (response modulation):** slow your voice and pause before replying, but do not go blank; stay responsive to what the other person is saying.[^s3][^s5]

## How to practise it

1. **Name the strategy you default to.** Notice whether you usually avoid, ruminate, hide or reinterpret; Gross and John found people differ reliably in their habits.[^s4]
2. **Rehearse reappraisal on small irritations.** Write one alternative, still-accurate meaning for a frustrating email before replying. In a 2021 test with 21,644 people in 87 countries, a short reappraisal exercise reduced negative emotions and raised positive ones compared with controls.[^s6]
3. **Move earlier in the sequence.** Meta-analytic evidence shows cognitive change works best, while suppressing feelings inside does little; prepare reframes before the meeting rather than relying on clenching through it.[^s2]
4. **Use distraction, not dwelling, for short waits.** Distraction had a small benefit in Webb's meta-analysis, while concentrating on the feeling did not help.[^s2]
5. **Debrief afterwards** with the self-check questions and note which strategy worked.[^s4]

## Evidence

The strongest evidence is for cognitive change. Webb, Miles and Sheeran's 2012 meta-analysis of 306 experimental comparisons found reappraisal had a small-to-medium effect on emotional outcomes (d about 0.36), attentional deployment overall had none, and suppression reduced visible expression (d about 0.32) but not felt emotion.[^s2] The 2021 Psychological Science Accelerator study, a pre-registered multi-country trial, replicated the benefit of brief reappraisal at very large scale.[^s6] Limits: most experiments use film clips or lab stressors rather than real workplace conflicts, effects are modest, and the social costs of suppression depend on culture - they were smaller among participants holding more Asian cultural values.[^s2][^s5] Treat the evidence as strong that strategies differ, and as an informed starting point for how they play out in a given team.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Explained by** Gross's process model, which sorts strategies by where they act in the emotion sequence.[^s1]
- **Demonstrated by** Webb, Miles and Sheeran's 2012 meta-analysis of strategy effectiveness and by Butler and colleagues' 2003 experiments on the interpersonal costs of suppression.[^s2][^s3]
- **Supported by** the Psychological Science Accelerator's 2021 multi-country reappraisal trial.[^s6]
- **Applied in** difficult conversations, the setting Butler and colleagues simulated with an upsetting discussion topic.[^s3]
- **Studied in** psychology, cutting across biological, cognitive, developmental, social, personality, clinical and health psychology.[^s1]
- **Related:** cognitive reappraisal and anxiety reappraisal (cognitive-change strategies), self-distancing, labeling emotions, and taking a cooling-off period (situation and timing choices); defensiveness and emotional contagion are what poor regulation can feed in a conversation.[^s1][^s3]
