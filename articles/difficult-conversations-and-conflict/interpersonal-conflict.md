# Interpersonal conflict

Interpersonal conflict is a dynamic process between people who depend on each other, in which they perceive a disagreement, feel that the other side is getting in the way of their goals, and respond with negative emotion.[^s3] All three ingredients matter: a disagreement that nobody cares about, or friction between people who do not need each other, rarely behaves like conflict. At work it is useful to ask *what* the conflict is about, because the answer changes how it affects the team and what to do next.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Organisational researchers long used "conflict" loosely, which made studies hard to compare. Henri Barki and Jon Hartwick reviewed that literature in 2004 and proposed that interpersonal conflict be defined by three properties — disagreement, interference and negative emotion — crossed with what the conflict is about, giving a typology that later measures could share.[^s3] In parallel, team researchers settled on a content-based split: **task conflict** over the work itself (what to do, which data to trust, what goal matters), **process conflict** over logistics (who does what, who owns which piece, how resources are allocated), and **relationship conflict** over personal friction such as clashing styles, values or norms.[^s4][^s1] Practitioner guidance from Harvard's Program on Negotiation adds **value conflict**, rooted in beliefs and identity, as a fourth type that resists the usual problem-solving moves.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Conflict is unavoidable when interdependent people pursue different goals with shared resources, so the practical question is not whether it happens but which kind is happening and whether it is spreading. A disagreement about launch dates can stay a task conflict, or it can turn into "she always overrides me", at which point it is a relationship conflict and much harder to resolve.[^s4] Naming the type helps a manager or peer choose a response: task disputes call for surfacing interests and solving the problem together, relationship friction for early, direct, listening-first conversations, and value disputes for understanding rather than winning.[^s5]

**Workplace example.** A product manager and a data analyst disagree about whether a churn dip is real. That is task conflict, and arguing about the data may improve the decision. If the PM then reassigns the analysis without telling the analyst, it becomes process conflict (who owns it). If the analyst starts describing the PM as someone who "never listens", it has become relationship conflict. The useful move is to deal with it while it is still about the numbers — agree on the test for whether the dip is real, and confirm who owns the analysis — before it turns personal.[^s5][^s4]

## Evidence

The idea that task conflict is good for teams while relationship conflict is bad was tested in a 2003 meta-analysis by Carsten De Dreu and Laurie Weingart. Both kinds were strongly and negatively correlated with team performance and member satisfaction — contrary to the predicted benefit of task conflict. Conflict hurt more on complex tasks such as decision making and projects, and task conflict did less harm when it was only weakly tied to relationship conflict.[^s2]

A larger 2012 meta-analysis by Frank de Wit, Lindred Greer and Karen Jehn (116 studies, 8,880 groups) refined that picture. Relationship and process conflict showed stable negative links with group outcomes, but task conflict did not have the strong negative link reported earlier. Task conflict looked more beneficial when it was weakly correlated with relationship conflict, in top management teams, and when performance was measured as decision quality or financial results.[^s1] A Columbia ICCCR summary of that study notes that conflict affected cohesion and satisfaction more than performance, and that task conflict harmed cohesion mainly when relationship conflict was also high.[^s4]

The evidence is therefore **mixed**: the harm of relationship and process conflict is well replicated, but the popular claim that "healthy debate" reliably improves performance holds only under conditions, chiefly that disagreement stays separated from personal friction. These findings are correlational and come mostly from self-reported conflict, so they show association rather than cause.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Interpersonal conflict is a pattern within interpersonal communication (`cm:interpersonal-communication`) and is the subject studied by the field of conflict resolution (`cm:conflict-resolution`), whose practitioners distinguish task, relationship and value disputes.[^s5] It is **demonstrated by** the de Wit, Greer and Jehn meta-analysis (`cm:de-wit-greer-jehn-2012`)[^s1] and the De Dreu and Weingart meta-analysis (`cm:de-dreu-weingart-2003`).[^s2] Relationship conflict erodes team cohesion, and task conflict does so mainly when relationship conflict is also high.[^s4] Broader situations such as the difficult conversation (`cm:difficult-conversation`) and models such as the Thomas-Kilmann conflict modes (`cm:thomas-kilmann-conflict-modes`) sit beneath this node. Practical responses include identifying interests behind positions (`cm:interests-vs-positions`), describing the other side's view accurately (`cm:perspective-taking`), and calming heated exchanges (`cm:de-escalation`), while psychological safety (`cm:psychological-safety`) is the team climate in which task disagreement is most likely to stay impersonal.[^s5][^s4]
