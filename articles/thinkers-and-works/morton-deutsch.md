# Morton Deutsch

Morton Deutsch was an American social psychologist who spent more than fifty years at Teachers College, Columbia University, where he held the Edward Lee Thorndike professorship of psychology and education and pioneered research on conflict resolution, cooperative learning and social justice.[^s1] Colleagues in international negotiation research describe him as one of the founding fathers of the conflict resolution field.[^s2] For anyone who has to handle disagreement at work, his central lesson is that the way people believe their goals are linked shapes how openly they talk, how much they trust each other and whether a dispute escalates.[^s3][^s4]

## Origin

After serving as an Air Force psychologist and navigator in the Second World War, Deutsch earned his PhD at MIT under Kurt Lewin.[^s2] His dissertation built on Lewin's idea that how group members' goals depend on each other drives group dynamics: he distinguished cooperative situations, where goals are positively linked, from competitive ones, where one side's gain is the other's loss, and he tested the difference by grading psychology students either on a shared group average or competitively against each other.[^s3] The competitively graded students tended to treat disagreements as contests with other members, while the cooperatively graded students tended to treat them as problems to solve together.[^s3]

He developed this into a full account of conflict in *The Resolution of Conflict* (1973), followed by *Distributive Justice* (1985) and, with Peter Coleman and Eric Marcus, *The Handbook of Conflict Resolution*, whose third edition appeared in 2014.[^s2][^s3] In 1986 he founded the International Center for Cooperation and Conflict Resolution at Teachers College, which he directed until 1997, and he supervised hundreds of doctoral students who went on to lead work in negotiation, mediation and peace studies.[^s1][^s2] His frameworks were also drawn on in Cold War US-Soviet negotiations and in Poland's peaceful transition from Communist rule in 1989.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Deutsch's theory of cooperation and competition predicts what happens to communication under each goal structure. Competition tends to bring blocked or poor communication, suspicion, coercion, threats and attempts to mislead; cooperation tends to bring open communication, helpfulness, friendly attitudes and attention to shared interests.[^s3][^s4] Coleman, reviewing the field in 2018, notes that a large body of later studies supported these predictions and that they shaped practice in education, business and international policy.[^s3]

His second big idea, which he called the crude law of social relations, is that the processes a relationship produces tend to recreate that relationship: cooperation breeds more cooperation and competition breeds more competition.[^s3][^s4] He labelled it crude because the effect does not appear every time, but it explains why a sharp email often draws a sharper reply, and why a small cooperative gesture can start a better cycle.[^s3] From this he argued that constructive conflict resolution looks like joint problem solving and destructive conflict looks like a win-lose contest that escalates and hardens positions.[^s3][^s4]

His bargaining experiments with Robert Krauss, published in the *Journal of Conflict Resolution*, tested what threats do to negotiators.[^s6] In their trucking game, two players who had to share a one-lane road earned the most when neither could block the other; giving one player a gate to threaten with lowered both players' earnings, and giving both players gates produced the worst results, because standoffs formed quickly.[^s5] The practical takeaway is that the ability to threaten, and especially counter-threatening, tends to cost both sides.[^s5]

In a workplace example, two team leads competing for one budget line can either argue over who deserves it or restate the problem as "how do we fund the release and the migration this quarter?". Deutsch's work predicts the second framing will draw out more honest information about real constraints, because it makes the goals positively linked.[^s3][^s4] His summary of supportive norms (honesty, respect, responsiveness, taking responsibility and seeking common ground) works as a practical checklist for keeping such a conversation cooperative.[^s4]

## Limits

Much of the early evidence came from laboratory games and student groups, and Deutsch himself acknowledged that the crude law describes a tendency rather than a rule.[^s3][^s5] The trucking-game findings describe one artificial bargaining situation, so they are best read as a warning about threats rather than an exact forecast of any real negotiation.[^s5]

## Connections

Deutsch introduced the theory of cooperation and competition, which became the psychological core of the conflict resolution field he helped found.[^s2][^s3] He was a student of Kurt Lewin, whose ideas about goal interdependence he extended.[^s3] He wrote *The Resolution of Conflict*, the 1973 book that set out his framework for constructive and destructive conflict.[^s2][^s3] In this graph his work underpins de-escalation, role reversal and the contrast between integrative and distributive negotiation, and it sits alongside Charles Osgood's work on reducing tension through reciprocated steps.[^s3][^s4]
