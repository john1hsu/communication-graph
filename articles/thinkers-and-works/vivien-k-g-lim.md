# Vivien K. G. Lim

Vivien K. G. Lim is a professor in the Department of Management and Organisation at NUS Business School in Singapore, with courtesy appointments in engineering leadership and psychology.[^s3] Her research covers leadership, workplace health, job loss, employee misbehaviour and discipline, and how technology changes work.[^s3] For anyone who communicates at work, her lasting contribution is a vocabulary for two everyday digital problems: rude or neglectful work email, which she and Thompson Teo called **cyber incivility**, and personal web use on company time, known as **cyberloafing**.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Lim trained in organisational behaviour at the University of Pittsburgh on an NUS overseas scholarship, then built her career at NUS.[^s3][^s6] In 2014 she became the Business School's first female full professor.[^s6] She edited *Applied Psychology: An International Review* from 2010 to 2015.[^s3]

Her technology work began with cyberloafing. In a 2001 conference paper with Teo and Geok Leng Loo, she defined it as employees voluntarily using company internet access during office hours to browse non-work sites or handle personal email.[^s2] They surveyed 188 Singaporean workers and ran focus groups. Many respondents felt the habit was justified when they had put in extra effort or felt unfairly treated, and most did not know of any internet-use policy.[^s2] The term itself is older; a later review traces its first use to 1995. That review credits Lim's 2002 journal article with establishing cyberloafing as an academic construct, explained through perceived organisational injustice rather than laziness.[^s5]

In 2009 Lim and Teo turned to the tone of email itself. They defined cyber incivility as computer-mediated behaviour that breaks workplace norms of mutual respect, whether or not the sender intends harm.[^s1] They separated active forms, such as hostile or demeaning messages, from passive ones, such as ignoring email, replying late or not at all, and using email for matters that need a conversation.[^s1] They described the study as an initial step in a field with little prior research.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Lim's 2009 study gave managers evidence that how they write email affects how people feel about their jobs. Among 192 banking and finance employees in Singapore, more incivility in supervisors' emails went with lower job satisfaction and commitment, and with higher intention to quit and more workplace deviance.[^s1] Passive incivility, the unanswered or ignored message, still predicted lower satisfaction and higher intention to quit.[^s1] So slow or missing replies are not neutral.[^s1] She recommended practical remedies: train staff in email etiquette, set clear policies against uncivil email, create forums to discuss email problems, and use upward feedback to spot supervisors who need coaching.[^s1]

A workplace illustration: an operations manager reviewing her own sent folder finds terse one-line replies, criticism copied to the whole team, and three requests left unanswered for a week. Using Lim and Teo's active/passive split, she moves criticism to private messages or calls, and she acknowledges every request within a day, even if only to give a date for a full answer.[^s1]

Her cyberloafing work carries a parallel lesson for communication. Employees who see internet misuse as fair payback for unfair treatment are reacting to how the organisation treats and talks to them. A clear, explained internet-use policy is part of the remedy; surveillance alone is not.[^s2][^s5]

## Evidence

Lim and Teo's 2009 findings are cross-sectional, single-source self-reports from one Asian industry, and the authors themselves warned against causal readings and cross-cultural generalisation.[^s1] Later work has used their 14-item Cyber Incivility Scale. Park, Fritz and Jex's 2018 diary study of 96 US employees found that on days with more uncivil email, workers ended the day more distressed. Job control and evening detachment from work softened that effect.[^s4] That supports the core claim with a stronger within-person design, though effect sizes and moderators vary across studies.[^s4] The cyberloafing research is likewise mostly survey-based and correlational.[^s5]

## Connections

With Teo, Lim **introduced cyber incivility**, naming and measuring rude and neglectful work email as a distinct form of **workplace incivility**.[^s1] She also **introduced cyberloafing** as a research construct, popularising rather than coining the term.[^s2][^s5] Her scale and framing **influenced** later diary research such as Park, Fritz and Jex (2018).[^s4] Her work relates to **email**, **chat etiquette** and **setting reply-time expectations**, where passive incivility shows up, and sits within **industrial-organisational psychology**.[^s1][^s3]
