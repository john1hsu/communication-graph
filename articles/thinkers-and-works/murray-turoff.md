# Murray Turoff

Murray Turoff was a distinguished professor of information systems at the New Jersey Institute of Technology (NJIT), described by the university as a founding father of computer-mediated communication, who died on 28 September 2022.[^s1] Historians of computing call him the "father" of the first computer conferencing system, EMISARI, the line of work that made written, many-to-many discussion by computer possible.[^s2] Much of what teams now call asynchronous communication - threaded discussions that people join when it suits them, with the record kept by the system - was first designed, named and studied by Turoff and his collaborators.[^s3][^s6]

## Origin

In 1971 Turoff built EMISARI (Emergency Management Information System and Reference Index) for the U.S. government to monitor economic disruptions and shortages during the wage and price controls; it stayed in use for about 15 years and included a live chat feature called the "Party Line".[^s1] Moving to NJIT, he developed EIES, the Electronic Information Exchange System, in 1976, one of the first multi-machine conferencing systems, offering group conferences, public posts, private messages and a directory.[^s1] With sociologist Starr Roxanne Hiltz, his research partner on EIES and later his wife, he wrote *The Network Nation: Human Communication via Computer* (1978), which won the Association of American Publishers award for best scientific, technical or medical book and became the standard reference on computer-mediated communication.[^s6][^s1] Hiltz credits Turoff's conferencing work with making her own research possible.[^s2] Earlier, with Harold Linstone, he edited *The Delphi Method* (1975), a 640-page collection that defines Delphi as a way of structuring a group communication process through feedback, chances to revise views and partial anonymity.[^s5]

## Key ideas

**Time-independent group talk.** A 1978 NSF report that Turoff led framed computerized conferencing as a written medium in which nobody has to share a place or a moment, the system remembers the discussion, and each person controls when and how they take part.[^s3] It argued that this control changes the psychology of the group, so findings from face-to-face meetings cannot simply be carried over.[^s3]

**Structure, not just a pipe.** The same report contrasted a single general messaging system with conferencing structures tailored to each group's task, a choice Turoff argued for throughout EIES.[^s3] His Delphi work carried the same idea: anonymity, feedback and revision rounds shape what a group can produce.[^s5]

**Predicting remote work.** *The Network Nation* forecast virtual communities, telecommuting, flaming, and tensions over privacy and anonymity decades before they became workplace issues.[^s2][^s6]

## Why it matters

Turoff's experiments are an early, still useful warning about moving decisions into async channels. In a 1986 *Human Communication Research* study, he, Hiltz and Kenneth Johnson found that five-person groups talking face-to-face produced two to three times as many communication units in the same time as computer-conferencing groups.[^s4] Decision quality was about the same in both modes, and the online groups' messages were more task-focused, but they were less likely to reach agreement.[^s4]

For a manager today, that suggests a practical split. Imagine a team choosing a vendor: a thread works well for gathering requirements and comparing options, but a team that only replies in the thread may stall at the final call. Following Turoff, set the structure first - a clear question, a deadline, a simple vote or ranking - and book a short live conversation if the thread has not converged.[^s3][^s4]

## Limits

The 1978 report described itself as hypotheses and open questions, not findings, and the 1986 results come from small laboratory groups using text-only systems, so they need checking against modern tools.[^s3][^s4] Turoff did not invent written asynchronous exchange - letters and electronic mail came first - so his claim is to group conferencing and its study rather than to delayed messaging itself.[^s2][^s1]

## Connections

Turoff **introduced** the idea of time-independent, system-remembered group discussion that underlies **asynchronous communication** (`cm:asynchronous-communication`), as set out in his 1978 conferencing report.[^s3] He **introduced computer conferencing** (`cm:computer-conferencing`) through EMISARI.[^s2] He **authored** *The Network Nation* (`cm:the-network-nation`) with Hiltz[^s6] and co-edited **The Delphi Method** (`cm:the-delphi-method`), which links him to the **Delphi technique** and to its relative, the **nominal group technique**.[^s5] He **influenced Starr Roxanne Hiltz** (`cm:starr-roxanne-hiltz`), whose sociology of online groups built on his systems.[^s2] He relates to **Ray Tomlinson** and **email**, the person-to-person side of the same history, and to **digital communication**, **group decision making** and **information overload**.[^s1][^s4]
