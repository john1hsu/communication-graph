# Adam Galinsky

Adam Galinsky is an American social psychologist and professor at Columbia Business School who studies negotiation, power, leadership, decision making and ethics.[^s5] For communication at work he matters for three ideas that are now standard advice: the first number in a negotiation acts as an anchor, understanding what the other side thinks is worth more at the table than feeling what they feel, and how much room you have to speak up depends on your power in the situation.[^s1][^s2][^s4] He has published more than 300 scientific articles and co-wrote the book *Friend & Foe* (2015) and wrote *Inspire* (2025).[^s5]

## Origin

Galinsky was in the management department at the University of Utah when he and Thomas Mussweiler published "First offers as anchors" in 2001.[^s1] In three experiments, the side that made the first offer - buyer or seller - got the better deal, and the opening number strongly predicted the final price, both face to face and over email.[^s1] The same paper showed the antidote: when the receiving negotiator concentrated on information that contradicted the anchor - the opponent's alternatives, the opponent's reservation price, or their own target - the first mover's advantage disappeared.[^s1] The graph credits him with introducing the first-offer technique in this sense: anchoring itself predates him, but this work turned it into tested negotiation advice.[^s1][^s3]

He later moved to Northwestern's Kellogg School, where he and colleagues extended the work. A 2011 Kellogg summary of his research with Gillian Ku and Mussweiler advised making a strong but not extreme opening in negotiations, countering an opponent's anchor quickly, and waiting to open when you lack market information - while noting that auctions reward low starting prices instead.[^s3] By 2016 he held the Vikram S. Pandit chair at Columbia and chaired its management division.[^s4]

## Why it matters

**Perspective-taking over empathy.** In a 2008 *Psychological Science* paper with William Maddux, Debra Gilin and Judith White, MBA students who were asked to imagine what their counterpart was thinking reached far more deals in a negotiation that needed a creative solution (76% versus 39% for controls), while students asked to imagine what their counterpart was feeling did no better than controls.[^s2] In a multi-issue job negotiation, perspective-takers created the most joint value and claimed more for themselves, whereas empathic recruiters mostly helped the other side.[^s2] For anyone negotiating a budget, a contract or a role, the lesson is to map the other side's interests and constraints deliberately, not just to be warm.[^s2]

**The range of acceptable behavior.** In his 2016 TEDxNewYork talk "How to speak up for yourself", Galinsky argued that each person has a range of behaviour others will accept, that stepping outside it brings backlash, and that power is what sets its width.[^s4] He offered five ways to widen it: advocate for others, take the other person's perspective, signal flexibility by offering choices, gain allies, and show expertise and passion.[^s4]

## Applying his ideas at work

Before a negotiation, decide whether you know the market well enough to open; if you do, open first with a reasoned, ambitious number.[^s3] If the other side opens first, write down their likely walk-away point and your own target before you reply, which is the move that cancelled the anchor in his experiments.[^s1] Before a hard conversation upward, ask what the other person needs to say yes, and frame your request as two or three options rather than a single demand.[^s2][^s4]

Example. Before: a project manager tells her director, "We need two more engineers, or the launch slips." After: she says, "To protect the March launch, I see three options - two contractors for eight weeks, cutting the reporting module, or moving the date two weeks. Which risk sits best with you?" Why: she has considered the director's constraints and signalled flexibility, the two moves Galinsky links to being heard without backlash.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The 2001 first-offer experiments and the 2008 perspective-taking studies are peer-reviewed, but both used student or MBA participants in simulated negotiations, and the 2008 results rest on three studies with samples of about 70 to 150 people each.[^s1][^s2] The five speaking-up steps are presented in a talk and press release as practical advice informed by research, not as a single tested intervention, so treat them as practitioner guidance.[^s4] His status here is `historical`: the article records his contributions, while the evidence for each idea is weighed in its own node.
