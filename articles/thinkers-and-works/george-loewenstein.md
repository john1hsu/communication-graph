# George Loewenstein

George Loewenstein is the Herbert A. Simon University Professor of Economics and Psychology in Carnegie Mellon University's Department of Social and Decision Sciences. Trained as an economist at Yale, he is credited with helping to found behavioural economics and neuroeconomics, and his research ranges across attention, curiosity, emotion in decision making, bargaining and conflict of interest.[^s1] For communicators, two of his contributions matter most: he co-authored the 1989 experiments that named the curse of knowledge, and he proposed the information-gap account of curiosity.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

In 1989 Loewenstein, Colin Camerer and Martin Weber published "The Curse of Knowledge in Economic Settings" in the *Journal of Political Economy*. A footnote credits the decision researcher Robin Hogarth with suggesting the name, so the paper introduced the concept and its first controlled test rather than coining the phrase alone.[^s2] In the experiments, Wharton students who had seen companies' actual earnings were paid to predict what less-informed students had forecast. Their judgments were pulled toward the true figures they knew, feedback did little to help, and trading in a market roughly halved the bias without removing it.[^s2] The authors noted that this makes one's own expertise seem more widely shared than it is, which makes expertise hard to pass on.[^s2]

Five years later, in a 1994 *Psychological Bulletin* review, Loewenstein argued that curiosity arises when people notice a gap between what they know and what they want to know, a felt deprivation that motivates them to seek the missing information.[^s4] With Leaf Van Boven and others he also developed the idea of empathy gaps: people underestimate how much a different emotional state would change their own behaviour, and because they predict others by first imagining themselves, they misjudge others too.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Both lines of work explain everyday communication failures at work. The curse-of-knowledge results show that the person who knows most is badly placed to judge what a listener already understands, and that simply getting feedback does not fix it.[^s2] The empathy-gap research adds that predictions about colleagues, customers or counterparts start from a self-simulation: in one study, buyer's agents who misjudged how much owners valued a mug made low offers and had only about a quarter of them accepted.[^s3] The practical lesson is to check understanding with the actual audience rather than trusting your own sense of what is obvious or how someone will feel.[^s2][^s3]

The information-gap theory gives the constructive side. Reviewers summarise it as treating uncertainty as a kind of hunger and information as food; a question that exposes a manageable gap can make people want the answer, and partial answers can sharpen rather than satisfy that want.[^s4] For a presenter or writer, that means opening with a real question or a puzzling fact the audience cares about before giving the answer, instead of front-loading every detail.[^s4] Curiosity is worth inducing: studies using trivia questions find that people remember answers they were curious about better, even after a delay.[^s5]

## Applying his ideas at work

Imagine an analyst about to brief a sales team on a new pricing model. Applying the curse of knowledge, she asks a colleague outside the project to explain the model back before the meeting, because her own feeling that it is clear is the judgment the bias distorts.[^s2] Applying the information-gap idea, she opens with the question the team actually has, such as why two similar customers were quoted different prices, and then walks through the answer step by step.[^s4] Note the difference from bottom-line-up-front writing: a curiosity opener suits a talk or training session where attention must be earned, while a busy decision-maker reading an email usually needs the answer first.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The 1989 study used small groups of business students forecasting company earnings, and the bias it measured varied across the eight companies tested, so treat it as a reliable direction rather than a fixed size; the graph's curse-of-knowledge node covers later replications.[^s2] Curiosity research supports the link to learning but also qualifies the gap model: the relationship between uncertainty and curiosity looks like an inverted U, so a gap that is too small or too large kills interest; curiosity can also arise from personal interest without any gap, and distraction can dissipate it.[^s4] Much of the memory evidence comes from a single trivia-question paradigm, and the neural accounts remain partly untested.[^s5] The empathy-gap chapter likewise reports that experience and even prompt feedback did not reliably remove the bias, so structured checks matter more than good intentions.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Introduced the curse of knowledge** - with Camerer and Weber, in the 1989 paper that named and first tested it; Robin Hogarth suggested the label.[^s2] That paper is recorded in this graph as the study that demonstrates the effect.
- **Introduced information-gap theory** of curiosity and **authored** *The Psychology of Curiosity* (1994), its founding review.[^s4]
- **Related to the empathy gap**, which he developed with Van Boven and colleagues, and to **egocentric bias**, since both rest on projecting one's own state onto others.[^s3]
- **Related to the illusion of transparency and audience analysis**, neighbouring ideas about overestimating how well others understand us and the habit that counteracts it.[^s2]
