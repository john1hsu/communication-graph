# Isaac Marks

Isaac M. Marks is a psychiatrist who spent his research career at the Bethlem-Maudsley Hospital and London's Institute of Psychiatry, where he became professor of experimental psychopathology.[^s2] His central contribution was to show, in trial after trial, that the most effective way to overcome a phobia is to face the feared situation in real life, and then to show that people can do this largely by themselves.[^s1][^s3] For anyone who dreads presenting, speaking up in meetings or making difficult calls, the graded, self-directed practice now taught in workplace and self-help programmes owes much to his work.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Marks was born in Cape Town in 1935 and qualified in medicine there in 1956. He trained in psychiatry at the Maudsley from 1960 to 1963 and did clinical research there and at the Institute of Psychiatry from 1964 until he became emeritus in 2000.[^s2] He was a founding member of the Royal College of Psychiatrists in 1971.[^s2]

His 1969 book *Fears and Phobias* drew on 1960s Maudsley research and concluded that, of the many routes to reducing fear, the most effective all involved some form of exposure.[^s1] At the time Joseph Wolpe's systematic desensitization, which paired imagined scenes with relaxation, was the leading behavioural method. Through the early 1970s Marks and colleagues compared it with flooding, meaning prolonged confrontation with the feared situation.[^s4] In a 1974 letter he argued that real-life (in vivo) practice was probably the potent ingredient. He said a study claiming that half the benefit was non-specific had included in vivo exposure in its control group. Andrew Mathews and Michael Gelder replied that the evidence for in vivo exposure being far more therapeutic was not yet unequivocal.[^s4] The idea won out: a 1984 trial from his group opened by noting wide agreement that phobic patients must, at some stage, confront what they avoid.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Marks's second contribution was scale. Therapist-led exposure works, but demand far outstrips the supply of therapists.[^s3] In 1984 Ghosh, Marks and Carr randomised 71 phobic patients, most of them agoraphobic, to self-exposure guided by a book, by a computer program or by a psychiatrist. All three groups improved similarly and kept their gains at six months. The book group needed about 40 minutes of clinician time, against roughly three to four hours for the others.[^s3] His 1978 self-help manual *Living with Fear* teaches graded self-exposure: face the situation, stay with it until the anxiety subsides, and keep a diary of progress.[^s5] A 2000 review called it the only self-help book the reviewer knew of that had been tested in randomised trials. It also inspired the lay self-help group Triumph Over Phobia.[^s5] He was also among the first to test whether nurses could deliver these treatments in the community, and later became a forerunner of computer-aided psychotherapy.[^s1]

A workplace illustration of the principle: an analyst who avoids speaking in team meetings writes a ladder of steps, such as asking one question, then summarising a point, then giving a two-minute update. She practises each step until it feels routine before moving up, and records each attempt and her anxiety in a diary. That is Marks's self-exposure routine applied to a communication fear rather than a phobia of lifts or crowds.[^s5][^s3]

His evidence-first stance also shaped how clinicians weigh options. He reported that exposure gains for panic and agoraphobia lasted after treatment ended, while gains from the drug alprazolam faded during taper and follow-up.[^s1] His work also found self-exposure the most effective option for compulsive rituals, with gains lasting four years.[^s6] He was critical of relying on medication for anxiety, and his findings fed into clinical guidelines in many countries.[^s1]

## Evidence

Marks's reputation rests on controlled trials rather than theory. One colleague's tribute calls him one of the most-cited behaviour therapists, with nearly 500 papers.[^s1] The self-exposure trial was explicitly preliminary. It had small groups, only six months of follow-up and weekly assessments, so it could not show how people would fare with no clinician contact at all.[^s3] The 1974 exchange shows that his view of in vivo exposure as the key ingredient was contested at the time, not handed down as settled fact.[^s4] Most of his trials studied clinical phobias, panic and obsessive-compulsive disorder. Applying them to everyday workplace nerves is a reasonable extension, but those trials did not test it directly.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

Marks **introduced** (popularised) **exposure therapy** in its modern in vivo and self-directed form, building on and testing against **systematic desensitization** from **Joseph Wolpe**.[^s1][^s4] He **authored** *Living with Fear* (1978) and *Fears, Phobias and Rituals* (1987), the latter praised for tracing how exposure therapy developed.[^s5] Because his findings entered clinical guidelines and his self-exposure method was built for people to run on their own, he relates to the graph's work on **social anxiety**, **speaking anxiety** and **communication apprehension**. The graph also links him to **David M. Clark**, whose article covers the later cognitive treatment of social anxiety.[^s1][^s3]
