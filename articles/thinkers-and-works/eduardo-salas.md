# Eduardo Salas

Eduardo Salas is an industrial-organizational and human-factors psychologist who has spent four decades studying how teams of experts coordinate, communicate and learn, and how to train them to do it better.[^s3][^s5] For communication practice his best-known contribution is giving team communication a precise, trainable shape: in a widely cited 2005 review he and his co-authors named closed-loop communication as one of three mechanisms that hold teamwork together.[^s1]

## Origin

Salas earned a PhD in industrial and organizational psychology from Old Dominion University in 1984, then spent 15 years as a senior research psychologist with the U.S. Navy, heading the Training Technology Development Branch at NAVAIR-Orlando.[^s3] There he set up a team-performance laboratory and studied operational teams in the field, an approach he describes as embedding himself with the people he studies.[^s6] He later held the Trustee Chair and Pegasus Professorship at the University of Central Florida before moving to Rice University, where he has chaired the Department of Psychological Sciences and directs a team-effectiveness lab.[^s3][^s5] He is a past president of the Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology, and in 2025 the American Psychological Association's group-psychology division named him Group Psychologist of the Year.[^s3][^s5]

## Why it matters

Before his 2005 synthesis, teamwork research was scattered across more than a hundred competing models that practitioners found hard to use. Salas, Dana Sims and Shawn Burke reviewed over 138 team-effectiveness models and distilled five core components (team leadership, mutual performance monitoring, backup behaviour, adaptability and team orientation) plus three coordinating mechanisms: shared mental models, mutual trust and closed-loop communication.[^s1] Building on McIntyre and Salas's 1995 work, they defined closed-loop communication as a three-step exchange in which the sender starts a message, the receiver takes it in, interprets it and acknowledges it, and the sender follows up to confirm the intended meaning arrived.[^s1] That definition is what turned a military radio habit into a named teamwork behaviour that later training programmes and studies could teach and measure.[^s1]

His second contribution is evidence that communication and coordination can be trained. A 2008 meta-analysis he led in *Human Factors*, pooling 93 effect sizes from 2,650 teams, found moderate, positive relationships between team training and cognitive, affective, process and performance outcomes, with the size of the effect depending on training content, team stability and team size.[^s2] In a later interview he argued that roughly 80% of what the military learned about teamwork transfers to health care, because task interdependence, not the setting, decides which teamwork skills a team needs.[^s7]

## Ideas a practitioner can use

- **Close the loop on critical messages.** The sender's follow-up, not the receiver's nod, is what proves the message arrived as intended.[^s1]
- **Pre-brief and debrief.** Salas reports that teams who pre-brief roles and how they will communicate, then debrief what to stop, start and continue, outperform teams that skip these steps.[^s6]
- **Treat communication as protocols.** In *Teams That Work* (2020), written with Scott Tannenbaum, communication is one of seven drivers of team effectiveness, alongside capability, cooperation, coordination, cognition, coaching and conditions.[^s4] Salas describes it as agreed information-exchange protocols: clear terms and timely information.[^s6]
- **Diagnose before you train.** He calls communication a big bucket that leaders should break into specific team problems, and recommends practice with feedback over long lectures and demonstrations.[^s7]

## Limits

Salas and his co-authors presented their ten research propositions about the Big Five as untested, and noted that evidence on leadership and on how teamwork needs change across a team's life was thin.[^s1] He has also cautioned that team training explains only part of team performance, with organisation, leadership and culture deciding whether new habits stick.[^s7] Much of the supporting evidence comes from aviation, military and clinical teams, so office applications are reasonable extrapolations rather than direct findings.[^s1][^s7]

## Connections

Salas is credited here with introducing, or at least popularising, closed-loop communication as a teamwork concept, through his 2005 article *Is There a "Big Five" in Teamwork?*, which he co-authored.[^s1] That article pairs it with shared mental models and mutual trust, and later team studies such as Espevik and colleagues' police-patrol research build on that framing.[^s1] He co-authored *Teams That Work* with Scott Tannenbaum.[^s4] His emphasis on the licence to speak up connects his work to psychological safety, and his preference for practice with feedback links it to behaviour-modelling training.[^s6][^s7]
