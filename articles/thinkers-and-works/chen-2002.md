# Leveraging the Asymmetric Sensitivity of Eye Contact for Videoconferencing

*Leveraging the Asymmetric Sensitivity of Eye Contact for Videoconferencing* is a 2002 paper by Milton Chen, then a Stanford doctoral student, presented at the ACM CHI conference.[^s1][^s2] It asked a practical question that every remote worker now meets daily: because the camera cannot sit inside the other person's face on your screen, how far can it be from their eyes before you stop seeming to look at them?[^s1] Its answer, that people are far more forgiving of gaze that drops slightly below their eyes than of gaze that drifts sideways or upward, became the usual justification for putting the camera on top of the screen and the other person's video just beneath it.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Chen worked in Stanford's Computer Graphics Laboratory and Interactivity Laboratory on class-scale videoconferencing; his doctoral thesis covered eye contact, frame rate and video size, and he presented the eye-contact work alongside a remote-classroom "video auditorium" at a Stanford HCI seminar in May 2002.[^s1][^s2] The paper built on earlier gaze research, including a 1969 Bell Labs PicturePhone study that put the loss of eye contact at about 4.5 degrees to the side and 5.5 degrees above or below.[^s1]

## What it found

Chen recorded a looker gazing at targets spread in eight directions around a camera hidden in a large projected display, then had 16 Stanford observers judge whether he seemed to be making eye contact; follow-up experiments added three more lookers, a live video link and a face-to-face comparison.[^s1] Looking about 1 degree up, left or right of the camera was enough to break eye contact, but observers were roughly an order of magnitude less sensitive to downward gaze.[^s1] To explain this, Chen proposed a "snap to contact" account: people cannot judge gaze precisely, so they assume they are being looked at unless they are sure they are not, and looking down changes the visible eye less than looking sideways.[^s1]

From this he derived design parameters: put the camera above the display and keep the angle between the lens and the remote person's rendered eyes under about 5 degrees, which works out to about 3 inches on a desktop monitor viewed from 3 feet.[^s1] A prototype placed the video window at the top centre of a 20-inch monitor just under a webcam, and all eight people who tried it reported eye contact.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The paper is where the familiar workplace advice comes from in its engineering form: raise the camera, drag the speaker's window up under the lens, and accept that looking slightly below the lens is hardly noticed while a side monitor is.[^s1][^s4] Later videoconferencing research routinely cites it for the asymmetry, including a 2022 *IEEE Access* study that found business-style calls were rated less effective when the camera was moved sideways from the screen centre.[^s4] A 2010 study by van Eijk and colleagues also reported asymmetric sensitivity, with tolerances of about 1.2 degrees horizontally and 1.7 degrees vertically.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The core asymmetry is now disputed. In 2022, Horstmann and Linke measured the "gaze cone" for video-call images and found it roughly symmetric vertically, about 8 degrees across, with no extra tolerance for downward gaze; they argue their data contradict the case for top-mounted cameras that Chen's study is usually cited for.[^s3] They suggest Chen's long viewing distance of about 2.4 metres and low image resolution may have widened the downward tolerance, and note that some of his conditions were not fully reported.[^s3] Chen himself flagged small samples, a single looker in the main experiment and untested generalisation.[^s1] A 2024 preprint found perceived eye contact peaked about 2 degrees below the lens, closer to Chen than to a symmetric cone, but it used still photos, four actors and 17 raters and has a much narrower tolerance of about 3 degrees.[^s6]

For practice, the safe reading is narrower than the popular one: keep the other person's face as close to the lens as you can, avoid sideways offsets, and do not rely on a large below-the-lens margin.[^s3][^s4][^s6]

## Connections

The paper introduced the design idea behind camera-level gaze, the technique of raising the camera and placing the other person's window just under it, which is why the technique article cites it.[^s1] It was written by Milton Chen.[^s1][^s2] Its asymmetry finding is supported by van Eijk and colleagues' 2010 study of 2D and 3D videoconferencing and challenged by Horstmann and Linke's 2022 gaze-cone experiments.[^s5][^s3] It belongs with video-call communication and with the broader skill of eye contact, since it addresses how eye contact survives the shift from a shared room to a screen.[^s1][^s4]
