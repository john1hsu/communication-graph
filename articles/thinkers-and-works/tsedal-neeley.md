# Tsedal Neeley

Tsedal Neeley is an organizational-behaviour scholar at Harvard Business School, where she has held the Naylor Fitzhugh Professorship of Business Administration and senior leadership roles in the MBA programme.[^s4] Her research asks what happens to communication, status and trust when global companies make everyone work in one shared language, usually English, and how leaders of dispersed teams can keep people connected.[^s1][^s2] For practitioners, her work explains why a meeting in a lingua franca can quietly sort a team into insiders and outsiders, and what to do about it.[^s3][^s5]

## Origin

Neeley's early field research followed a Paris-based high-tech firm that had adopted English as its common language. In a 2013 *Organization Science* paper she reported that its non-native English speakers felt a loss of status whatever their fluency, and showed similar resentment and distrust towards native-speaking colleagues.[^s1] How fluent people believed they were mattered more than their measured fluency, and those who rated themselves as moderately fluent were the most anxious about their performance and their jobs.[^s1]

She then studied Rakuten, the Japanese online retailer whose chief executive announced in March 2010 that roughly 7,100 employees had two years to become proficient in English or risk demotion; at the time only about one in ten spoke it.[^s3] Neeley wrote a Harvard case on the programme, which the company called "Englishnization", and later expanded the research into *The Language of Global Success* (Princeton University Press, 2017), based on about 650 interviews at Rakuten sites in eight countries over the five years after the switch.[^s2][^s3] The book argues that a common corporate language turns everyone into a kind of expatriate: native-language "linguistic expats" at home, native English speakers who must absorb an unfamiliar company culture, and people native to neither, who in her data adjusted most easily.[^s2]

With Pamela Hinds and Catherine Cramton she also ran an ethnographic study of 96 members of six globally distributed software teams. Differences in lingua franca fluency, location and nationality created potential faultlines, but these turned into divisive "us versus them" subgroups only in teams that also had power contests, and the two reinforced each other.[^s5]

Her later book, *Remote Work Revolution: Succeeding from Anywhere* (HarperCollins, 2021), applied the same concern with distance, trust and inclusion to remote and hybrid teams.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Neeley's findings explain behaviour that managers often misread. A capable engineer who goes quiet in English-language meetings may be protecting status rather than lacking ideas, and the most anxious people are often the middle-fluency speakers, not the weakest.[^s1][^s3] Language friction can also become the visible symbol of deeper disputes over power between sites, so fixing only vocabulary may not end the conflict.[^s5]

Her practical advice follows from this. Leaders should state clearly that working in a second language is not a deficiency and that native speakers share responsibility for making communication work; managers should recognise that people often underrate their own skills and use benchmarks to reduce anxiety; and companies expanding abroad need a deliberate language strategy rather than an unplanned default.[^s3] For remote teams, she recommends agreeing explicit communication norms, relaunching the team every six to eight weeks, choosing the channel to fit the task rather than defaulting to video, and building emotional trust through deliberate, voluntary self-disclosure alongside the faster cognitive trust that comes from reliability.[^s4]

A workplace illustration: a project lead notices that colleagues in one overseas office rarely speak in the weekly English-language call. Applying Neeley's work, the lead asks for written input before the call, slows the pace, invites contributions by name, and treats the silence as a status and fluency problem to address rather than as disengagement.[^s1][^s3]

## Limits

Her language research rests mainly on qualitative case studies and interviews in a small number of companies, which is well suited to uncovering mechanisms but cannot by itself show how often the effects occur elsewhere.[^s1][^s5] The Rakuten study describes one company with an unusually strong top-down mandate, so results may differ where a lingua franca emerges gradually.[^s2][^s3] The remote-work guidance is synthesis and practitioner advice rather than tested interventions.[^s4]

## Connections

Neeley **introduced** much of the workplace framing behind communicating across languages (`cm:communicating-across-languages`): her argument that firms need a language strategy and that native speakers must adapt too underlies that technique.[^s3] She **authored** *The Language of Global Success* (`cm:the-language-of-global-success`), her Rakuten study, and *Remote Work Revolution* (`cm:remote-work-revolution`), her guide to leading dispersed teams.[^s2][^s4] Her study with Hinds and Cramton (`cm:hinds-neeley-cramton-2014`) is a key piece of evidence on language barriers in a `cm:multicultural-team`.[^s5] Her remote-work advice connects to `cm:channel-selection` and `cm:video-call-communication`.[^s4]
