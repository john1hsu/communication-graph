# The Effects of Feedback Interventions on Performance

This 1996 *Psychological Bulletin* paper by Avraham N. Kluger and Angelo DeNisi is the study most often cited when someone says that feedback can backfire. Pooling 607 effect sizes from 23,663 observations, it found that giving people feedback improved their performance on average by d = 0.41, a moderate effect, but that more than a third of feedback interventions made performance worse than giving no feedback at all.[^s1] For anyone who gives feedback at work, its lesson is that the question is not whether to give feedback but what the feedback makes the other person pay attention to.[^s3]

## Origin

Kluger, at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, and DeNisi began with a historical review. They showed that harmful effects of feedback had been reported since the early 1900s but were routinely explained away, partly because influential reviews such as Thorndike's law of effect and Ammons' 1956 summary had treated feedback as reliably beneficial.[^s3][^s1] In 1992 they searched psychology and technical-report databases, considered about 3,000 papers and reports, and kept only the 131 (about 5%) that compared a feedback group with a control group that got none, measured performance, had at least 10 participants and reported enough statistics to compute an effect.[^s2] They followed it with a shorter 1998 review for a general psychology audience and a 2000 article for managers on 360-degree appraisals.[^s3][^s4]

## What the study did and found

Over 38% of the effects were negative; after excluding 91 effects from a single researcher's studies, roughly a third still were.[^s2] The authors showed that sampling error could not explain this spread, and that the sign of the feedback did not either: on average, positive and negative feedback did not differ.[^s1][^s3]

To account for the pattern they proposed feedback intervention theory. People regulate behaviour by comparing feedback with goals; attention is limited; and feedback moves attention between three levels, from the details of the task, to motivation for the task, to the self.[^s1][^s3] Feedback works best when it keeps attention on the task and how to learn it, and loses power as it draws attention to the self.[^s1] Consistent with this, interventions carrying praise or discouraging, critical messages had weaker effects; pairing feedback with goal setting strengthened them; and effects were more positive on familiar or simple tasks.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Most workplace feedback carries self-level cues by default: ratings against colleagues, "great job", or criticism of the person rather than the work. The study suggests those are exactly the elements that dilute or reverse feedback's benefit.[^s3] DeNisi and Kluger applied this to 360-degree appraisals, arguing that many such systems are designed in ways that make feedback less effective, and urged organisations to evaluate them rather than assume they help.[^s4]

A workplace illustration: after a quarterly review, a team lead tells an engineer, "You're in the bottom quartile on ticket closure - you need to step it up." That message is normative and aimed at the self. A version closer to the study's findings would say, "Tickets that need a second reviewer are taking four days; let's aim for two next quarter and try pairing on the first review." It points to the task, gives specific information about how to improve and adds a goal, the combination the meta-analysis associated with larger gains.[^s3][^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The headline result has held up, so it is rated robust here. A 2020 meta-analysis of 435 educational studies (994 effects, more than 61,000 participants) again found a positive average (d = 0.48 after removing outliers) with very high heterogeneity, and reported that 17% of effects were negative, most of them from feedback that carried little information, such as rewards or punishments alone.[^s5] A 2025 Cochrane review of 292 studies of audit and feedback for health professionals found a median absolute improvement of 2.7% with wide variation, larger where baseline performance was low, delivery was interactive and an action plan was attached.[^s6]

The limits are real. The original studies did not measure attention, so feedback intervention theory's mechanism could only be inferred from outcomes; the moderator tests were correlational and limited to two-way interactions; the search was mostly English-language published work; and personality could not be examined.[^s2] The share of harmful effects is also lower in later, differently scoped meta-analyses, so "a third of feedback hurts" should be read as a 1996 estimate, not a constant.[^s5]

## Connections

The study was **introduced by Avraham N. Kluger** (`cm:avraham-n-kluger`) and **Angelo DeNisi** (`cm:angelo-denisi`), and it proposed the **feedback intervention theory** (`cm:feedback-intervention-theory`) that the feedback concept is explained by.[^s1] It is **supported by Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie's 2020 meta-analysis** (`cm:wisniewski-zierer-hattie-2020`), which partly re-analysed its data and confirmed both the average benefit and the variability,[^s5] and by the **2025 Cochrane review by Ivers and colleagues** (`cm:ivers-et-al-2025`), which found the same variable pattern in professional practice.[^s6] Its authors **applied it to performance reviews** (`cm:performance-review`), especially 360-degree appraisals.[^s4] It sits under **feedback** and underpins task-focused techniques such as **Situation-Behavior-Impact** and **feedforward**, and it is a natural companion to Hattie and Timperley's feedback model and to everyday **one-on-one meetings**, a common setting for the task-focused feedback it recommends.[^s3][^s5]
