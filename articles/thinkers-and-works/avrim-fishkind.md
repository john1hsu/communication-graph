# Avrim Fishkind

Avrim Fishkind is an American emergency psychiatrist based in Houston, Texas. In 2002 he was medical director of the NeuroPsychiatric Center of Houston; by 2012 he was with JSA Health Telepsychiatry, and in 2018 he was its chief executive and chief medical officer and a past president of the American Association for Emergency Psychiatry (AAEP).[^s1][^s2][^s3] His contribution to communication practice is a compact, ten-point script for talking an agitated person down, which he first published as "10 commandments for safety" and which later became the backbone of the most widely cited clinical guidance on verbal de-escalation.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Fishkind's 2002 article in *Current Psychiatry* argued that psychiatrists were trained to reach for medication and restraint and under-trained in talking, and that verbal de-escalation may be the best way to prevent violence on a unit.[^s1] He described agitation as a progression from restlessness to verbal threats to physical aggression, and offered ten rules to interrupt it: keep a safe distance and leave an exit, stay calm in voice, face and posture, let one person do the talking, keep language brief and repeat it, name the person's wants and feelings, listen and check your understanding, agree where you can or agree to disagree, set honest limits, offer choices, and debrief everyone afterwards.[^s1] He was candid that repetition is the point: a simple message may need saying many times before an agitated listener takes it in.[^s1]

A decade later the AAEP's Project BETA (Best Practices in Evaluation and Treatment of Agitation), launched in October 2010, published five consensus statements on agitation, one of them on verbal de-escalation.[^s4] Fishkind was a co-author of that statement, led by Janet Richmond, which set out ten domains of de-escalation and cited his 2002 article as the literature source for them.[^s2] The two lists match almost point for point - personal space, not being provocative, one person making verbal contact, conciseness, wants and feelings, close listening, agreeing or agreeing to disagree, clear limits, choices and optimism, and debriefing.[^s1][^s2] In 2018 Fishkind republished the guidelines for clinicians as "10 commandments for verbal de-escalation", adding a graduated fallback from stating a fact through persuasion and inducement, with coercion only as a last resort.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Outside hospitals, the value of Fishkind's version is that it is short enough to remember under stress and concrete enough to train. Each rule is a behaviour a receptionist, team lead or security officer can check in the moment - how close am I standing, is only one of us talking, have I said back what they want - rather than an instruction to "be calm".[^s3] The Project BETA statement framed the goals the same way: keep everyone safe, help the person regain control of their own behaviour, and avoid restraint and coercive moves that inflame agitation, reporting that verbal de-escalation often works in under five minutes.[^s2] Law-enforcement trainers have since adapted the ten domains for police encounters, while noting that some clinical rules, such as always leaving an exit, may conflict with an officer's duty to contain a scene.[^s5]

For workplace communicators, the takeaway is the shape of the script: lower your own arousal first, make one calm point of contact, listen for and name the feeling, agree with what is true, and only then state a limit as a matter of fact with real choices attached.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

Fishkind's 2002 rules are a clinician's distillation of experience and existing training programmes, not the output of trials; the article reports no outcome data.[^s1] The Project BETA authors themselves wrote that scientific studies of verbal de-escalation are few and seldom specify techniques or test their efficacy, so the ten domains rest on expert consensus.[^s2] His attribution as the source of "de-escalation" is therefore narrow: the idea of calming agitation with words predates him, and what he introduced is the ten-step verbal format that BETA canonised and that later guides adapt.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Fishkind is credited here with introducing (in the sense of codifying and popularising) the ten-point verbal de-escalation script, confidence medium because the wider practice is older.[^s2] He authored *Calming agitation with words, not drugs* (2002) and co-authored the Project BETA verbal de-escalation consensus statement (2012).[^s1][^s2] His rules target verbal aggression, lean on active listening and labeling emotions ("identify wants and feelings"), and use proxemics in the personal-space rule, which links him to difficult conversations at their most heated.[^s1][^s3]
