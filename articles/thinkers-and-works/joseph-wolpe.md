# Joseph Wolpe

Joseph Wolpe (1915-1997) was a South African-born psychiatrist and one of the founders of behaviour therapy.[^s2][^s4] Two of his clinical tools now sit at the centre of workplace communication training: assertive responding, which he prescribed as a way to break the hold of social anxiety and carefully separated from aggression, and systematic desensitization, the graded-exposure method later adopted for fear of public speaking.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Wolpe was born in Johannesburg and trained in medicine at the University of the Witwatersrand, earning his MB in 1939 and a research MD in 1948.[^s2][^s3] As an army medical officer in the Second World War he treated what were then called war neuroses, and, unimpressed by the drug and psychoanalytic treatments of the day, he turned to Pavlov's conditioning, Clark Hull's learning theory and animal studies of induced neurosis.[^s2] His 1958 book *Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition* argued that anxiety could be weakened by evoking a response incompatible with it, and that treatments should be tested systematically rather than taken on faith.[^s2][^s4] He moved to the United States in 1956, worked at the University of Virginia and then Temple University, where he founded a behaviour therapy unit, and retired to Pepperdine in 1988.[^s2][^s3] He co-founded the Association for Advancement of Behavior Therapy in 1966, founded the *Journal of Behavior Therapy and Experimental Psychiatry*, and received the American Psychological Association's Distinguished Scientific Award for the Applications of Psychology in 1979.[^s2][^s3]

Assertion was one of his first practical inhibitors of anxiety: he told anxious clients to act assertively in the situations they feared, on the reasoning that standing up for oneself and feeling anxious do not easily coexist.[^s3] The idea had a precursor. Andrew Salter's 1949 *Conditioned Reflex Therapy* urged inhibited people to express their feelings openly, including through "I" talk, but it made little impact at the time; Wolpe folded open self-expression into a systematic therapy and gave it lasting currency.[^s1][^s5] With his former student Arnold Lazarus he wrote *Behavior Therapy Techniques* (1966) and built the first questionnaire for measuring assertiveness.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

Wolpe's most useful legacy for people at work is a distinction rather than a slogan. He set assertiveness apart from aggression: you can state a need or refuse a request without putting the other person down.[^s1] That three-way contrast - passive, aggressive, assertive - still frames how trainers teach someone to push back on an unrealistic deadline, decline extra work or raise a concern with a manager. Instead of swallowing the objection or snapping "That's ridiculous," the assertive version names the constraint and offers a route forward: "I can't deliver all three by Friday without dropping testing; which one matters most?"[^s1]

His second legacy is treating communication fear as something learned and therefore unlearnable in small steps. His habit of asking clients to imagine anxiety-provoking scenes in graded order, built on Edmund Jacobson's relaxation work, became systematic desensitization, the same step-by-step logic now used to tame fear of presenting.[^s3] For a reader the practical lesson is that assertiveness is rehearsed, not summoned: start with a low-stakes request, then work up to the meeting you dread.[^s1][^s3] Salkovskis notes that the once-radical idea that fears shrink when you face them has become common sense.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

Assertiveness training grew quickly in the 1970s, aided by Alberti and Emmons's 1970 self-help book and by the civil rights and women's movements, and then faded from the research literature after the 1980s as funding moved to diagnosis-specific trials; its methods often survived under new names, such as interpersonal effectiveness skills.[^s1] Speed, Goldstein and Goldfried's 2018 review finds the training helped with social anxiety, depression and self-esteem in a range of studies, though many trials are old and small.[^s1] Two cautions follow. The popular "assertive rights" lists come from Alberti and Emmons and later self-help writers, not from Wolpe, whose aim was clinical: reducing anxiety.[^s1] And Salter's obituary observes that the pioneering credit for assertion and "I" talk is seldom given to him.[^s5]

## Connections

Wolpe introduced assertive communication in its modern clinical sense, framing assertion as an anxiety-reducing skill and distinguishing it from aggression.[^s1] He authored *Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition* (1958), where his reciprocal-inhibition rationale first appeared, and co-authored *Behavior Therapy Techniques* (1966) with Arnold Lazarus.[^s2] He was influenced by Andrew Salter, whose imagery-and-feeling techniques helped lay the foundation for systematic desensitization.[^s5] He is related to systematic desensitization, his best-known procedure, and to graded assertiveness, saying no and I-statements, all descendants of the assertion training he championed.[^s1][^s3] His live demonstrations of therapy are often compared with Carl Rogers's earlier recordings of therapy sessions.[^s2]
