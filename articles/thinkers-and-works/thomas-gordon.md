# Thomas Gordon

Thomas Gordon (1918-2002) was an American clinical psychologist who took the listening skills of client-centred therapy out of the counselling room and packaged them as short, teachable courses for parents, teachers and managers.[^s3][^s4] Much of the everyday vocabulary of workplace conflict comes from his "Gordon model": I-messages versus blaming you-messages, the twelve communication roadblocks, active listening as reflecting back feelings and meaning, deciding who "owns" a problem, and the no-lose method of settling disputes.[^s1][^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Gordon was born in Paris, Illinois, studied at DePauw and Ohio State, and earned his PhD at the University of Chicago in 1949 after serving in the Army Air Force in the Second World War.[^s3] At Chicago he counselled students at the university's counselling centre in 1946-47 under his professor and mentor Carl Rogers, contributed a chapter to a Rogers book on client-centred therapy, and wrote his dissertation on what blocks communication.[^s1] A spell at the National Training Laboratory drew him to leadership; his 1955 book *Group-Centered Leadership* sold poorly, but in 1957 his first leadership course introduced the twelve communication roadblocks.[^s1] He moved to California as a consultant in 1954, opened a private practice in 1958 and founded Effectiveness Training, Inc. (later Gordon Training International) in 1968.[^s3]

In 1962 he designed a course for parents, and his 1970 book *P.E.T.: Parent Effectiveness Training* added I-messages and the "behaviour window" for working out whose problem a behaviour is.[^s1][^s3] Teacher Effectiveness Training followed, and Leader Effectiveness Training applied the same model to supervisors; Gordon credits a twelve-year consulting relationship with a Los Angeles-area employer of nearly 1,000 staff, whose managers were trained in it, with shaping the leadership course.[^s1][^s2] His organisation reports his books in more than 32 languages, P.E.T. taught to over a million parents, and Nobel Peace Prize nominations in 1997, 1998 and 1999; he also received the American Psychological Foundation's 1999 gold medal for psychology in the public interest.[^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

Gordon's central workplace idea is that the ordinary way people raise problems is blaming and judgemental, which makes the listener defensive. He labelled that pattern the you-message and offered the I-message, a non-blaming description of the behaviour, its concrete effect on the speaker and the speaker's feeling, as the alternative.[^s5][^s2] A manager who says "You're careless with the client files" sends a you-message; "When the client file goes out without the signed annex, I have to reopen it with legal, and I'm worried about the deadline" sends an I-message. Breeding's textbook notes that the two are easily confused, since a "when you…" opener can still carry blame.[^s5]

His roadblocks are a practical checklist for listeners. Ordering, threatening, moralising, advising, criticising and even praising or sympathising tend to shut a conversation down when the other person is the one with the problem.[^s4] The remedy is active listening, which Gordon took from client-centred practice and adopted under a name borrowed from a former student's article.[^s1] The model's sorting question, who owns the problem, tells a leader which tool to reach for: listen when the other person is troubled, send an I-message when the behaviour is causing you a problem.[^s4] When needs genuinely collide, his no-lose method, six steps adapted from John Dewey's problem-solving sequence, defines the problem in terms of each side's needs before anyone proposes a solution.[^s1] Later scripts such as nonviolent communication's four-part request extend his three-part I-message.[^s5]

## Limits

Gordon's influence rests far more on training reach than on controlled trials. The California Evidence-Based Clearinghouse lists one meta-analysis and nine studies of P.E.T. from the 1970s to the 1990s; only one was randomised, most were small with no follow-up, and results were mixed, with some showing better conflict-resolution skills or attitudes and others showing no difference from controls.[^s6] There is no comparable body of evidence on Leader Effectiveness Training in workplaces, and much published material about it comes from his own organisation.[^s2][^s6] Treat the specific tools on their own evidence: the I-statements and accusatory you-statements articles summarise the rating studies that test his you-message idea.[^s5]

## Connections

Gordon **introduced the accusatory you-message** as the blaming opposite of the I-message, a contrast textbooks still teach.[^s5] He **introduced the communication roadblocks** in his 1957 leadership course and the **no-lose method** of conflict resolution, which he **adapted from John Dewey**; his whole approach was **influenced by Carl Rogers**, his mentor at Chicago.[^s1] He **authored *Parent Effectiveness Training***, the 1970 bestseller that spread the model, and **authored *Leader Effectiveness Training***, its workplace version.[^s3][^s2] He is also credited with I-statements and is related to active listening, problem ownership and defensiveness, the reaction his methods aim to avoid.[^s1][^s4]
