# Alvin Toffler

Alvin Toffler was an American writer and futurist whose bestselling books included *Future Shock*, *The Third Wave* and *Powershift*, and who co-founded a consulting firm that advised companies and governments on technological and social change.[^s4] Britannica lists *Future Shock* (1970) among the notable basic works of futurology.[^s3] For people who communicate at work, his lasting contribution is vocabulary: he gave a mass audience the phrase information overload for the experience of receiving more input than you can sensibly process, and he coined adhocracy for the fluid, project-based organisations that generate so much of that input.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Toffler was born in New York in 1928, studied English at New York University, worked in factories as a welder, and then became a labour reporter and later labour editor and columnist at *Fortune* before going freelance in 1962.[^s5] His wife Heidi Toffler researched and edited his major books and was later credited as co-author.[^s5][^s4] *Future Shock* argued that society was changing faster than people could adapt, describing the condition as too much change in too short a time; it sold millions of copies in more than 100 countries.[^s5][^s6]

Obituaries often credit Toffler with coining "information overload", but the scholarly record is more precise.[^s5][^s6] Information scientists David Bawden and Lyn Robinson trace the phrase to the social scientist Bertram Gross, who in 1964 used it for a state in which a system's information inputs exceed its capacity to process them; Toffler's book is what brought the idea to wide public attention.[^s1] They also note that the underlying complaint is far older, running from the sociologist Georg Simmel in the late nineteenth century back to medieval and classical laments about too many books.[^s1] The accurate attribution, then, is that Gross named it and Toffler popularised it.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Toffler's framing treated overload as a human problem, not just a technical one: Bawden and Robinson summarise his claim that overloaded perception, thinking and decision-making cause real distress.[^s1] That framing shapes how workplace communication is discussed today. When a team lead says the inbox is unmanageable, they are using Toffler's lens, and the practical response follows from it: send fewer and clearer messages, put the decision or request first, and agree when replies are expected, instead of assuming that more information is always better.[^s1]

His second coinage points to a cause. Britannica credits Toffler with coining adhocracy in 1970 for an emerging form of organisation built around temporary, flexible teams rather than rigid hierarchy, a type that Henry Mintzberg developed more fully in 1979.[^s2] In a matrix or project-based company, people sit on several short-lived teams at once, so status updates, chat threads and meeting invitations multiply. A practical takeaway for a manager setting up a cross-functional project is to decide early which channel holds decisions and who needs to be told what, because an adhocracy does not supply those routes by default.[^s2][^s1]

## Limits

Toffler wrote as a futurist and popular author, not as an experimental researcher, so his books are best read as influential framing rather than evidence.[^s3][^s1] The concept he popularised also has critics: Bawden and Robinson report that some scholars have called overload a phantom or a myth of modern culture, that studies find many people cope without much trouble, and that Clay Shirky reframed the problem as filter failure rather than too much information.[^s1] They nonetheless conclude that overload is real and deserves to be taken seriously, with remedies ranging from filtering and better information design to deliberately slowing down.[^s1] Popular attributions that make Toffler the inventor of the term should be corrected to Gross.[^s1][^s6]

## Connections

Toffler popularised information overload, the barrier this graph treats under its own article, while Bertram Gross is credited with the phrase itself.[^s1] He introduced the term adhocracy, a way of organising that increases the volume of lateral messages people must handle.[^s2] He wrote *Future Shock*, the 1970 book that carried both ideas to a mass readership.[^s3][^s5] His view that people have limited capacity to absorb change and information links him to cognitive load and to the wider study of organisational communication, and the email and messaging overload that managers complain about today is the workplace form of the problem he described.[^s1]
