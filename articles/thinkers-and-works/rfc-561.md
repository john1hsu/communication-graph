# RFC 561

RFC 561, "Standardizing Network Mail Headers", is a four-author ARPANET proposal dated 5 September 1973 that gave network email its first shared header format.[^s1] It defined a message as a header, a blank line and then the body, and named three standard header items - FROM (the author and host), DATE (date, time and time zone) and SUBJECT (a free-text line) - with any other keyword-and-colon field allowed after them.[^s1] Its authors were Abhay Bhushan of MIT, Ken Pogran of MIT's Multics group, Ray Tomlinson of BBN and Jim White of SRI.[^s1] For anyone writing email at work today, it is the document where the subject line first appears as a standard part of every message.[^s2]

## Origin

By 1973 network mail travelled over the ARPANET's file transfer protocol, which had no way to say who wrote a message, when, or what it was about; each host formatted that information its own way, so programs could hardly sort or process incoming mail.[^s1] The authors saw new protocol commands as the long-term answer but proposed a quick interim convention, asked mail programs that already added headers to adopt it, and urged other hosts to encourage their users to follow it.[^s1] Historian of email Craig Partridge reads the proposal as requiring every message to begin with the three fields From, Subject and Date, and notes that its blank-line separator between header and body survives in email today.[^s2] It had no To field, so a reader could not see who else had received a message, and it wrote addresses with the word "at" rather than the @ sign.[^s2]

RFC 561 came from an unofficial committee, so it could only recommend; a 1977 successor described it as still an unofficial standard, followed by most because it was useful.[^s3] Later standards built on it: RFC 680 (1975) added To, CC and reply fields, RFC 724 and RFC 733 (1977) formalised the format, and RFC 822 (1982) became the basic Internet standard for decades.[^s2] The current specification, RFC 5322 (2008), still carries the Subject field.[^s4]

## Why it matters

The subject line exists so that a reader can tell what a message is about before reading it. RFC 561 gave it a fixed, labelled place in every header; its own example is a specific announcement title, "Multi-Site Journal Meeting Announcement", not a vague word.[^s1] The 1977 standard spelled out the purpose: the Subject field should give as much information as needed to summarise or indicate the nature of the message.[^s3] RFC 5322 still defines it as a short string identifying the topic of the message.[^s4] The habit of writing informative, specific subject lines is therefore not a modern productivity trick but a use of the field as its designers intended.[^s3][^s4]

A second design choice also still matters. When John Vittal later built the "answer" command, it copied the original subject into the reply, which turned one-way memos into threaded conversation.[^s2] That is why a stale subject follows a thread long after its topic has changed, and why RFC 5322 advises a single "Re: " prefix rather than a growing stack of them.[^s4]

## Lessons for workplace email

Read as a design document, RFC 561 suggests three practical habits. First, treat the subject as a summary, not a greeting: compare "Question" with "Decision needed by Friday: Q3 vendor renewal", which does the job the 1977 definition describes.[^s3] Second, change the subject when a reply thread turns to a new topic, because reply tools carry the old subject forward automatically.[^s2][^s4] Third, remember that the standards made the subject optional after 1977, so nothing in the software forces a useful one; only the writer can.[^s3][^s4]

## Limits

RFC 561 is a technical specification, not communication research; it says nothing about how readers triage email or which subject lines work best, so its relevance is as the origin and intended purpose of the field.[^s1] Partridge treats the three fields as required, while the 1977 standard made Subject optional and kept only date and originator mandatory, so claims that subject lines have always been compulsory overstate the record.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

RFC 561 is **authored by** Ray Tomlinson with Bhushan, Pogran and White; Tomlinson had already built the first network email between hosts.[^s1][^s2] It **introduces** the subject line that the email subject lines technique teaches people to write well, since Partridge identifies it as the first standard to put a Subject field in every message.[^s2] It is **applied in** email, the channel whose header it standardised.[^s1] It sits under digital communication; the later header standards it led to, from RFC 680 to RFC 5322, kept its basic shape, and related works in this graph such as *The Network Nation* and the 1995 netiquette guide RFC 1855 cover how people came to use the medium.[^s2][^s4]
