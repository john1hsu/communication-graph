# Nicholas Epley

Nicholas Epley is a behavioural scientist at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business, where he holds the John Templeton Keller Distinguished Service Professorship and directs the Roman Family Center for Decision Research.[^s5] His research asks how people reason about other people's minds and why capable, well-meaning people still misread each other.[^s5] For anyone trying to communicate better at work, his experiments supply both a diagnosis, that we use our own head as the template for everyone else's, and two concrete fixes: list others' contributions before your own, and ask instead of guessing.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Epley was already at Chicago's business school when he co-authored the 2006 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* paper with Eugene Caruso and Max Bazerman that introduced the "unpacking" manipulation behind considering others' contributions first.[^s1] Participants who went through each collaborator and that person's contributions before judging their own claimed less of the credit, but the exercise lowered satisfaction among the heaviest contributors, especially in groups rewarded as a whole.[^s1] In 2018 Tal Eyal, Mary Steffel and Epley reported 25 experiments in which telling people to imagine another person's point of view did not reliably make them more accurate about that person and sometimes made them slightly less accurate; only perspective getting, hearing the other person's view directly, improved accuracy.[^s2]

He gathered this line of work for a general audience in *Mindwise: How We Understand What Others Think, Believe, Feel, and Want* (Knopf, 2014).[^s4] The book covers why people assume others share their thoughts and feelings and overrate how well they know family and friends, and it says plainly that it will not make other people fully readable.[^s4] His honours include the American Psychological Association's 2011 early-career award for distinguished scientific contribution and a 2015 book prize for promoting social and personality science.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Much of Epley's work shows that senders overestimate how clearly they come across. In a 2005 study with Justin Kruger, people sending sarcastic or serious messages expected recipients to read the tone correctly about 78% of the time; voice listeners managed about 75%, but email readers only about 56%.[^s3] The gap closed when senders read their message aloud in the opposite tone, which pointed to egocentrism, not carelessness, as the cause.[^s3] Epley's practical advice was to use email for plain content and switch to a call when emotion matters.[^s3]

The same egocentric pull distorts conversation choices. In a 2021 set of 12 experiments with more than 1,800 participants, Michael Kardas, Amit Kumar and Epley found that strangers expected deep conversations to be more awkward and less enjoyable than they turned out to be, and underestimated how interested partners were in hearing personal thoughts.[^s6] People chose deeper topics when told that others usually underrate that interest.[^s6]

## Applying his work at work

Three habits come straight from his findings. Before writing a status update or self-review, name each colleague and what they did before describing your own share, and plan for the uneven split that list may reveal.[^s1] Before acting on what a peer or client "must" think, ask them; imagining harder is not a substitute for new information.[^s2] Before sending an email whose tone matters, reread it in the opposite tone or pick up the phone.[^s3]

A workplace illustration: a team lead is sure a quiet engineer resents a reorganisation and drafts a carefully reassuring email. Epley's findings suggest two problems: the lead's reading of the engineer is a guess, and the reassuring tone may not come through in text. A short call that opens with a question, such as asking how the change looks from the engineer's side, tests both assumptions at once.[^s2][^s3]

## Limits

His findings are mostly laboratory and student or online samples, and effect sizes vary with the task.[^s2][^s6] The 2018 paper does not show that imagining another view is useless, only that it did not reliably raise accuracy; the authors found it reduced reliance on one's own view without supplying better information.[^s2] The credit-unpacking fix carries a social cost for high contributors that teams should plan for.[^s1] Press summaries of single studies, including those cited here, report averages and should not be read as fixed rules for every person or culture.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

Epley **introduced** considering others' contributions first (`cm:considering-others-contributions-first`) through the 2006 unpacking studies with Caruso and Bazerman.[^s1] He **introduced** perspective getting (`cm:perspective-getting`) as the evidence-backed alternative to perspective taking in his 2018 work with Eyal and Steffel.[^s2] He **authored** *Mindwise* (`cm:mindwise`), his book-length synthesis on reading other minds.[^s4] His email-tone research ties egocentric bias to channel selection, and his conversation studies bear on small talk and self-disclosure.[^s3][^s6]
