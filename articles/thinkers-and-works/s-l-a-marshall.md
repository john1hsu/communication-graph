# S. L. A. Marshall

Samuel Lyman Atwood Marshall (1900-1977) was a newspaperman turned U.S. Army historian whose way of questioning soldiers straight after a fight became the model for the after-action review, the team debrief now used in hospitals, fire services and project teams.[^s1] He is also a cautionary figure. His most famous finding, that only about a quarter of infantrymen fired their weapons, was later shown to rest on no recorded data.[^s2][^s3] For anyone who runs debriefs or reports what a group said, he offers two lessons: how to get people to rebuild an event together, and why the write-up must stay faithful to what they actually said.

## Origin

Marshall served on the Army team documenting the Second World War as it happened. In the Pacific he found that orders and official records could not explain what had really happened in a battle. So he developed what he called the "interview after combat": he gathered the survivors of an action as soon as the fighting stopped and led them through it together, step by step.[^s1] He first used it on Makin Island in 1943, questioning commanders and men as one group over several days and fitting their accounts together like a jigsaw.[^s4] By the end of the war more than 2,000 such interviews had been held in Europe alone, and Marshall and colleagues carried the method on to Korea and Vietnam.[^s1] His 1947 book *Men Against Fire* drew on these interviews for its claims about soldiers' behaviour in combat.[^s2]

## Key ideas

**Rebuild the event with the people who were there, together.** Marshall ran the sessions in chronological order, starting from the first contact and using a map to establish who was where and what each person did next.[^s4] Hearing each other's accounts let participants correct details and fill gaps that no single witness could.[^s1]

**Set rank aside and keep it non-punitive.** Army researchers later singled out the method because it drew out objective, reliable accounts of performance in an atmosphere that did not assign blame.[^s1] An officer who escorted Marshall in Korea in 1953 described the sessions as informal and open-ended, held within a few hours of combat and lasting one to two hours.[^s3]

**From interview to after-action review.** In the 1970s the Army turned away from the old umpire's critique, a lecture by a senior officer delivered long after an exercise that bred resentment. Researchers at the Army Research Institute built a new participant-led discussion, which became the after-action review. They named Marshall's interview as one of its two foundations, alongside simulation systems that gave everyone an objective record of what had happened.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Marshall's method anticipates what good debriefs still need: speed, everyone in the room, a shared timeline before any judgement, and a climate in which junior people can contradict senior ones.[^s1] Even his harshest critic, the Army historian Roger Spiller, credited the interview after combat as a real innovation from which soldiers and commanders learned.[^s2]

The criticism matters as much. Spiller found that Marshall's sample size changed from telling to telling (about 400 companies, later 603, later "something over 500"). His assistant did not recall him ever asking who had fired, and his notebooks and letters showed no statistical work. Spiller concluded the ratio of fire could not be proved and drew its authority from a scientific gloss.[^s2] A 2003 study in the Army War College's journal added an escort officer's testimony that the firing question came up only in passing. It judged the method effective for reconstructing battles but the figures likely an exaggeration or a guess.[^s3] The workplace lesson is that a debrief is only as trustworthy as its record: write down what people said, keep counts you can show, and do not let the facilitator's story replace the group's.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

- **Introduced the interview after combat** — the post-action group interview he devised in the Pacific in 1943.[^s1]
- **Influenced the after-action review** — Army researchers cite his interview as a model for the AAR's non-punitive, participant-led discussion.[^s1]
- **Authored *Men Against Fire*** (1947), the book that published the contested ratio-of-fire claim.[^s2]
- **Challenged by Spiller (1988)** — the RUSI Journal critique that found no evidence for the fire ratio.[^s2]
- **Challenged by Chambers (2003)** — new testimony that the fire question was incidental and the figures probably a guess.[^s3]
- **Related:** open-ended questions and facilitation, which his sessions relied on, and psychological safety, the non-punitive climate the AAR inherited from them.[^s1][^s3]
