# Hesitation phenomena in spontaneous English speech

"Hesitation phenomena in spontaneous English speech" is a 1959 paper by Howard Maclay and Charles E. Osgood, published in the linguistics journal *Word*. It describes itself as an exploratory, quantitative look at four kinds of hesitation in unscripted talk: filled pauses ("uh", "um"), unfilled or silent pauses, repeats and false starts.[^s1] Later reviews treat it as the source of the standard way of sorting disfluencies, which is why most modern advice about filler words, and most research on them, starts from its categories.[^s2]

## Origin

Maclay and Osgood worked from recordings of about twelve participants speaking freely at a conference, rather than reading prepared text, and coded every hesitation by type and by where it fell in the sentence.[^s1] Osgood was already known for measuring word meaning, and the paper's stated aim was psycholinguistic: to use hesitations as a window on the units in which speakers plan speech and on how uncertain each choice is.[^s1] The Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics lists the paper in the bibliography of Willem Levelt's 2013 history of the field, a sign of its standing as an early classic.[^s5]

## Key findings

In Kathryn Bock's 1996 review of language-production methods, the usual taxonomy of disfluencies is credited to this paper.[^s2] Bock also reports two of its results that still matter: two independent coders agreed on about 95% of hesitation judgements, showing the categories could be scored reliably, and the four types showed few sizeable correlations with one another, within or across speakers, suggesting they have different sources.[^s2] Later summaries of the paper report that most filled pauses fell at word boundaries, that both filled and silent pauses came more often before content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives) than before function words, and that filled pauses were relatively more likely than silent ones to precede function words.[^s3]

## Why it matters

For anyone working on their delivery, the paper's main lesson is that hesitations are not one habit. A speaker who says "um" a lot may not also repeat words or restart sentences, because the types barely correlate, so it pays to record yourself and count each type separately before trying to fix anything.[^s2] Its link between hesitation and the choice of the next content word also suggests where to practise: in a status update or client briefing, the risky moments are the names, figures and key terms, so rehearsing those words, and allowing a short silent pause instead of a filler while you find them, targets the points where planning load is highest.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The sample was small and narrow, a dozen or so conference speakers, and later studies have not always agreed with its word-class pattern; Gosy's 2023 review notes that a 1971 study found the opposite tendency for filled pauses.[^s3] The paper's framing has also been questioned. It belongs to a tradition that treated "um" and "uh" as symptoms of trouble in speech production.[^s6] Malte Belz argues that "filled pause" is a misnomer, since there is no actual pause in the sound signal, and that the label biases research towards the symptom view; Herbert Clark and Jean Fox Tree's 2002 account instead treats fillers as signals that announce an upcoming delay.[^s4] Later corpus work also shows that filler use shifts over time and across groups, with "um" gaining on "uh" in several Germanic languages and used more by women and younger speakers.[^s6] Treat the paper as the origin of the vocabulary and the counting method, not as the last word on what fillers mean to listeners.

## Connections

The paper **introduces** the category of **filled pauses** (`cm:filled-pauses`) as part of the four-way taxonomy that later reviews follow.[^s2] It was **authored by Howard Maclay** (`cm:howard-maclay`) **and Charles E. Osgood** (`cm:charles-osgood`).[^s1] It is **studied in psycholinguistics** (`cm:psycholinguistics`), where it appears among the field's historical sources.[^s5] Its symptom framing is **challenged by Clark and Fox Tree (2002)** (`cm:clark-fox-tree-2002`), who recast fillers as signals.[^s4] It also connects to **silence and pauses**, **paralanguage** and **vocal delivery**, because silent pauses are the other half of its pause categories and both kinds are part of how a speaker sounds.[^s2][^s3] Later studies of fillers in presentations, such as **Christenfeld (1995)**, and practice methods such as **habit reversal training** build on the counting approach it established.[^s2]
