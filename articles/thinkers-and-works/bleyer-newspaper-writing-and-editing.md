# Bleyer's Newspaper Writing and Editing

*Newspaper Writing and Editing* is a 1913 textbook by Willard Grosvenor Bleyer, then chairman of the journalism course at the University of Wisconsin, written to give student reporters specific rules with worked examples instead of lectures and miscellaneous newspaper models.[^s1] Its central lesson is that the opening of a piece - the "lead" - must carry the gist of the news, so a hurried reader gets what matters first. That rule, and its named failure of burying the point, is what workplace writers inherit today as "don't bury the lede" and "bottom line up front".[^s1][^s6]

## Origin

Bleyer (1873-1935) was a Milwaukee-born English instructor who began teaching a journalism course at Wisconsin in 1905, outlined a four-year journalism programme in 1906 and later headed the university's School of Journalism; he also chaired the National Council on Education for Journalism from 1923 to 1935.[^s2] The university dates its journalism school to that single course of 25 students and to his conviction that professional and academic training should go together.[^s3] He wrote the book after seven years of teaching, and a second edition followed in 1923 with much of the original content unchanged.[^s1][^s4] It was not the first such textbook: Edwin Shuman's *Steps into Journalism* appeared in 1894, and most of the early textbooks of the period dealt with the mechanics of news writing.[^s4] The news form these books taught had grown up in the late nineteenth century; Chip Scanlan's summary for Poynter traces it to the costly telegraph and wire services, and reports historian David Mindich's view that it took shape in coverage of Lincoln's death in 1865.[^s5]

## What it says

- **The beginning is the most emphatic position.** Readers skim a column, so a climax saved for the end is lost on them; the most important part of the news goes in the first paragraph and the most important idea of each paragraph in its first words.[^s1]
- **The lead carries the gist.** A lead states what happened, who was involved, when, where, why and how, and plays up the single most interesting "feature"; for a short story that can be one sentence of 30 to 75 words.[^s1]
- **Write so the end can be cut.** Least important details go last, so a reader can stop early and an editor can trim from the bottom without losing anything essential; each paragraph should be able to serve as the ending.[^s1]
- **Editors fix buried points.** When "the real point of the news has been buried by the writer", the copy-reader must bring it out, rewrite the weak lead and reorder the story.[^s1]
- **Not every piece must summarise.** Bleyer gave the lead deliberately heavy coverage, yet accepted that stories written to entertain can open differently, provided the first sentences make the reader want to go on.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The book turned a newsroom habit into a teachable rule with a reason attached: busy people read the top and skim the rest, so importance, not chronology, decides order.[^s1] The same rule survives almost unchanged in current university guidance on news writing, which tells writers to put the most important facts first, keep the lead short, avoid retelling events in the order they happened, and expect editors to cut from the bottom.[^s6] For workplace writers the transfer is direct. A status email that opens with project history, or a briefing that reaches the recommendation on its last slide, fails Bleyer's tests: the hurried reader misses the point, and the message cannot be cut after its first paragraph without losing it.[^s1][^s6] Two of his checks translate well: could your reader stop after the opening and still act, and would anything essential be lost if the last paragraph were deleted?[^s1][^s6]

## Limits

Bleyer's rules are distilled practice, illustrated with examples from well-edited papers, not experiments; his claim about how quickly people read is an observation, not a measurement.[^s1] The book codified rather than invented the summary lead, which earlier textbooks and newsrooms already used, so it is better seen as an early and influential statement than as the origin of the idea.[^s4][^s5] Its scope is news; the book itself concedes that writing meant to entertain or engage can open with something other than a summary, which matters for stories, speeches and explanatory pieces at work.[^s1] Accounts of exactly when and why the news form arose also differ among historians.[^s5]

## Connections

The book **introduces** the anti-pattern of **burying the lede** (`cm:burying-the-lede`) in the sense that it names the writer's buried point as a fault for editors to fix, decades before the newsroom idiom became common; the edge is marked low confidence because earlier textbooks may have made the same point.[^s1][^s4] It is **authored by** Willard Bleyer (`cm:willard-bleyer`), founder of Wisconsin's journalism programme.[^s2][^s3] Its lead-first rule is a form of **message structure** (`cm:message-structure`) and the newsroom ancestor of **bottom line up front** (`cm:bottom-line-up-front`), the **pyramid principle** (`cm:pyramid-principle`) and **informative headings** (`cm:informative-headings`), and its "the beginning is most emphatic" reasoning parallels **Effective Writing for Army Leaders** (`cm:effective-writing-for-army-leaders`).[^s1][^s6]
