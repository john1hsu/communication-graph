# Effective Writing for Army Leaders

*Effective Writing for Army Leaders* (Department of the Army Pamphlet 600-67, 2 June 1986) is a short guide that told U.S. Army leaders how to demand and coach clear staff writing. It sets out, for leaders, the standards of AR 600-70, *The Army Writing Program*: writing a reader can understand in a single rapid reading, generally free of errors in grammar, mechanics and usage, and clear, concise, organised and to the point.[^s1] Its practical core is three habits - main point first, active voice, short sentences and paragraphs - plus two mentoring tools for checking drafts.[^s1] It is the earliest official source we found that tells writers to put the "bottom line" up front, the habit now taught as BLUF.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The pamphlet was issued by Headquarters, Department of the Army, and applied to the Active Army, National Guard, Reserve and Army civilians.[^s1] It followed AR 600-70, the 1985 regulation that created the Army Writing Program; that regulation has since been rescinded, and the pamphlet itself has been obsolete since January 2013.[^s3] Its foreword, from Army Chief of Staff Gen. John A. Wickham Jr., tied better writing to clear, concise communication across the force.[^s3] The pamphlet justifies itself on readers' time: it says too much Army writing is wordy, confusing and hides its main idea, that leaders lose time and decisions suffer as a result, and that information overload makes a more effective style necessary.[^s1] It does not claim the idea as new - it tells leaders to insist on the bottom line first "as business writers do".[^s1]

## What it says

- **Bottom line first.** Put the recommendation, conclusion or reason for writing in the first or second paragraph, not at the end.[^s1] Leaders are told to ask what the reader would need to keep if everything else were cut.[^s1]
- **Packaging.** Distinct from formatting, packaging is the structure: a short purpose sentence, then the main point, then clearly separated sections.[^s1]
- **Active voice.** The major style change; the pamphlet teaches writers to spot passive verbs (a form of "to be" plus a past participle) and to put the doer first, and estimates that dropping the passive shortens documents by about a fifth.[^s1]
- **Short sentences, words and paragraphs.** An average of 15 words or fewer per sentence, mostly short words, and paragraphs about an inch deep.[^s1]
- **Quick-screen edit.** A reviewer reads the draft once, highlighting the bottom line, passive verbs, long words or jargon, and mechanical errors; the writer then moves the bottom line up and fixes only what is marked.[^s1]
- **Clarity index.** Average sentence length plus the percentage of words of three or more syllables, with about 30 as the target, below 20 abrupt and above 40 hard to read.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The pamphlet turned "write clearly" into checks a busy manager can run on someone else's draft in minutes, and those checks still shape Army and civilian staff writing. The Command and General Staff College's 2009 student text restates the same standard and names BLUF and active voice as the two essentials, along with 15-word sentences and short paragraphs, and lists DA Pam 600-67 among its references.[^s2] TRADOC's action-officer course likewise teaches stating the purpose and main point up front, placing the recommendation, conclusion or reason for writing in the first or second paragraph, and cites the 1986 pamphlet.[^s4]

For a workplace reader the lesson is the mentoring method, not the military setting. A team lead reviewing a junior analyst's status memo can do a quick-screen edit: highlight the sentence that is the bottom line (often found in the last paragraph), circle passive verbs and jargon, and hand it back with one instruction - move the highlighted sentence to the top and fix only what is marked.[^s1] That gives specific, fast feedback instead of a rewrite, and the analyst learns the pattern.[^s1][^s4]

## Limits

This is an institutional guide, not research: the pamphlet cites no studies for its rules, and we found no evaluation of whether the Army Writing Program improved comprehension or decisions.[^s1][^s3] Its numeric targets are rules of thumb; the clarity index counts sentence and word length, not whether a reader understands the content. Writing about the program three decades later, an Army officer argued that good writing had faded from daily Army practice, displaced in part by slide briefings, and proposed new measures to revive it - a sign that issuing standards did not by itself sustain the habit.[^s3] Later Army guidance kept the core rules but moved them into AR 25-50, which TRADOC's course treats as binding standards rather than suggestions.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduces bottom line up front:** it is the earliest official instruction we found to put the "bottom line" first, though it credits business writers with the habit; later Army texts name it BLUF.[^s1][^s2]
- **Introduces the quick-screen edit and the clarity index,** the two mentoring tools leaders were to use on subordinates' drafts.[^s1]
- **Part of the Army Writing Program,** created by AR 600-70 in 1985; the pamphlet outlines that regulation's standards for leaders.[^s1][^s3]
- **Broader / related:** it is a writing style guide; its active-voice, short-sentence and main-point-first rules parallel plain-language guidance; the clarity index belongs to the family of readability formulas; and its packaging advice is aimed mainly at staff memorandums.[^s1][^s2][^s4]
