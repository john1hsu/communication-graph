# The Relative Effectiveness of Active Listening in Initial Interactions

This 2014 study by Harry Weger, Gina Castle Bell, Elizabeth Minei and Melissa Robinson is one of the few controlled experiments that pits active listening against other everyday ways of responding to someone. Strangers who were paraphrased and invited to say more felt better understood than strangers who got advice or bare acknowledgement, yet the listener was not rated as more likable or satisfying to talk to than one who offered advice.[^s1][^s2] For anyone trained to "always reflect back", it is a useful calibration: listening technique buys a specific benefit, not a general boost in how people see you.

## Origin

The authors, based at the University of Central Florida, West Chester University and Baruch College, noted that active listening is taught across social work, nursing, sales, leadership and crisis negotiation, but that very few experiments had compared it with alternative responses.[^s1] They trace the idea to Carl Rogers' 1951 account of empathic listening in therapy and credit Thomas Gordon with turning it into a teachable method.[^s1] The study extends the lead author's 2010 experiment, in which 180 students giving opinions in peer interviews received either a paraphrase or a simple acknowledgement; there, paraphrasing made the interviewer seem more socially attractive but did not make speakers feel more understood or more satisfied.[^s4][^s1] The 2014 version moved to peer-to-peer first conversations and added advice as a third condition.[^s1]

## What the study did and found

Participants were 115 undergraduates from an introductory communication course, randomly paired with one of ten trained confederates.[^s1][^s3] Each pair took turns on two light prompts, weekend plans and a disappointment with the university, for about five minutes.[^s1] Confederates either paraphrased and asked for elaboration when it fitted, offered suggestions, or mainly gave nods and short back-channel cues; nonverbal involvement was held moderate in every condition so that only the verbal response changed.[^s1]

Active listening raised feeling understood above both other conditions (eta squared about .09).[^s1] It also beat bare acknowledgement on conversational satisfaction and on how socially attractive the partner seemed, but it did not beat advice on either measure, and the authors describe these effects as small.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

At work, many first conversations - with a new report, a new client, a candidate - are short and low-stakes, like the ones in this study. The result suggests that paraphrasing and a follow-up question are a safe way to make the other person feel understood, which the authors call a low-risk strategy for initial interactions.[^s1] It also cautions against treating advice as automatically harmful: when it follows naturally from a problem the speaker raised, advice was liked as much as reflection, and the authors argue it can belong in a good listening response.[^s1]

A practical example: in a first one-to-one, a new analyst mentions that the reporting tool keeps timing out. Before, the manager jumps straight in with three workarounds. After, the manager says, "So the timeouts are costing you most of Monday mornings - what have you tried so far?", and offers one workaround only once the analyst has finished. The study's logic is that the reflection is what signals understanding, while the advice, once invited, does no harm to liking.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

The finding is real but modest, so it is rated mixed here. Effects were small, the precursor study found no understanding benefit from paraphrasing alone, and no direct replication of the 2014 design has been located.[^s1][^s4] The sample was mostly young, white, female students; partners were strangers who expected no further contact; the topics were deliberately mundane; and active-listening confederates received about four hours of training against one hour or less for the other conditions.[^s1][^s3] Nonverbal behaviour was not varied, and each participant had only one conversation.[^s3]

The broader literature points the same way with stronger, mostly correlational support. A 2024 meta-analysis of 664 effect sizes found that feeling listened to at work correlates with better relationships and performance, though about three-quarters of the data were self-reports and the performance link fell to about r = .21 without them.[^s5] Experiments by Itzchakov and colleagues found that attentive, non-judgemental listening lowered speakers' social anxiety and made their own views clearer to them.[^s6]

## Connections

The study grew out of **Weger, Castle and Emmett's 2010 paraphrasing experiment** (`cm:weger-castle-emmett-2010`), which it extends to peer conversations, and it is **influenced by Carl Rogers** (`cm:carl-rogers`) and **Thomas Gordon** (`cm:thomas-gordon`), to whom it traces active listening.[^s1] It deliberately **contrasts active listening with advice-giving** (`cm:advice-giving`) and with simple acknowledgement.[^s2] It sits under **active listening** and is closely related to **paraphrasing** and **open-ended questions**, the two behaviours its confederates used, and to the **Kluger et al. 2024 meta-analysis** (`cm:kluger-et-al-2024`) of perceived listening at work.[^s1][^s5]
