# Thomas Gilovich

Thomas (Tom) Gilovich is a social psychologist at Cornell University, where he is the Irene Blecker Rosenfeld Professor of Psychology and co-directs the Cornell Center for Behavioral Economics and Decision Research.[^s5] He studies everyday judgment: why people misread chance, regret what they did not do, and misjudge how they look to others.[^s4] For communicators, his lasting contribution is two named biases, the illusion of transparency and the spotlight effect, which explain why speakers under-explain their meaning and over-worry about their nerves and slip-ups.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Gilovich earned his PhD at Stanford in 1981, studying under the social psychologist Lee Ross, and joined Cornell's faculty the same year.[^s4] His early work on false beliefs, including the claim that basketball "hot hand" streaks are largely illusory, fed into his book *How We Know What Isn't So: The Fallibility of Human Reason in Everyday Life*.[^s4] He later worked with Dale Griffin and Daniel Kahneman on a 2002 volume on heuristics and biases in intuitive judgment and co-wrote a widely used social psychology textbook.[^s5] He was elected to the American Academy of Arts & Sciences in 2012.[^s6]

The communication work came out of a run of collaborations with his collaborators Kenneth Savitsky and Victoria Medvec. In 1998 the three published "The illusion of transparency" in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, the paper that named the tendency to overestimate how well others can read our inner states.[^s1][^s3] In 2000 Gilovich, Medvec and Savitsky followed with the spotlight effect paper, best known for a study in which students wearing a Barry Manilow T-shirt greatly overestimated how many people noticed it.[^s4] Gilovich says the spotlight research grew out of his studies of regret: many regrets about things people did not do trace back to fear of what others would think.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Both biases come from the same mechanism: people anchor on their own vivid experience, of a racing heart or an embarrassing shirt, and adjust too little for what an observer actually sees.[^s3] At work that produces two predictable failures. Speakers who assume their intent is obvious leave it unstated, and people who assume everyone noticed a stumble hold back in meetings or over-apologise.[^s1][^s4]

Gilovich and colleagues tested the consequences in settings practitioners care about. In a 2003 *Negotiation Journal* paper with Leaf Van Boven and Medvec, negotiators trying to hide their preferences thought they had given away more than they had, and experienced negotiators trying to signal some preferences overestimated how well the other side picked them up; a third study showed the result was not simply the curse of knowledge.[^s2] In a public-speaking study with Savitsky, speakers who were told about the illusion of transparency overestimated their visible nervousness less and were rated by observers as more confident.[^s3][^s5]

## Applying his work at work

Two habits follow directly from his findings. Before a negotiation or a sensitive email, say the thing you are assuming the other person already picked up, because they probably did not.[^s2] Before a presentation, remind yourself, and anxious colleagues, that the audience sees far less of your nerves than you feel; simply knowing about the illusion helped speakers in the original study.[^s3][^s5]

A workplace illustration: a project lead who thinks her frustration with a slipping deadline was obvious in last week's stand-up concludes the team is ignoring her. Gilovich's work suggests the more likely story is that nobody registered it. A single explicit sentence, naming the date at risk and what she needs, does more than another hint.[^s1][^s2]

## Limits

The original studies mostly used American university students, and Gilovich himself describes generalisation across cultures, ages and eras as an open question; cross-cultural spotlight studies have so far shown no clear difference.[^s5] A 2010 conceptual replication with live peer audiences confirmed the illusion of transparency in public speaking but found a pattern that anchoring and adjustment alone does not explain, which its authors linked to the widespread belief that everyone is nervous when speaking.[^s3] Gilovich says the core spotlight finding has held up over 25 years while some questions remain open.[^s5] Read his work as identifying reliable biases whose size varies by person and setting, not as fixed percentages.[^s3]

## Connections

Gilovich **introduced** the illusion of transparency (`cm:illusion-of-transparency`), named in his 1998 paper with Savitsky and Medvec.[^s1] He also **introduced** the spotlight effect (`cm:spotlight-effect`) in the 2000 paper with Medvec and Savitsky.[^s4] He **authored** *How We Know What Isn't So* (`cm:how-we-know-what-isnt-so`), his book on everyday reasoning errors.[^s4] He was **influenced by** his doctoral adviser Lee Ross (`cm:lee-ross`).[^s4] Victoria Medvec, his co-author on both founding papers, appears in the graph through her negotiation research, and his transparency findings bear directly on speaking anxiety and sit beside George Loewenstein's curse of knowledge as egocentric limits on perspective-taking.[^s2][^s3]
