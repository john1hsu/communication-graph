# John Whitmore

Sir John Whitmore (1937-2017) was a British coaching pioneer whose book *Coaching for Performance* introduced most managers in the English-speaking world to the GROW model and to the idea that a manager can coach rather than instruct.[^s1][^s4] He defined coaching as unlocking a person's potential to maximise their own performance, and gave it two aims: deepening the coachee's self-awareness and increasing their personal responsibility.[^s1] For anyone running a one-on-one today, his lasting contribution is a simple discipline: ask questions that help the other person see the situation clearly and own the next step, instead of supplying the answer.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Whitmore came to coaching from sport. He was a European champion racing driver and then spent about six years in business before visiting the Esalen Institute around 1969, where the human potential movement's focus on emotion and learning from experience made a strong impression on him.[^s2] Reading Timothy Gallwey's *The Inner Game of Tennis* (1972) was, in his own account, the moment he understood how learning works; he then set up the Inner Game organisation in the UK under licence from Gallwey, first as a tennis and ski school and later in business.[^s2] In 1992 he published *Coaching for Performance*, which has been translated into many languages and used as a textbook; a heavily revised fifth edition appeared in 2017, the year he died, with new material on building a coaching culture across an organisation.[^s2][^s3][^s4] He worked with large multinational companies on coaching cultures and leadership programmes and advised the Institute of Coaching.[^s4]

## Key ideas

**Awareness before instruction.** Whitmore described awareness as a magnifying glass that sharpens learning, and argued that teaching often disrupts learning because it tries to fix things from outside.[^s2] He distinguished this from traditional sport coaching, which he saw as rooted in behavioural correction of technique, whereas the newer coaching worked on the learner's internal obstacles.[^s2]

**Responsibility builds commitment.** Coaching, for Whitmore, aims to move people from dependence through independence to interdependence, so that the coachee chooses and owns the action.[^s1][^s2]

**A sequence managers can use.** The GROW model - Goal, Reality, Options, Will or Way forward - was devised by Graham Alexander and popularised by Whitmore; Passmore and colleagues describe it as the most widely recognised coaching approach in the English-speaking world and the dominant model in the UK and continental Europe.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Whitmore's framing changed what a manager does when a team member brings a problem. Picture a project lead asked by an analyst how to handle a client who keeps widening the scope. The instructing reply is to draft the email for her. The Whitmore-style reply asks what outcome she wants from the next client call, what has happened so far, what options she sees, and which one she will try by Friday - leaving her more aware of the pattern and responsible for the fix.[^s1][^s2] He also pushed coaching beyond one-to-one sessions: by 2009 he saw European organisations spreading coaching principles to managers who were never called coaches, and whole institutions in education and healthcare adopting the approach.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

Whitmore's influence rests on practice and publishing rather than research. Most empirical studies of workplace coaching date from 1995 onward, after his ideas had spread, and Passmore and colleagues trace the field through phases from case studies to randomised trials and meta-analyses.[^s1] GROW itself has seen variants such as TGROW, REGROW and GROWTH rather than direct tests of the original sequence.[^s1] Accounts also differ on its theoretical roots: Passmore and colleagues call GROW grounded in behavioural psychology, while Whitmore placed his coaching in the humanistic tradition and contrasted it with behavioural sport coaching.[^s1][^s2] The fifth edition's "bestselling" and "founding text" claims come from its publisher.[^s3] Treat Whitmore as the person who popularised manager coaching, not as evidence that it works.

## Connections

- **Introduced** (popularised) the **GROW model**, devised by Graham Alexander, through *Coaching for Performance*.[^s1]
- **Introduced** (popularised) **coaching** as a management skill, with his widely quoted definition and its twin goals of awareness and responsibility.[^s1]
- **Authored** ***Coaching for Performance*** (1992; fifth edition 2017).[^s3]
- **Influenced by** **Timothy Gallwey**, whose Inner Game approach he licensed and carried into business.[^s2]
- **Related:** **one-on-one meetings** and **open-ended questions**, the setting and the tool of his question-led, one-to-one approach; **solution-focused coaching**, another structured approach managers learn alongside GROW.[^s1][^s2]
