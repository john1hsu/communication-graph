# Checking email less frequently reduces stress

This 2015 paper in *Computers in Human Behavior* by Kostadin Kushlev and Elizabeth W. Dunn of the University of British Columbia is the experiment behind the common advice to "check email three times a day". It asked heavy email users to limit checking for one week and to check freely for another, and found that they felt less stressed in the limited week.[^s1] For anyone deciding how to handle their own inbox, or what response norms to set for a team, it is the clearest causal evidence that checking frequency itself, not only email volume, affects how stressful the day feels, while also showing how modest and conditional that effect is.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Kushlev, then a PhD candidate in UBC's psychology department, ran the study with Dunn, a well-being researcher in the same department; UBC announced the results in December 2014 when the paper appeared online.[^s4] The authors recruited people in Vancouver through posters, newspaper ads, listservs and word of mouth, looking for heavy email users who sometimes felt overwhelmed and had some freedom over when they checked. Of 142 who signed up, 124 completed surveys in both weeks; about two-thirds were students, and the rest worked in fields such as health care, finance, administration, IT and academia.[^s1]

## What the study did and found

The design was a counterbalanced within-person field experiment. In the limited week, participants were told to check email three times a day, keep the mail program closed otherwise and turn new-mail alerts off; in the unlimited week, they checked as often as they liked with the mailbox open and alerts on. A random number generator set the order, and a ten-minute survey arrived at 5 pm each weekday.[^s1]

The manipulation worked but was hard to follow: people checked about 4.7 times a day in the limited week versus about 12.5 in the unlimited week, so few kept to three. The number of emails received and answered did not differ between weeks, so the change was in how often people looked, not in how much mail they handled.[^s1] UBC's summary noted that most participants found it difficult to hold back from checking.[^s4]

Daily stress was lower in the limited week, a moderate effect (d = 0.37), as were tension during an important activity (d = 0.45) and feeling distracted by email (d = 0.51). Weekly stress ratings, self-rated productivity and most other well-being measures showed no direct difference.[^s1] Within-person analyses linked lower daily stress to better mood, mindfulness, sense of mastery and sleep, which the authors read as benefits flowing through stress, though those links are correlational.[^s1]

## Why it matters

A workplace illustration: a project coordinator keeps Outlook open with pop-up alerts and glances at every new message during planning work. The study suggests that closing the client and processing mail at a few set times, with the same overall volume, could make that work feel less tense, even if it does not by itself raise output.[^s1] Its authors stressed that individual willpower is not enough: because colleagues' expectations of fast replies were not controlled, they argued that team or organisation-wide norms about response times may be needed for larger gains.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The paper is rated mixed here. Its authors called it exploratory: many outcomes were tested, the stress effect was just significant (p = .04), checking was self-reported, there was no separate no-instruction control, the convenience sample had flexible schedules, and effects were measured over a single week. They also warned that roles needing constant updates might find limited checking more stressful, and that it is no cure-all for well-being.[^s1]

Later evidence points both ways. Mark and colleagues logged the email use of 40 information workers over 12 workdays with biosensors and found that batching was linked to higher rated productivity for long email days but found no evidence that it lowered measured stress.[^s2] A 2019 field experiment with 237 smartphone users, co-authored by Kushlev, found that batching phone notifications three times a day reduced stress and raised attentiveness and mood compared with normal alerts, while switching notifications off entirely increased anxiety and fear of missing out.[^s3] Together they suggest that fewer, predictable check-ins can help, but that the size of the benefit depends on the person, the job and how the change is made.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

The study was **introduced by Kostadin Kushlev** (`cm:kostadin-kushlev`) and **Elizabeth Dunn** (`cm:elizabeth-dunn`), and it was **applied to email** (`cm:email`), the channel whose checking pattern it manipulated.[^s1] It is **supported by Fitz and colleagues' 2019 notification-batching experiment** (`cm:fitz-et-al-2019`), which extended the three-times-a-day schedule to phone alerts,[^s3] and **challenged by Mark and colleagues' 2016 sensor study** (`cm:mark-et-al-2016`), which found no stress benefit from batching in natural use.[^s2] It sits under **scheduled inbox checks**, the technique it tests, and relates to **information overload**, **workplace stress**, **attention management** and **setting reply-time expectations**, the team norm its authors said would make individual limits easier to keep.[^s1][^s4]
