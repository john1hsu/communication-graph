# Michael Ross

Michael Ross is a social psychologist of the University of Waterloo in Ontario whose research explains two everyday communication failures: teammates who each believe they did most of the work, and status updates that promise dates the team will not hit.[^s1][^s2] With Fiore Sicoly he named egocentric bias in responsibility judgments, and with Roger Buehler and Dale Griffin he ran the studies that made the planning fallacy a mainstream research topic.[^s1][^s2] In 2016 the Society for Personality and Social Psychology gave him its Career Contribution Award, which recognises major theoretical or empirical contributions to the field.[^s4]

## Origin

Ross and Sicoly, both at Waterloo, started from a familiar workplace irritant: two collaborators who each feel they deserve first authorship, and each suspects the other of taking advantage.[^s1] They proposed that people generally accept more responsibility for a joint product than their partners attribute to them, and that the pattern is widespread but only becomes visible when people must divide credit explicitly.[^s1] Their 1979 paper, reprinted in the 1982 *Judgment under Uncertainty* collection, tested this across several settings.[^s1] In its best-known study, married couples estimated their own share of household activities such as preparing breakfast, shopping and making important decisions; the partners' estimates summed to more than 100%, so at least one of them had to be overestimating.[^s3] Ross and Sicoly's explanation was largely about memory and attention: our own contributions are easier to notice and recall than other people's, so they loom larger when we judge who did what.[^s3][^s1]

Fifteen years later Ross turned to a related problem in forecasting. Buehler, Griffin and Ross's five studies, published in 1994, asked why people underestimate how long their own tasks will take.[^s2] Honours students predicted their theses would take about 34 days; they took about 56, and only about 30% finished by their own best-guess date.[^s2] Think-aloud data showed why: roughly three-quarters of people's thoughts while estimating were about future plans, and only about 7% about past experience.[^s2] People also explained away their own past lateness as caused by outside, one-off events, which made that history feel irrelevant to the new estimate.[^s2]

## Key ideas

**Credit disputes are often honest.** Ross and Sicoly's account implies that colleagues who over-claim are usually not lying; they simply remember their own effort in vivid detail and other people's in outline.[^s1][^s3] Later work found that motivated self-enhancement can add to this, but that accessibility alone is enough to produce the bias.[^s3]

**The inside view misleads estimates.** People build a detailed story of how the work will go and neglect the record of how similar work actually went.[^s2] Simply recalling past projects did not help; asking people to connect past experience explicitly to the current task removed the optimistic bias in one study, though it did not make estimates accurate.[^s2]

**Others see what we miss.** Observers predicting someone else's completion time gave later, more realistic dates than the person doing the work and paid more attention to that person's track record and likely obstacles.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Both findings land in everyday team communication. In a retrospective, an engineering lead who says "I basically carried the migration" may sincerely believe it, while three colleagues privately feel the same about their part; Ross's work suggests the fix is to make everyone's contributions visible before anyone summarises credit.[^s1][^s3] Caruso, Epley and Bazerman built directly on that work: asking people to think about each collaborator's contribution in turn reduced over-claiming across four experiments.[^s3] They also found a cost: in cooperative groups, high claimers who did this enjoyed the collaboration less, so the technique works best paired with genuine recognition rather than as a way to deflate people.[^s3]

For estimates, the practical lesson is that a project manager asking "when will it be done?" gets a better answer by also asking "how long did the last three similar pieces of work take?", and by inviting a colleague outside the work to sanity-check the date.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The married-couple study is a single classic finding; the strongest support for Ross's idea is that later teams kept finding it in new settings, including journal author teams and group essay tasks.[^s3] The planning-fallacy studies used students and short, mostly academic tasks, and the intervention that removed optimism still left estimates inaccurate, so treat the work as a reason to bring evidence into estimates rather than a formula for correcting them.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced** egocentric bias, through the 1979 Ross and Sicoly research on responsibility for joint work.[^s1]
- **Influenced** the technique of considering others' contributions first, which Caruso, Epley and Bazerman developed from his over-claiming findings.[^s3]
- **Influenced** research on the planning fallacy through the 1994 Buehler, Griffin and Ross studies on task-completion estimates.[^s2]
- **Related:** Nicholas Epley, who extended this line of work to email and group credit; status reporting, where optimistic dates surface; and the curse of knowledge, another case of our own perspective shaping what we think others know.[^s3][^s2]
