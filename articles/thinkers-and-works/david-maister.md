# David Maister

David H. Maister (born 1947) is a British-born business school professor turned adviser who spent more than two decades counselling consulting, law, accounting and other professional firms.[^s1][^s2] For communicators his lasting contribution is a set of concrete, teachable habits for talking with clients: listen before you advise, explain what you will do and why, report progress before you are asked, and treat the client's experience of the work as part of the work.[^s3]

## Origin

Maister was born in London and studied mathematics, economics and statistics at the University of Birmingham and operations research at the London School of Economics, then earned a doctorate (D.B.A.) at Harvard Business School in 1976.[^s2] After teaching at the University of British Columbia, he was on the Harvard Business School faculty from 1979 to 1985, teaching the management of service businesses, before leaving to work as an independent adviser based in Boston.[^s1][^s2]

His academic work began in operations: books on trucking and airlines with his mentor Daryl Wyckoff, and in 1985 a chapter, "The Psychology of Waiting Lines", in the edited volume *The Service Encounter* (Lexington Books).[^s2][^s6][^s5] His books for practitioners followed, all from Free Press: *Managing the Professional Service Firm* (1993), *True Professionalism* (1997), *The Trusted Advisor* (2000, with Charles H. Green and Robert M. Galford), *Practice What You Preach* (2001) and *First Among Equals* (2002, with Patrick McKenna), followed by the self-published *Strategy and the Fat Smoker* (2008).[^s1][^s2]

## Key ideas

**How clients judge service.** Maister argued that clients often cannot evaluate expert work directly, so they judge the experience around it. His handouts list nine things that shape how much value a client feels: understanding and explanation, a sense of control, a sense of progress, access, responsiveness, reliability, appreciation, feeling important, and respect.[^s3]

**Keep the client informed without being asked.** His practical rules include telling the client in advance what will happen, sending progress summaries the client can forward internally without editing, confirming meeting outcomes the same or next day, raising scope changes promptly and getting approval, and anticipating questions rather than waiting for them.[^s3]

**Listen first, then frame.** Good listeners, in his account, probe, summarise, empathise and notice unspoken feelings, and they ask what the client has already considered before offering a view. The five-step trust process from *The Trusted Advisor* - engage, listen, frame, envision, commit - builds on that.[^s3]

**Explain in the client's language.** He advised plain language over jargon, explaining afterwards what was done and why, and making reports easy for the client to pass on to their own colleagues.[^s3]

**Explained waits feel shorter.** His 1985 waiting-lines chapter set out eight propositions about how people experience delays, including that unexplained and uncertain waits feel longer.[^s4][^s6] A workplace translation: when a deliverable slips, telling the client why and giving a firm new date protects the relationship far more than silence does.

## Evidence and influence

Maister's client advice is practitioner guidance distilled from consulting work, not a body of tested interventions, and his wider philosophy linked satisfied staff to better service, satisfied clients and profit.[^s2][^s3] His waiting-lines propositions have drawn more formal testing. A 1996 UK study of 300 people queuing at a food outlet examined two of the propositions, confirmed that perceived and actual waits differ, and proposed revisions to the list.[^s4] A 2007 video experiment noted that his principles are widely cited but seldom tested, and found that giving people an explanation for a wait significantly changed how long it seemed, whether the explanation blamed the customer or the provider.[^s5] In 2002 he was named one of the world's top 40 business thinkers in a Financial Times/Prentice Hall survey.[^s1]

## Connections

Maister is linked to client communication as a populariser rather than a coiner: he did not invent the idea of communicating with clients, but his handouts and books made its everyday habits - informing, explaining, listening and expectation-setting - explicit for professional firms, so this attribution carries low confidence.[^s3] He authored *The Trusted Advisor*, the book behind the trusted advisor framework and its trust-building steps, with Green and Galford.[^s1] He authored *Managing the Professional Service Firm* (1993), his first major book for practitioners.[^s2] He authored "The Psychology of Waiting Lines" (1985), whose propositions about explained and uncertain waits later researchers put to the test.[^s5][^s4] His advice relates to status reporting, active listening, plain language, credibility and delivering bad news.[^s3]
