# The illusion of transparency: Biased assessments of others' ability to read one's emotional states

This 1998 *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* paper by Thomas Gilovich, Kenneth Savitsky and Victoria Husted Medvec is the original evidence for the illusion of transparency: the tendency to think our nerves, feelings and intentions show more plainly than they do.[^s2] Across three sets of experiments, people who lied, hid disgust or felt alarm consistently guessed that observers could read them better than observers actually could.[^s1] For anyone who presents, negotiates or gives hard news at work, its practical lesson cuts two ways: your nerves are less visible than you fear, and your unspoken concerns are less visible than you assume.

## Origin

The authors framed it as a problem of perspective-taking: we know our own inner state vividly, start from it when imagining how we look, and adjust too little toward an outsider's view.[^s2] They connected the work to the "curse of knowledge", arguing that the illusion is a stronger, experience-driven form of the same egocentric pull.[^s1]

## What the study did and found

**Lying (Studies 1a-1c).** In a round-robin game, small groups of undergraduates answered personal questions, each person lying once while the others tried to spot the lie. In Study 1a (39 students), liars predicted that about 49% of the group would catch them; only about 26% did, roughly chance.[^s1] Study 1b repeated the result (50% predicted, 27% actual).[^s1] Study 1c added silent "yoked" observers who knew the same facts as each liar but did not tell the lie themselves; those observers predicted much lower detection (about 25%) than the liars did (about 44%), which suggests the bias comes from experiencing the lie, not from a general belief that lies are easy to spot.[^s1] Truth-tellers showed no illusion.[^s1]

**Disgust (Studies 2a-2b).** Participants tasted 15 drinks, five of them a foul vinegar mixture, while trying to keep a straight face; observers later watched the video. Tasters expected about 4.9 of 10 observers to pick out the bad drinks, but the actual average was about 3.6, close to chance.[^s1] There was no overestimate for pleasant drinks, so the effect appeared only when the hidden state was intense.[^s1]

**Bystanders (Studies 3a-3b).** In groups solving anagrams, a confederate openly cheated. Participants felt more concerned than they appeared and believed their concern was more visible than others rated it.[^s1] The authors proposed that if every bystander thinks their own alarm is showing and sees everyone else looking calm, each concludes the others are unworried, one route to the well-known failure to intervene.[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

The paper gives a mechanism for two everyday communication failures. Speakers over-manage nerves no one can see, and colleagues under-say what they want, need or object to because it feels obvious. Van Boven, Gilovich and Medvec carried the idea into bargaining in 2003: negotiators trying to hide their preferences overestimated how much leaked out, and experienced negotiators trying to signal some preferences overestimated how well their counterparts picked them up.[^s3]

A workplace illustration: in a vendor call, a project lead is privately alarmed by a proposed delivery date and assumes her frown has made that clear, so she says nothing and the date is agreed. A version informed by the study would put the concern in words ("That date doesn't work for our launch; can we look at the 20th?") and ask the vendor to restate the agreed date before the call ends.[^s3][^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The direction of the effect has held in later work, which is why it is rated `mixed` rather than `robust`. The negotiation studies replicated it with different tasks and ruled out a pure curse-of-knowledge account.[^s3] Rai, Mitchell and Faelling's 2012 experiments with lies about personal memories again found that speakers overestimated how many listeners would spot their lies, though listeners did show some genuine ability to tell lies from truths.[^s4] A small 2009 study with 25 children found the pattern only for pleasant drinks, not unpleasant ones, a reminder that the effect depends on age, display rules and the task.[^s5]

The original studies have clear limits. Samples were small groups of college students, and several tests rested on 8 to 20 group-level data points.[^s1] The authors acknowledged that they had not directly tested anchoring and adjustment; their support for it was indirect (for instance, transparency estimates correlated with private self-consciousness, r = .41), and they suggested manipulations such as delay or cognitive load for future work.[^s1] No large multi-lab replication of the 1998 paradigms was found for this article, so precise effect sizes should be treated cautiously.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

The study was **introduced by Thomas Gilovich** (`cm:thomas-gilovich`), **Kenneth Savitsky** (`cm:kenneth-savitsky`) and **Victoria Medvec** (`cm:victoria-medvec`), and it named the barrier it sits under, the **illusion of transparency**.[^s1] The authors **explained** the effect by **anchoring and adjustment** (`cm:anchoring-and-adjustment`) from one's own feelings, while noting the explanation was untested.[^s1] It is **supported by Van Boven, Gilovich and Medvec's 2003 negotiation studies** (`cm:van-boven-gilovich-medvec-2003`)[^s3] and by **Rai, Mitchell and Faelling's 2012 lie experiments** (`cm:rai-mitchell-faelling-2012`).[^s4] It is related to the **curse of knowledge** and the broader **egocentric bias**, to the authors' later **spotlight effect** paper, and to work on **speaking anxiety**, including Savitsky and Gilovich's 2003 follow-up and MacInnis, Mackinnon and MacIntyre's 2010 classroom replication, both of which built on these experiments.[^s1]
