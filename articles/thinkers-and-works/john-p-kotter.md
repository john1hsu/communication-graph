# John P. Kotter

John P. Kotter is a leadership and change scholar at Harvard Business School, where he is the Konosuke Matsushita Professor of Leadership, Emeritus.[^s1] For communicators he matters for two pieces of writing: a 1995 *Harvard Business Review* article that named under-communication as one of the main reasons organisational transformations fail, and a 1980 article, written with John Gabarro, that framed the relationship with one's boss as something to be deliberately managed through information, expectations and trust.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Kotter studied at MIT and Harvard and joined the Harvard Business School faculty in 1972; he received tenure and a full professorship in 1980 at the age of 33, one of the youngest people in the university's history to do so.[^s1] His research centres on leadership, on how organisations carry out large transformations, and on winning and keeping support for good ideas.[^s1] He has written 18 books, has received the 2012 McKinsey Award for the most significant *HBR* article of that year, and co-founded the advisory firm Kotter International.[^s1]

The change article drew on more than 100 companies he had watched over the previous decade and described eight errors that derail transformation, from failing to create urgency to failing to anchor new behaviours in the culture.[^s2] He expanded it into the 1996 book *Leading Change*, which set out his eight-step change framework and became an international bestseller.[^s5] He later wrote *A Sense of Urgency*, arguing that raising urgency is the hardest of the eight steps.[^s5]

## Why it matters

Kotter's fourth error, under-communicating the vision by a factor of ten, is the reason many change plans now include a communication plan at all.[^s2] He described three patterns: a single meeting or memo that uses a vanishing share of the year's internal communication; a leader who gives many speeches that still reach almost nobody's daily work; and messages that grow louder while senior people act against them, which breeds cynicism.[^s2] His fixes are practical: use every channel, especially ones wasted on routine news, tie performance reviews, Q&A sessions and operating reviews back to the change, and remember that leaders' deeds communicate more than their words.[^s2]

In a workplace, that means a director announcing a move to a new ordering system does not stop at the all-hands. She has team leads explain what changes for each team in their weekly meetings, puts progress and early wins in the monthly review, answers the same questions again on the intranet, and makes sure her own team is the first to stop using the old spreadsheet.[^s2]

The earlier article with Gabarro is the source of much "managing up" advice. It argues that boss and subordinate depend on each other, that the subordinate should study the boss's goals, pressures and work style as well as their own habits towards authority, and that the relationship rests on agreed expectations, a good flow of information including bad news, dependability and honesty, and selective use of the boss's time.[^s3] It also offers a channel tip still useful today: brief a boss who prefers listening in person and follow up in writing, and send a boss who prefers reading a memo first, then discuss it.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

Kotter's claims come from consulting and case observation, not controlled studies; the factor-of-ten figure is an estimate, not a measurement.[^s2] A 2012 review of about fifteen years of change-management research by Appelbaum and colleagues found support in the literature for most individual steps of the eight-step model, but no formal study of the model as a whole; it concluded that the model's popularity owes more to its practical, easy-to-use format than to evidence of results, while finding no evidence against it.[^s4] The managing-your-boss guidance is likewise practitioner wisdom drawn from case examples.[^s3] Readers should treat both as well-tested checklists rather than proven recipes, and pair them with field research such as realistic merger previews when deciding how to communicate change.[^s4]

## Connections

Kotter **introduced** communicating organisational change as a central leadership task in the practitioner literature, by naming under-communication of the vision as a leading cause of failed transformations.[^s2] He **authored** *Leading Change* (1996), which turned the article's eight errors into an eight-step process,[^s5] and, with John Gabarro, **authored** "Managing Your Boss" (1980), the root of the managing-up idea.[^s3] He is related to organisational communication, the wider field his change work feeds; to the realistic merger preview, field evidence that candid, early information helps during change; and to delivering bad news, since both his articles stress that leaders and subordinates must share unwelcome facts.[^s2][^s3][^s4]
