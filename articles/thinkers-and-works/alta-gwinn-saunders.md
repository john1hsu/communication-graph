# Alta Gwinn Saunders

Alta Gwinn Saunders (1886-1948) taught business English at the University of Illinois for three decades and is remembered as one of the founders of business communication as an academic field.[^s1][^s2] Her textbook *Effective Business English* treated written English as a working tool for getting business done, and her organising work in the 1930s gave teachers of business writing their own professional association.[^s1][^s3] Many habits that workplace writers now take for granted, such as writing to a specific reader, keeping it clear and short, and choosing a courteous, positive tone, were already at the centre of her teaching.[^s1]

## Origin

Saunders earned bachelor's (1907) and master's (1910) degrees in English at Illinois.[^s1] Before returning to teach, she had practical business experience: she opened and ran a tea room and co-managed a department store.[^s1] She rejoined the university in 1918 as Instructor of Business English, became associate professor in 1925 and full professor in 1938, a rare rank for a woman at the time.[^s1][^s2]

*Effective Business English*, first published in 1925 and revised through the 1930s (second edition 1939), taught letters and reports through worked examples.[^s1][^s5] She also wrote *Business Reports: Investigation and Presentation* (1929), a guide to job-application letters, and later books on business literature and conversation.[^s1][^s2][^s5]

In December 1935, her Illinois colleague C. R. Anderson concluded that business-writing teachers needed an association of their own, because existing bodies focused on literature, advertising or marketing.[^s3] Saunders was one of the six organisers, with Anderson and colleagues from NYU, Columbia, Wisconsin and Northwestern.[^s3] The group recruited its first members in 1936 and adopted the name American Business Writing Association in 1937. It became the American Business Communication Association in 1967 and the Association for Business Communication in 1985.[^s3] Saunders served as its president in 1941.[^s3] She died in June 1948 in a plane crash while travelling to meet her publisher about a new printing of her textbook.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Saunders helped turn business writing from a clerical skill into a taught, reader-centred discipline.[^s1][^s3] The archive's summary of her principles reads like a modern checklist:[^s1]

- **Understand the reader.** She grounded sympathetic writing in an understanding of human nature, so the writer talks to the reader rather than down to them or at them.[^s1]
- **Learn from good models.** Pick up the custom and manner of business letters by studying good ones.[^s1]
- **Be clear and concise.**[^s1]
- **Use positive suggestion** rather than negative phrasing.[^s1]
- **Watch your register.** Be careful about slang and its overuse.[^s1]
- **Be courteous** throughout.[^s1]

A present-day illustration: a team lead drafting a reply to a client complaint can apply her approach directly. Instead of opening with company policy, she names the client's problem, says what will happen next in plain words, and frames the remedy positively. The point is to write to that reader's concerns, not at them.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Saunders is a historical figure, so her influence rests on archival and institutional records, not on experiments. Two of the three main sources come from her own university and her own professional association, which are well placed but not neutral.[^s1][^s2][^s3] Some of her sample letters, such as a personalised direct-mail sales letter for hats, now read as dated.[^s1]

Historians of the field also warn against "pioneer" narratives. Brooks's 1991 history of business-communication curricula argues that earlier accounts mostly celebrated the founders of a *craftsmanship* approach.[^s4] That approach centred on the well-made sales letter, the AIDA formula and the "you attitude".[^s4] From the 1960s, a managerial, problem-solving approach rooted in case teaching displaced it, treating writing as part of ongoing relationships and decisions rather than one-off persuasion.[^s4] Saunders's emphasis on letters and on the reader fits the earlier tradition. Her work is a foundation for modern business writing, not the whole of it.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

Saunders **introduced** (helped found and popularise) **business writing** as a university subject, through her teaching and the association she co-organised.[^s1][^s3] She **authored** *Effective Business English* and *Business Reports: Investigation and Presentation*.[^s1][^s2] Her advice anticipates **audience analysis** and **plain language**, and her report textbook relates to the **business report** as a genre within **written** and **organisational communication**.[^s1][^s2]
