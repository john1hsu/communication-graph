# Erving Goffman

Erving Goffman (1922-1982) was a Canadian-born American sociologist who studied what happens when people are in each other's presence: how they greet, defer, save each other's embarrassment, take turns and present themselves.[^s2] He argued that face-to-face interaction is an orderly domain of social life with its own rules, which he called the interaction order, and that it deserves study in its own right rather than as a by-product of larger social structures.[^s1][^s6] Much of today's vocabulary for the social side of workplace talk - face, face-saving, impression management, front stage and backstage - traces back to him.[^s4][^s5]

## Origin

Goffman was born in Manville, Alberta, in 1922, took his first degree at the University of Toronto and his master's and doctorate at the University of Chicago, where he trained in sociology and social anthropology.[^s2][^s3] He taught at the University of California, Berkeley from 1958 and moved in 1968 to the University of Pennsylvania as Benjamin Franklin Professor of Anthropology and Sociology.[^s3] His essay "On Face-Work" (1955) defined face as the self-image a person claims in an encounter, expressed in terms of socially approved attributes, and face-work as what people do to keep their conduct consistent with it.[^s4] *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (first published in 1956, expanded in 1959) compared everyday impression management to a performance with front and back regions.[^s4][^s3] Later books included *Asylums*, *Stigma*, *Interaction Ritual*, *Frame Analysis* and *Forms of Talk* (1981), the last applying his approach to conversation itself.[^s2][^s3][^s6]

He served as president of the American Sociological Association in 1981-82 and died in November 1982; his presidential address was published after his death.[^s3][^s2][^s6] "The Interaction Order," published in 1983, sums up his case: face-to-face interaction arises whenever people are physically together, cannot be reduced to group behaviour or individual psychology, and should be treated as a substantive field.[^s1][^s6]

## Why it matters

Goffman gives workplace communicators a lens for the part of a message that is not about information. Every request, correction or disagreement also makes a claim about the people involved, and listeners react to that claim as much as to the content.[^s4][^s5] Three of his ideas translate directly into practice:

- **Face.** Each person in an encounter has a public self-image they expect others to respect, and much polite behaviour is mutual help in keeping it intact.[^s4][^s5] A team lead who corrects an analyst's numbers in front of a client threatens that face; the same correction in a quick private word, framed around the data, usually lands without a fight. Later linguists, notably Brown and Levinson, combined this sociological idea of face with speech-act theory to describe face-threatening acts such as requests and criticism.[^s5]
- **Front and back regions.** People act differently on the "stage" where an audience watches than in the backstage where they relax and prepare.[^s4] This helps explain why a project's real status is often discussed in the corridor after the steering meeting rather than in it, and why leaders who want candour need settings that feel less like a performance.
- **The encounter as an order.** Because face-to-face talk runs on shared, largely unspoken rules about attention, turns and deference, small breaches - checking a phone while someone speaks, talking over a junior colleague - are read as messages about status and respect.[^s6][^s1]

## Ideas you can use at work

Use his ideas as prompts for planning a conversation. Before giving feedback or saying no, ask whose face the message threatens and how the person could accept it without looking foolish in front of others.[^s4][^s5] Choose the setting with the audience in mind: sensitive corrections belong in a backstage setting, not in front of the people whose opinion the listener cares about most.[^s4] In meetings, watch for the "ritual" work that keeps an encounter going - acknowledging contributions, offering someone a graceful way to change position - because removing it makes ordinary disagreement feel personal.[^s6]

## Limits

Goffman's method was observational and interpretive rather than experimental, so his ideas are frameworks for noticing, not tested causal claims; the article is marked `historical` for that reason. Scholars note that treating interaction mainly as ritual and self-presentation is a narrow lens, because much workplace interaction is also instrumental, aimed at getting a task done.[^s6] In pragmatics, researchers have questioned the tight link that later theories drew between face and politeness, and some argue for returning to Goffman's broader, situational notion of face rather than the fixed universal categories built on it.[^s5] Cultural norms for what threatens face vary, so the concept travels better than any particular rule of thumb drawn from it.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced** the study of **face-to-face conversation** as a domain of its own, which he named the interaction order and defended in his final address.[^s1][^s6]
- **Introduced** **face and face-work**, defined in his 1955 essay, and a dramaturgical account of **impression management** in *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*.[^s4]
- **Authored** *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* and *Interaction Ritual*, among other books.[^s2][^s6]
- **Related** to **politeness theory**, whose face-threatening acts draw on the notion of face he introduced, and to the **apology** and *Mea Culpa*, a study of apology that engages with his work.[^s5]
- **Related** to **interpersonal communication**, **conversational turn-taking** and **small talk**, the everyday encounters his work describes, and to **feedback**, where face concerns shape how criticism is heard.[^s4][^s6]
