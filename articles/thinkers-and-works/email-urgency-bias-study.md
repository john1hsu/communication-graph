# You don't need to answer right away! (Giurge & Bohns, 2021)

This 2021 paper by Laura M. Giurge of London Business School and Vanessa K. Bohns of Cornell's ILR School named the "email urgency bias": people who receive a non-urgent work email, particularly in the evening or at the weekend, assume the sender wants an answer faster than the sender really does.[^s1][^s3] Across eight preregistered experiments with 4,004 participants, that misreading was tied to more stress for receivers and, through stress, to lower well-being; a short note from the sender stating the timeline shrank the gap.[^s1] It is the main evidence behind the advice to say plainly when a reply is, and is not, needed.[^s3]

## Origin

Bohns and Giurge framed the question with two older ideas. Boundary theory describes how people manage the line between work and home, and the authors cast well-meaning colleagues as unintentional intruders on that line when they email during someone's off-hours.[^s2] Research on egocentrism, including earlier experiments showing that email writers overrate how clearly their intent comes across, suggested that senders and receivers would each judge the message from their own vantage point.[^s2] The authors also argued that a norm of the always-available ideal worker makes fast replies feel like proof of commitment.[^s2] Cornell announced the findings in September 2021, ahead of publication in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*.[^s3]

## What the studies did and found

Most studies randomly assigned working adults, many of them full-time US employees recruited online, to imagine or recall being either the sender or the receiver of the same non-urgent work email; one drew on public-sector employees in Spain during remote working in late 2020.[^s2] Receivers consistently expected the sender to want a reply sooner, with gaps of roughly a third to two-thirds of a standard deviation.[^s2] A neutral observer condition fell between the two sides, so both erred, but receivers erred more.[^s2] Marking an email urgent raised everyone's expected speed without removing the perspective gap, and the gap was largest when the message arrived outside working hours.[^s2] Receivers' higher expectations went with higher perceived stress, and Study 1 linked that stress to more burnout and poorer work-life balance.[^s2]

In Study 6, with 852 analysed participants, senders' emails either did or did not end with a sentence saying the matter was not urgent and could be handled whenever convenient. Without the note, receivers of off-hours email still overestimated the expected speed; with it, the gap was no longer significant, and the stress pathway through expectations faded too.[^s2] The note added no new facts, since every version already described the email as non-urgent; what changed was that the sender said so.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Picture a team lead who drafts tomorrow's task list on Sunday night and emails it so she will not forget. The study suggests her reports will likely read the timing as a signal to act tonight, even though she expects nothing until Monday, and that adding "no need to look at this before Monday" removes most of that pressure.[^s2][^s3] The authors placed the fix with senders: state the expected reply time, especially off-hours.[^s3] A later LSE summary of Giurge's work adds advice for receivers (ask when urgency is unclear) and for organisations (agree which channel carries urgent requests), and warns that email footers or scheduled sending help little without explicit expectations.[^s5]

The cost of unstated expectations is not trivial. In three studies, including one of 138 employee-partner pairs, Becker and colleagues found that an organisation's expectation that staff monitor email after hours was linked to anxiety, poorer health and relationship strain, spilling over to partners.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

Rated mixed. The perspective gap replicated across all eight studies, which were preregistered with open data, but every study came from the same two authors, and no independent replication has been found.[^s1][^s2] Most used recalled or imagined emails rather than real inboxes, there was no longitudinal follow-up, and the authors flagged untested moderators such as power differences, time zones and chat tools.[^s2] The key Study 6 interaction was only marginal before the sample was split, so the sender-note remedy is less certain than the bias itself.[^s2] Becker's correlational field work fits the stress mechanism but tested organisational norms, not individual emails.[^s4]
