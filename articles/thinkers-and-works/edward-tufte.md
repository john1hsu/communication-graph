# Edward R. Tufte

Edward R. Tufte is a statistician and political scientist, now professor emeritus of political science, computer science and statistics at Yale and a senior critic at the Yale School of Art, whose research concerns statistical evidence and scientific visualisation.[^s1] For workplace communicators he matters for two ideas: that charts, tables and reports should put the evidence itself in front of the reader with as little decoration as possible, and that slide decks built from bullet outlines are a poor vehicle for serious reasoning.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Tufte earned a BS and MA in statistics at Stanford and a PhD in political science at Yale, and taught at Princeton before returning to Yale, for 32 years of teaching in all.[^s2] His early books were in political science and statistics: *Size and Democracy* (1973, with Robert A. Dahl), *Data Analysis for Politics and Policy* (1974) and *Political Control of the Economy* (1980).[^s2][^s1] He then founded his own publisher, Graphics Press, which issued *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983), *Envisioning Information* (1990) and *Visual Explanations* (1997).[^s2] He has consulted for the U.S. Census Bureau, the CDC, the National Science Foundation and NASA, and is a fellow of the American Statistical Association, the American Academy of Arts and Sciences and the Society for Technical Communication.[^s2][^s1]

In May 2003 he published the essay *The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint*, revised to 28 pages that September and expanded into a 32-page second edition in April 2006 that also became a chapter of his book *Beautiful Evidence*.[^s3] His analysis of a Boeing briefing slide about the damaged space shuttle Columbia was first posted on his website so that it could feed into the accident board's work, and the essay's later printings include the board's findings.[^s3]

## Key ideas

**Show the data, cut the decoration.** In the 1983 book Tufte defined the data-ink ratio - the share of a graphic's ink that actually presents data - and argued that designers should raise it by removing non-data ink.[^s5][^s6] He coined "chartjunk" for decorative elements that tell the viewer nothing new.[^s6]

**Slides fragment reasoning.** His PowerPoint essay argues that the slide format, and above all its default bullet hierarchy, chops analysis into fragments; by his counts, slide-style pages carry roughly 2-10% of the typographic information of a non-fiction book page.[^s3] In a 2004 interview he framed this as an ethical issue as well as a design one, arguing that the software's cognitive style favours pitching and marketing over truth-seeking, something he did not say of Word or Excel.[^s2]

**Write the report.** His remedy for complex technical or analytical material is a short, dense written report or handout people can study at their own pace, with the live talk used to discuss it.[^s3][^s4]

## What to take from him

- When the decision rests on numbers, send a one- or two-page written analysis before the meeting rather than spreading it across slides.[^s3][^s4]
- Before sharing a chart, delete gridlines, 3-D effects, logos and colour that carry no data, then check whether the point is now easier to see.[^s5][^s6]
- Put the most important finding in a full sentence, not a sub-bullet, so that it keeps its weight as it is passed up the hierarchy.[^s3]

## Evidence and influence

Tufte's prescriptions are expert design argument rather than tested interventions, and parts of them have been challenged. Technical-communication scholar David Farkas (2006) called the PowerPoint essay seriously flawed though raising important issues: it judges slides as free-standing documents rather than aids to a live speaker, treats the sensible use of handouts as proof that slides fail, and is too deterministic about the tool, since the NASA case points largely to organisational culture.[^s4] On graphics, a 2007 study in which 87 students compared a standard bar graph with Tufte's minimalist version found a clear preference for the conventional design, though the authors note unfamiliarity may explain part of it.[^s5] A 2020 interview study of 20 visualisation practitioners, summarising later experiments in which embellished charts sometimes aided recall and engagement, found the field moving away from strict minimalism towards judging decoration by audience, medium and purpose.[^s6][^s5] His core advice - put the evidence and the conclusion in plain view - remains widely taught.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

Tufte is linked to death by PowerPoint as its most influential populariser rather than the coiner of the phrase: his 2003 essay and Columbia analysis gave the complaint its best-known argument, so the attribution carries medium confidence.[^s3] He authored *The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint* (2003; second edition 2006) and *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983).[^s3][^s2] He introduced the data-ink ratio, the principle that graphics should devote their ink to data.[^s5] His PowerPoint critique is challenged by Farkas (2006), who places more of the blame on users and organisations than on the software.[^s4] He relates to assertion-evidence slides, presentations, business reports, executive briefings and information overload, the formats and problems his advice targets.[^s3][^s4]
