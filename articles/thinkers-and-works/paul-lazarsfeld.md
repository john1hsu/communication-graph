# Paul Lazarsfeld

Paul Felix Lazarsfeld (1901-1976) was an Austrian-born sociologist who did much to create modern survey research and the empirical study of mass communication.[^s1] He took a doctorate in applied mathematics in Vienna in 1925, moved into applied psychology, and emigrated to the United States in 1933 on a Rockefeller Foundation fellowship.[^s1] He then spent three decades at Columbia University, where he taught until 1970.[^s1] For anyone who asks questions at work, from a customer interview to a staff survey, he is the figure behind a practical tension: tightly worded closed questions are fast and countable, but open ones show why people answer as they do.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Lazarsfeld founded a series of applied research institutes, starting in Vienna in the 1920s. With Marie Jahoda and Hans Zeisel he wrote the 1933 Marienthal study of an unemployed community.[^s3] In 1937 he became director of the Rockefeller-funded Office of Radio Research at Princeton. The project moved to Columbia in 1940 and became the Bureau of Applied Social Research, which he led through the 1940s.[^s1][^s3] There he turned the commercial opinion poll into survey research that could measure what radio and print did to attitudes.[^s3] He later served as president of both the American Association for Public Opinion Research and the American Sociological Association.[^s3]

## Key ideas

**Open versus closed questions.** In the 1940s, survey researchers split into two camps. Commercial and national pollsters wanted fixed-choice questions they could field and tabulate quickly. Interview-minded researchers argued that only open questions revealed what respondents meant.[^s4][^s2] Foddy's textbook history lists Lazarsfeld, with George Gallup, among those whose large surveys made the closed format standard.[^s4] Yet in a 1944 article Lazarsfeld also defended open interviews, because they let analysts work inductively and see the motives behind an answer. He proposed a compromise: put open questions at the start and end of an interview, and use them to probe deviant cases.[^s2] His own 1940 election study, *The People's Choice*, paired detailed closed questions with open ones about how voters reached their decisions.[^s2][^s1]

**The two-step flow.** *The People's Choice* (1944) reported that media influence often did not reach voters directly. It passed first to attentive opinion leaders, who relayed it through personal conversation.[^s3] Elihu Katz, a former member of the Bureau, reviewed the follow-up studies in 1957. He concluded that they largely corroborated the hypothesis but refined it considerably.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Lazarsfeld's compromise is still good practice for workplace questioning. Closed questions confirm and count. Open ones explain the numbers and catch answers your options did not anticipate.[^s2][^s5] Later research backs his caution. Krosnick and Presser's 2010 review reports that listed options steer answers, that adding an "other" box rarely fixes this, and that open questions are usually better for asking about quantities. Closed questions still dominate because coding open answers costs time and money.[^s5] The two-step flow carries a related lesson for change communication. A message often lands through trusted colleagues who talk about it, not through the broadcast alone.[^s3][^s6]

## Evidence and limits

Lazarsfeld's arguments for open interviewing largely lost the practical battle. Berg and Ternullo note that cheaper fixed-choice polling won out, and that open-ended questions were later dropped from major election studies.[^s2] His role in the debate is also read in different ways. Some histories place him in the closed-question camp, while others stress his defence of open interviews.[^s4][^s2] The two-step flow came from 1940s election studies and was revised as researchers measured personal influence more directly, so it describes a tendency rather than a rule.[^s6]

## Connections

Foddy's history credits Lazarsfeld with helping make **closed questions** the default format of large surveys. He also argued for the **open questions** that should accompany them, a mix that companion techniques such as **follow-up questions** still serve.[^s4][^s2] He and his Columbia colleagues introduced the **two-step flow of communication**,[^s3] which was first reported in **The People's Choice**.[^s1][^s3] His survey studies of how media shift attitudes in the 1940s ran alongside the experimental persuasion research of **Carl Hovland** at Yale.[^s1] His radio research helped found mass communication research within **communication studies**.[^s3]
