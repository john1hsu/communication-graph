# The Physician as Teacher

*The Physician as Teacher* is a compact handbook on clinical teaching by Thomas L. Schwenk and Neal A. Whitman, first published by Williams & Wilkins in Baltimore in 1987 as a 203-page paperback with a bibliography and index.[^s4] A second edition, credited to Whitman and Schwenk, was published by Whitman Associates in Salt Lake City in 1997.[^s2] Its lasting footprint in workplace communication is indirect: it is the reference that later feedback guidance cites for the idea of "sandwiching" criticism between praise.[^s1]

## Origin

Schwenk, then an associate professor of family practice at the University of Michigan Medical School, wrote it with Whitman to help doctors who teach students and residents. In a University of Michigan teaching paper, Schwenk frames clinical teaching as an interaction shaped by the teacher's role, the learner's role, outside conditions and how they interact, and points readers to the book for a fuller treatment.[^s3] A 2008 review of the second edition describes two parts: the first treats teaching as communication and sets out three teacher styles (attentive silence, cooperative negotiation and persuasive confrontation), drawing on lessons from employer-employee and parent-child relationships; the second covers methods such as lectures, group discussion, rounds and bedside teaching.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The book matters to communicators for where one of its terms went. In 2002, Anne Dohrenwend published "Serving Up the Feedback Sandwich" for family physicians who supervise staff, describing one specific criticism placed between two specific praises - and attaching her first citation, for the word "sandwiching", to Schwenk and Whitman's 1987 book.[^s1] Her article does not say the authors invented the practice, so the book is best read as the earliest documented source this graph has for the name, not proof of who first used the technique.[^s1]

The book's wider message about feedback is stricter than the popular sandwich. Schwenk's teaching paper describes effective feedback as descriptive rather than judgmental, specific and well timed, and aimed at improvement rather than at compliments or criticism for their own sake.[^s3] Dohrenwend's version keeps that spirit: both praises must be sincere and specific, the criticism must name one behaviour with an alternative, and the learner should get a chance to respond.[^s1]

## Reading it today

For a manager or mentor, the useful takeaway is the book's framing rather than any single formula: teaching someone at work is a conversation whose style should fit the learner and the moment, ranging from listening quietly to negotiating a plan to confronting a problem directly.[^s2][^s3] The 2008 reviewer rated it very good for beginners - concise, readable, with chapter summaries and many examples - but noted thin coverage in places and some repetition, so experienced teachers may want more advanced texts.[^s2]

## Connections

- **Feedback sandwich** - the book is the source Dohrenwend cites for the term "sandwiching", recorded here as `introduces` with medium confidence because she credits the name to it without calling it the inventor.[^s1]
- **Authors** - written by Thomas L. Schwenk and Neal A. Whitman (Williams & Wilkins, 1987).[^s4]
- **Feedback** - its advice that feedback be descriptive, specific, timely and improvement-focused places it under the broader feedback concept.[^s3]
- **Active listening** and **praise and recognition** - its "attentive silence" style and the sincere, specific praise in the sandwich link it to these practices.[^s1][^s2]
