# Message Equivocality, Media Selection, and Manager Performance

*Message Equivocality, Media Selection, and Manager Performance* is a 1987 *MIS Quarterly* article by Richard L. Daft, Robert H. Lengel and Linda Klebe Trevino. It reported field research with middle- and upper-level managers and made three claims: media differ in how many information cues they can carry; managers tend to choose richer media for ambiguous messages and leaner media for clear-cut ones; and high-performing managers are more attentive than low performers to that match between message and medium.[^s1][^s2] It is the work that framed choosing a channel as a managerial skill rather than a matter of habit or convenience.

## Origin

The article grew out of an Office of Naval Research programme at Texas A&M University on organisations as information-processing systems. A 1984 technical report by Lengel and Daft asked 95 managers at a large Houston petrochemical company which medium they would use for each of 60 real communication incidents. As the interpretive load of an incident rose, the share choosing face-to-face rose from about 14% to 84%, while addressed written documents fell from about 62% to 11%.[^s3] Daft and Lengel's 1986 *Management Science* paper then set out the theory: uncertainty (missing data) is reduced by more information, but equivocality (competing interpretations) is reduced only by exchanging views, and media can be ranked by richness - face-to-face, telephone, personal documents, impersonal documents, numeric reports - according to feedback speed, number of cues, personal focus and language variety.[^s7] The 1987 *MIS Quarterly* article applied that ranking to how managers actually choose, added the performance comparison, and drew implications for email and other electronic media that were then entering offices.[^s1][^s2]

A companion paper the same year in *Communication Research* widened the picture. Content analysis of incidents described by 65 managers in 11 organisations found three kinds of reasons for a channel choice: the message's ambiguity relative to the medium's richness, situational limits such as time and distance, and what the medium itself symbolises - choosing face-to-face can signal that a matter is important or that the sender cares.[^s4]

## Why it matters

The article gave practitioners a usable rule: match the richness of the channel to the ambiguity of the message, and treat that matching - "media sensitivity" - as part of managerial competence.[^s1][^s2] A later Air Force study summarised the 1987 finding as managers whose media perceptions fitted the theory tending to receive higher performance appraisals, and used it to argue that leaders should be deliberately media-sensitive.[^s5] Most modern advice on when to call rather than email traces back to this line of work.

## Using it at work

Before sending, ask whether the receiver mainly lacks facts or is likely to read the situation differently from you. A schedule update or a data request is low in equivocality, so a short email or chat lets the receiver process it on their own time.[^s7] A reorganisation, a disputed priority or a sensitive performance concern is high in equivocality, so open with a conversation and use writing to confirm what was agreed.[^s7][^s1] Also consider the symbolic message: the managers in the companion study often chose face-to-face partly for what it signalled, not only for its content, so the channel itself says something about how much the matter counts.[^s4]

For example, a team lead who must tell a colleague their project is being paused might first draft a two-line chat message. Reframed by this work, the lead books a 15-minute call to explain the reasons and hear the colleague's concerns, then sends a short written recap of next steps.[^s4][^s7]

## Evidence and limits

The 1987 findings rest on self-reported choices by managers in a small number of organisations, and later research complicated them.[^s3][^s4] Reviewing the field in 2005, Robert and Dennis described support for media richness theory as mixed, noted that it neglects social and situational influences, and cited Markus's study in which only about 43% of respondents chose the telephone where the theory predicted it.[^s6] They proposed a "paradox of richness": rich media raise a receiver's motivation to attend but can reduce their ability to process complex content, so complex messages may need a mix of rich and lean channels.[^s6] An Air Force thesis found members followed the theory's predictions about two-thirds of the time, least often for symbolic choices, and higher-ranking members were not consistently more media-sensitive.[^s5] Treat the article as a strong historical framing and a reasonable default, not as a proven law of performance.

## Connections

- **Introduces channel selection** - the article framed choosing a medium to fit a message's ambiguity as something managers do, and do better or worse.[^s1] It introduced the idea of **media sensitivity**, the high performers' closer attention to that fit.[^s2]
- **Authored by** Richard L. Daft, Robert H. Lengel and Linda Klebe Trevino.[^s1]
- **Influenced by media richness theory** - it builds directly on the richness hierarchy and the 1984 Lengel and Daft field data.[^s3][^s7]
- **Challenged by the paradox of richness** - Robert and Dennis argued that richer is not always better for understanding complex messages.[^s6]
- Related to **email**, the lean medium whose spread the article anticipated, and to **media synchronicity theory**, a later rival account of channel fit.[^s1][^s6]
