# The emerging field of emotion regulation

"The emerging field of emotion regulation: An integrative review" is a 1998 article by psychologist James J. Gross in *Review of General Psychology*.[^s1] It argued that research scattered across developmental, clinical, social, personality, cognitive, biological and health psychology was really one subject, offered a working definition of it, and proposed the process model that most later work on regulating feelings has used as its map.[^s1][^s2] For communicators, it is the source of the now-familiar distinction between changing how you read a tense moment and simply keeping a straight face over it.[^s1]

## Origin

Gross wrote the review when, by his account, emotion regulation had only recently become a fairly independent research area; he traced its first systematic study to developmental psychology and the way infants and children learn to manage distress.[^s1] He also placed it in two older lines of work: the psychoanalytic study of anxiety and ego defences, and the stress-and-coping tradition, especially Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman's split between problem-focused and emotion-focused coping.[^s1] The review does not claim to have coined the term; what it did was give the scattered literature a shared definition, a vocabulary and a set of open questions.[^s1]

Its definition treats emotion regulation as the ways people shape which feelings arise, when, and how those feelings are felt and shown.[^s1] Gross added several boundary choices: regulation can raise, hold or lower both pleasant and unpleasant emotions; it can be deliberate or automatic; and it is not good or bad in itself.[^s1] He then separated it from neighbouring ideas. Coping, in his framing, centres on reducing negative states over long stretches and includes practical problem-solving; mood repair focuses on diffuse moods rather than specific emotions; defences are usually unconscious and studied as stable traits; and "affect regulation" is the umbrella over all of them.[^s1]

## The process model

The review's best-known contribution is a model that orders regulation by where it acts in the unfolding of an emotion - situation, attention, meaning, response.[^s1] Four families act before the emotion is fully formed (antecedent-focused): **situation selection** (choosing which meetings, people or settings to enter), **situation modification** (changing the setting, such as turning a confrontation into a phone call), **attentional deployment** (distraction, concentration, or the trap of rumination) and **cognitive change** (reappraising what an event means or how well you can cope with it).[^s1] A fifth, **response modulation**, acts after the emotion has started, for example relaxation or hiding your expression.[^s1]

Gross supported the model with early experiments: reappraising an upsetting film tended to reduce felt distress, while suppressing visible expression left some feelings intact, raised sympathetic arousal, and in other studies impaired memory for what was happening.[^s1] He was candid that the model was a set of working distinctions rather than a full taxonomy, that it said little about mechanisms or relative effectiveness, and that inferring regulation is hard because you rarely know what the unregulated response would have been.[^s1]

## Why it matters

The model turned "stay calm" into choices a person can actually make at different moments of a hard conversation. Before a difficult performance review, a manager can select the setting (a private room, not a corridor), modify the situation (send the agenda in advance), direct attention (to the evidence, not the employee's sigh) and reappraise (treating pushback as information, not an attack) - each earlier than the last-ditch move of masking irritation.[^s1] This is why later communication advice about reappraisal, self-distancing and timing a cooling-off period can trace back to a common frame.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The model has proved durable as an organising scheme. A 2012 meta-analysis coded 306 experimental comparisons by the review's families and called the process model perhaps the most widely used model to date.[^s2] Its results only partly matched the review's early contrasts: cognitive change had the largest average effect (d = 0.36), attentional deployment had none overall (distraction helped, concentrating on the feeling hurt), and suppressing facial expression actually worked modestly, while suppressing the experience or the thoughts did not.[^s2]

Later critics argued that the field had read the model too simply. Bonanno and Burton (2013) called the habit of treating reappraisal as always good and suppression as always bad a "fallacy of uniform efficacy", and argued that what predicts adjustment is flexibility - reading the situation, having a repertoire, and switching when a strategy fails.[^s3] Gross himself later extended the model to cover the decisions around strategy use - noticing the need to regulate, choosing a strategy, carrying it out, and monitoring it over time - and applied it across emotions, stress, impulses and moods.[^s4] The 1998 review is therefore best read as the field's founding map, not its last word: useful for naming options, weaker on predicting which one will work in a given meeting.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

The review is **authored by** James J. Gross, and it **introduces** the process model of emotion regulation, which it sets out as its central proposal.[^s1] It also **introduces** emotion regulation in the sense of naming and defining it as a distinct field and popularising its modern definition, while crediting earlier developmental work.[^s1] It is **influenced by** Richard Lazarus, whose appraisal and coping research supplied both the reappraisal experiments and the coping distinctions it builds on, and it is **studied in** psychology.[^s1] It is **supported by** the Webb, Miles and Sheeran meta-analysis, which used the model's families as its coding scheme.[^s2] Related nodes include cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression, the two strategies its later experiments made famous, and regulatory flexibility, the critique that reframed them as context-dependent.[^s1][^s3]
