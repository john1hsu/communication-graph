# Army Field Manual 6-0

*FM 6-0, Commander and Staff Organization and Operations* is the U.S. Army's field manual on how a commander's staff is organised and how it does its routine work: planning, running estimates, staff papers and briefings. The 2014 edition (amended in 2015 and 2016) is the version most often cited for briefings, because its chapter 7 sets out, in about five pages, what kinds of briefing exist, what each must contain and how to prepare one.[^s1] For civilian readers it is the clearest official statement of the briefing discipline that management writers later adapted for "briefing an executive".[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

FM 6-0 was issued by Headquarters, Department of the Army, on 5 May 2014, superseding an earlier Army tactics manual on staff organisation.[^s1] It did not invent its briefing taxonomy. A 1996 Army lesson plan for intelligence officers already taught the same four types and told briefers to give the bottom line and the recommended course of action first.[^s2] The Marine Corps teaches the same four types at its officer Basic School, so the taxonomy is shared military practice that FM 6-0 codified rather than created.[^s3] In May 2022 the Army released a revised FM 6-0, refocused on the command-and-control system, with planning material moved to a new FM 5-0.[^s4]

## What it says

- **Four types.** An *information* briefing presents facts so the audience understands them and asks for no decision; a *decision* briefing seeks an answer or a choice of course of action; a *mission* briefing passes on critical details of an order; a *staff* briefing keeps the commander and staff current so their efforts stay coordinated.[^s1]
- **Recommendation first.** The decision-briefing format puts the problem statement and the recommendation in the introduction, before the facts, assumptions, options and comparison. The briefer words the recommendation precisely so it can become the decision statement, and asks for a decision if none is given.[^s1]
- **Match the format to what the decision maker knows.** A leader new to the problem gets the full format; one who knows it gets something closer to a decision paper - problem, essential background, impacts, recommendation - with the detail held in reserve.[^s1]
- **Four steps: plan, prepare, execute, assess.** Planning starts with the audience: who they are, how well they know the subject, and how the decision maker likes information presented. Preparation includes verifying facts, answering likely questions in advance and rehearsing. Assessment means writing a memorandum for record of what was decided and sending it to whoever must act.[^s1]
- **Delivery.** Brevity rules out long introductions; conclusions must follow from facts and assumptions; questions may come at any time and should be answered or explicitly deferred.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Most workplace advice on executive briefings - say why you are there, lead with the ask, keep the evidence in reserve, expect interruption - is a restatement of these military formats.[^s1][^s3] The manual's value to a civilian is that it is short, concrete and checkable. Suppose a project manager has 15 minutes with a vice-president to get a vendor switch approved. Following the decision-briefing format, she opens by saying it is a decision brief, states the problem in one sentence and gives her recommendation; then she covers facts (including the unfavourable ones), assumptions, the two alternatives and the criteria she used; then she restates the recommendation and asks for the decision. Afterwards she emails a short record of what was approved and who acts next.[^s1] The record step is the one most civilian teams skip, and it is what prevents two attendees leaving with different understandings of the decision.[^s1]

## Limits

FM 6-0 is doctrine, not research: it gives no evidence that its formats improve understanding or decisions, and we found no study testing them.[^s1] Its formats also invite ritual. Army writers have proposed short alternatives - such as a two-minute drill in which each staff section gives a time-stamped update - because longer update briefings repeat information and still leave units with conflicting requests.[^s5] A Virginia Tech thesis argued that slide-driven briefing had become the default in U.S. military and government settings and that staged delivery could matter more than content in which ideas won - a critical view, not a tested finding.[^s6] Readers should also treat it as one edition: the 2022 revision reorganised the manual, so check the current text before citing paragraph numbers.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduces the executive briefing (as codified practice):** its chapter 7 gives the named briefing types and formats that civilian briefing advice draws on, though the taxonomy predates it in Army and Marine Corps instruction.[^s1][^s2][^s3]
- **Related:** its recommendation-first decision brief is bottom line up front applied to speech - the 1996 lesson plan uses that phrase - and parallels the Army's earlier staff-writing guidance in *Effective Writing for Army Leaders*;[^s1][^s2] its planning checklist is a form of audience analysis and its preparation step requires rehearsal;[^s1] staff briefings overlap with status reporting;[^s5] and critics of slide-heavy briefing connect it to death by PowerPoint.[^s6]
