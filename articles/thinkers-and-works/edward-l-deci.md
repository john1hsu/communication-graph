# Edward L. Deci

Edward L. Deci was an American psychologist who spent his whole academic career at the University of Rochester and, with his long-time collaborator Richard Ryan, built self-determination theory (SDT), one of the most widely used accounts of human motivation.[^s1] For communicators his lasting contribution is a simple, testable claim: the *way* a request, a piece of feedback or a reward is delivered - as useful information that leaves people room to choose, or as pressure - changes whether people engage willingly or merely comply.[^s3][^s4] Much of today's advice on autonomy-supportive language, coaching conversations and non-controlling feedback traces back to his research programme.[^s3]

## Origin

Deci joined Rochester's psychology department in 1970, later held the Gowen professorship in the social sciences, and retired as professor emeritus in 2017; he died in February 2026, aged 83.[^s1] His 1971 paper on how extrinsic rewards affect intrinsic motivation started decades of work with Ryan; in the early 1980s the two co-led Rochester's Human Motivation Program.[^s1] Their 1985 book *Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior* set out the theory, Deci's 1991 *Why We Do What We Do* brought it to general readers, and their 2017 Guilford volume consolidated it; in 2015 they co-founded the non-profit Center for Self-Determination Theory.[^s1] SDT holds that people thrive when three basic needs are met - competence, autonomy and relatedness - and that intrinsic motivation is sustained when the social surroundings support them.[^s1]

He took the theory into the workplace with James Connell and Ryan in a 1989 field study in the service division of a large office-equipment company (Xerox, per the acknowledgements), with data from nearly 1,000 technicians and managers.[^s2] The paper defined managers' support for self-determination as three communication habits: allowing choice and initiative, giving positive feedback that is informational rather than controlling, and acknowledging the employee's perspective and feelings.[^s2] That definition is the root of the modern idea of autonomy support at work.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Deci's research gives managers a precise vocabulary for a familiar experience: the same instruction can land as help or as a leash. In later reviews with Marylène Gagné he described autonomy-supportive managers as those who acknowledge the other person's view, give information in a non-controlling way, offer choice and invite self-initiation, and he reported that giving a meaningful rationale and admitting a task may be dull helped people take on even uninteresting work as their own.[^s3] The same programme found that identical performance-contingent rewards raised motivation when delivered in a supportive climate and lowered it when delivered in a controlling one, so incentive schemes framed around pressure to hit the numbers can backfire.[^s3]

A workplace illustration: a support-desk lead announces a new ticket-quality metric with "Scores below 90 will be flagged to me." Applying Deci's lens, she instead explains why the metric exists, asks agents which parts of it they find unfair, lets each choose how to raise their score, and frames weekly results as information for them rather than a ranking for her.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence

The 1989 study is instructive about both promise and limits. Managers' autonomy support was unrelated to employee attitudes at first, when pay freezes and job insecurity dominated, but correlated with trust in the company and with satisfaction later; after a 13-day-per-branch training intervention, trained managers became more autonomy supportive than comparison managers, while gains for their staff were modest (more trust and satisfaction with advancement, and a rise in overall satisfaction against falling comparison branches).[^s2] The authors themselves cited small samples, a missing comparison group at the final wave and possible regression to the mean.[^s2]

The reward findings were long contested. Critics, notably Cameron and Pierce's 1994 meta-analysis and Eisenberger and Cameron in 1996, argued that the undermining effect of rewards was largely a myth.[^s4] Deci, Koestner and Ryan's 1999 meta-analysis of 128 experiments, summarised in 2001, answered that expected tangible rewards did reduce free-choice intrinsic motivation, whereas verbal praise generally raised it - but only when delivered in an informational style; praise delivered in a controlling style lowered it.[^s4] Gagné and Deci acknowledged that most of this evidence came from laboratory experiments and that many work-motivation scholars remained unconvinced it generalised to organisations.[^s3] Treat the communication lesson - informational beats controlling - as better supported than any blanket claim that pay harms motivation.

## Connections

Deci, with Ryan, **introduced self-determination theory**, the needs-based account of motivation behind his whole career.[^s1] With Connell and Ryan he **introduced autonomy-supportive language** for managers, defining it as choice, informational feedback and acknowledging perspective in the 1989 Xerox study.[^s2] His work is related to **psychological reactance**, the backlash that controlling messages provoke; to **work motivation** and **employee engagement**, where SDT is now a leading account;[^s3] to **feedback intervention theory**, a separate account of when feedback helps; and to **coaching** and **manager one-on-ones**, the conversations where autonomy support is most often practised.[^s3][^s4]
