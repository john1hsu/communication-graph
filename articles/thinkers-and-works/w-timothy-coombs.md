# W. Timothy Coombs

W. Timothy Coombs is an American communication scholar whose main research and consulting area is crisis communication: what an organization should say, and in what order, when an accident, product harm, scandal or rumour threatens the people it serves and its reputation.[^s5] He holds a professorship in communication at Texas A&M University, is a senior research fellow of Penn State's Arthur W. Page Center, and has edited the *Journal of Contingencies and Crisis Management*.[^s4][^s5] For practitioners, his lasting contribution is a simple, testable idea: there is no single right crisis response, because the right words depend on how much blame your audience already assigns you.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Coombs trained first in engineering at Bowling Green State University, then took graduate degrees at Purdue, where his 1990 PhD covered issue management, public affairs and organizational communication.[^s4][^s6] He taught at Illinois State, Clemson, Wayne State, Eastern Illinois and the University of Central Florida before moving to Texas A&M in 2015.[^s6] When he started, crisis communication was not yet a recognized academic specialty; by his account it only began to be treated as one in the late 1980s or early 1990s, and his work helped build it.[^s4]

His textbook *Ongoing Crisis Communication: Planning, Managing, and Responding* first appeared from Sage in 1999 and has gone through several later editions; he also wrote *Code Red in the Boardroom* (2006) and co-edited the *Handbook of Crisis Communication* (2010) with Sherry Holladay.[^s5][^s6] With Holladay he published the first experimental tests of situational crisis communication theory in 2002, in which 130 students rated real crisis cases; the crises sorted into victim, accidental and preventable clusters, and in every cluster more perceived responsibility went with a worse reputation.[^s2] His 2007 *Corporate Reputation Review* article then set out SCCT in full.[^s1] Professional recognition followed, including the Public Relations Society of America's Jackson, Jackson & Wagner Behavioral Science Prize in 2002 and the Institute for Public Relations' Pathfinder Award in 2013.[^s5][^s6]

## Why it matters

SCCT, rooted in attribution theory, says the reputational threat of a crisis depends on initial crisis responsibility, the organization's crisis history and its prior reputation.[^s1] Responses fall into denial, diminish and rebuild groups, with bolstering as a supplement, and Coombs insists that instructing information (what people should do to stay safe) and adjusting information (concern and corrective action) come before any reputation work.[^s1] He also argues that over-accommodating does not pay: in an accident, a full apology yields no more reputational benefit than an excuse paired with good adjusting information.[^s1]

A workplace illustration: a software firm's payment service goes down for three hours after a vendor's data centre fails. Applying Coombs's approach, the communications lead first tells customers what is affected and what to do, expresses concern and explains the fix, then states the cause plainly. Because customers are likely to see this as an accident rather than a preventable failure, the lead avoids both blaming the vendor in a way that sounds like denial and an over-the-top apology, and saves a stronger rebuild response for a case where the firm was clearly at fault or had a history of similar outages.[^s1][^s2]

## Limits

His theory's early evidence came from student samples reading short crisis descriptions, a limitation Coombs and Holladay acknowledged.[^s2] Later work is less flattering to the matching idea: Ji and Kim report that a 2016 meta-analysis of SCCT studies (Ma and Zhan) found the effect of response strategies on protecting reputation to be rather limited.[^s3] Treat his framework as a disciplined way to think about responsibility and audience needs, not as a guarantee that the right formula will save a reputation.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

Coombs **introduced** situational crisis communication theory (`cm:situational-crisis-communication-theory`), first tested in `cm:coombs-holladay-2002` and fully presented in 2007.[^s1][^s2] He is widely credited with helping establish `cm:crisis-communication` as a research specialty.[^s4] He **authored** *Ongoing Crisis Communication* (`cm:ongoing-crisis-communication`) and co-edited the *Handbook of Crisis Communication* (`cm:handbook-of-crisis-communication`).[^s5][^s6] SCCT's response groups link his work to `cm:denial`, `cm:apology` and `cm:trust-repair`, and the field it sits in is `cm:public-relations`.[^s1][^s3]
