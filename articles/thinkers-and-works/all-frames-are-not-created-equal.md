# All Frames Are Not Created Equal

*All Frames Are Not Created Equal* is a 1998 review article by Irwin P. Levin and Gary J. Gaeth of the University of Iowa and Sandra L. Schneider of the University of South Florida, published in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*.[^s2] Its argument is that "framing" - describing the same facts in positive or negative terms - is not one effect but three, and that researchers had been getting conflicting results because they were measuring different things under the same name.[^s1] For anyone who writes proposals, updates or requests at work, it is the paper that separates "how I describe a number" from "how I describe the consequences of acting", and so tells you which body of evidence actually applies to your message.[^s2][^s5]

## Origin

By the late 1990s framing research had grown out of Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's 1981 risky-choice studies into a sprawling literature whose findings did not line up.[^s1] Levin, Schneider and Gaeth reviewed it and proposed a typology of three kinds of valence framing: risky choice framing, the standard Tversky-Kahneman effect on willingness to take a gamble; attribute framing, which shifts how people evaluate a single characteristic of an object or event; and goal framing, which affects how persuasive a communication is.[^s2] For each type they described typical examples and the processes most likely to drive it, and argued that grouping studies this way resolves apparent contradictions and points to better research.[^s1][^s2]

## What the typology says

The three types differ in what is framed and what is measured. In **attribute framing** one feature is described as a positive or negative proportion - meat labelled "75% lean" rather than "25% fat", or a project team described by its success rate rather than its failure rate - and people rate the positively described option more favourably.[^s5] In **risky choice framing** people choose between a sure thing and a gamble of equal expected value, and tend to avoid risk when options are described as gains and accept it when they are described as losses.[^s5] In **goal framing** the action is the same in both versions; what changes is whether the message stresses the benefits of doing it or the costs of not doing it.[^s4][^s5] The paper's claim about goal framing was that emphasising what people lose by not acting tended to be more persuasive.[^s5]

## Why it matters at work

The typology is a practical diagnostic. Before relying on "framing" advice, ask which kind of frame your message uses. Reporting a supplier's on-time rate as "92% on time" rather than "8% late" is attribute framing; presenting a sure-but-modest plan against a risky-but-bigger one is risky choice framing; telling staff what they gain by finishing a security course versus what they risk by skipping it is goal framing.[^s2][^s5] Evidence from one type does not transfer to another: a 2002 follow-up study found the three effects were statistically unrelated within the same people, so someone swayed by one kind of frame is not necessarily swayed by the others.[^s3] The common workplace mistake - citing loss aversion from risky-choice research to justify loss-framed persuasive messages - is exactly the category error the paper warned against.[^s1][^s3]

Sher and McKenzie add a caution for writers: logically equivalent frames are not always informationally equivalent, because speakers tend to pick the frame that highlights what they consider salient, and listeners can reasonably read that choice as a signal.[^s5] Choosing a frame is therefore part of what you communicate, not neutral packaging.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

The typology has held up as an organising scheme. Piñón and Gambara's 2005 meta-analysis adopted it and judged it useful for structuring the literature; across the studies they pooled, they reported average effects of d = 0.44 for goal framing, 0.44 for risky choice framing and 0.26 for attribute framing, with the number of options in a task the strongest moderator of goal framing.[^s4] The paper's specific claim about goal framing has fared worse. In Levin and colleagues' own 2002 within-subject study of 102 participants, attribute and risky choice framing produced reliable effects but goal framing did not.[^s3] O'Keefe and Wu's 2012 meta-analysis of 33 randomised studies (N = 4,168) found no significant difference in persuasiveness between gain-framed and loss-framed skin-cancer prevention appeals.[^s6] The paper is best read as the origin of the categories, not as proof that loss frames persuade.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

The paper was **authored by** Irwin P. Levin, Sandra L. Schneider and Gary J. Gaeth.[^s2] It **introduces** **gain-loss framing** - which it called goal framing - as a distinct type of framing about persuasive communication, and set out **attribute framing** as a separate category concerned with evaluations of single characteristics.[^s2] It was **influenced by** **Amos Tversky**, whose 1981 work with Daniel Kahneman supplied the risky-choice effect the typology starts from.[^s1] Its scheme is **supported by** Piñón and Gambara's 2005 meta-analysis, which organised the field around it.[^s4] Its goal-framing claim is **challenged by** the 2002 within-subject study by Levin, Gaeth, Schreiber and Lauriola and by O'Keefe and Wu's 2012 meta-analysis.[^s3][^s6] It sits under **persuasion** and alongside **leadership framing**, **risk numbers as natural frequencies** and **cognitive bias**, which deal with other ways the wording of facts shapes decisions.[^s5]
