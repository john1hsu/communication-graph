# Scott Fahlman

Scott E. Fahlman is a research professor of computer science at Carnegie Mellon University (CMU) who is credited with proposing the sideways smiley, ":-)", as a marker for jokes in online text.[^s2][^s3] He made the suggestion on 19 September 1982 on one of the CMU computer science department's electronic bulletin boards ("bboards"), and the convention spread to other universities and research labs within months.[^s1][^s3] For anyone writing work email or chat, his proposal is the origin story of a problem that has not gone away: plain text strips out the voice and face that tell a listener whether you are joking, irritated or relaxed.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

In the early 1980s the CMU bboards were a busy, informal forum, and many posts were humorous.[^s3] Fahlman's own account describes the pattern that kept recurring: a reader would miss the sarcasm in a post, reply with a long serious rebuttal, others would pile in, and the original discussion would be buried.[^s1] In at least one case, he recalls, a joke was read as a genuine safety warning.[^s1] Naomi Baron's account of the episode identifies it as a mock "WARNING" about a mercury spill that some readers took seriously.[^s5]

Fahlman's fix was a short character sequence to be read sideways: ":-)" to mark a post as a joke, and ":-(" for posts that were not, which he wryly suggested might be the more economical thing to label.[^s1][^s2] Variants such as the winking ";-)" and a smiley with glasses soon followed, and lists of dozens of "smilies" circulated within a few months.[^s3] The original messages were lost for two decades until a 2001-2002 search of old CMU backup tapes, sponsored by a Microsoft researcher and carried out by CMU facilities staff, recovered the thread.[^s1]

Fahlman is careful about the claim. He describes himself as at least one of the inventors, believes he came up with the idea independently, and acknowledges reports that others typed similar faces earlier; his 1982 post is the one that caught on.[^s1] CMU itself treats the post as the emoticon's birth and traces the later winking, crying and pictorial emoji back to it.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Fahlman's problem is the one that tone-in-text guidance still addresses. Linguists Eli Dresner and Susan Herring cite his 1982 proposal as the starting point and argue that emoticons rarely just show emotion; more often they signal what the writer is doing with the words, such as joking, softening a request or toning down a complaint.[^s4] That is the job a smiley still does at the end of "can you take another pass at slide 4 :)": it tells the reader the request is friendly, not a reprimand.[^s4]

Fahlman is also a useful sceptic about his own invention. He agrees with critics that skilled writers seldom need to label a joke and suggests the smiley may have done written communication more harm than good; he defends it mainly for casual online posts, where a few smileys can head off flame wars.[^s1] He also dislikes software that converts the typed characters into small pictures, saying it removes the whimsy of the original.[^s1]

The research since 1982 supports his caution more than his invention's popularity. Baron reviews studies showing that the meaning of emoticons depends on the relationship, the group, personality and culture, that in some one-to-one exchanges readers relied more on the words than on the emoticon, and that a single symbol can carry several meanings, so it does not reliably remove ambiguity.[^s5]

## Applying the history at work

Read Fahlman's story as a design brief rather than a licence to add smileys. The failure he saw was a reader filling in tone the writer never intended; the durable fix is to put the tone into words first ("no rush", "half-joking here", "this is a small fix") and treat an emoji as an optional extra in informal internal chat.[^s1][^s4] When a thread starts to escalate the way the bboard arguments did, the lesson is to stop adding symbols and change channel to a call.[^s1][^s5]

## Limits

The account of the 1982 thread rests largely on Fahlman's recollection and the recovered posts he published; the earlier precedents he mentions are unverified either way.[^s1] Calling him "the inventor of the emoticon" overstates what he claims, and the claim that his smiley grew into today's emoji is a lineage CMU draws rather than a documented design history.[^s1][^s2] The practical advice above is drawn from later communication research, not from anything Fahlman tested.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

- **Introduced the emoticon** ":-)" and ":-(" as typed tone markers on CMU's bulletin boards in 1982.[^s1][^s2]
- **Introduced the practice behind emoji and tone in text**: marking the intended force of a written message with a symbol; confidence is medium because later emoji use goes well beyond joke-marking.[^s4]
- **Related to paralanguage and nonverbal communication**, the vocal and facial cues that emoticons try to stand in for, and to **the illusion of transparency and egocentric bias**, which explain why writers assume their tone is obvious when it is not.[^s4][^s5]
- **Related to email and Ray Tomlinson**, as another early design decision that still shapes how people write online at work.[^s2]
