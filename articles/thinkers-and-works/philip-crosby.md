# Philip Crosby

Philip B. Crosby (1926-2001) was an American quality-management executive, consultant and author, best known for the "zero defects" standard and for defining quality as conformance to requirements.[^s2][^s1] For communicators he matters for a narrower reason: the short spoken case to a senior executive that we now call the elevator pitch is first documented in print in his advice to quality managers.[^s5][^s6][^s7]

## Origin

Born in Wheeling, West Virginia, Crosby served as a Navy and later Marine medical corpsman, then worked in quality roles at Crosley, Bendix and Martin Marietta, where he developed his zero-defects ideas and began writing and speaking.[^s8] From 1965 he spent 14 years as ITT's vice-president of corporate quality, rising from line inspector earlier in his career.[^s8][^s1] *Quality Is Free* (1979) became a best-seller; that year he left ITT and founded Philip Crosby Associates and its Quality College in Winter Park, Florida, which trained tens of thousands of managers.[^s1][^s4] He wrote 13 books in all.[^s2]

The elevator speech appears in the 1981 second edition of his career book *The Art of Getting Your Own Sweet Way* (McGraw-Hill; first edition 1972).[^s5][^s6] Etymology researchers report that Crosby told students of quality management to learn a roughly one-minute set of ideas, ready to deliver to the big boss in the time an elevator ride allows.[^s5][^s6] Later writers carried "elevator speech" into management advice through the 1980s, and "elevator pitch" is in print by 1997.[^s5] Other origin stories, such as screenwriters cornering producers in lifts, circulate too, so Crosby is best described as the earliest documented source rather than the proven inventor.[^s7]

## Why it matters

Crosby's career is a case study in getting a technical subject onto an executive agenda. He framed quality in the language senior managers already used - money - by measuring it as the cost of doing things wrong, which let him argue that prevention pays for itself.[^s1][^s4] In a 1997 interview he credited the book's success to translating a subject that had become jargon into plain English for executives, and said that anyone with an idea has to sell it, because no one else will.[^s3] The elevator speech is the compact form of that stance: know your case well enough to make it in a minute, to the person who can act on it.[^s5]

Three practical lessons carry over to everyday workplace communication:

- **Prepare the short version before you need it.** Crosby's point was readiness for an unplanned moment with a decision-maker, not a scripted presentation.[^s5][^s6]
- **Translate into the listener's measure.** Expressing quality problems as avoidable cost made a technical issue a business decision.[^s1][^s4]
- **Treat selling the idea as part of the job.** Specialists who wait to be asked are rarely heard.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

Crosby's reputation rests on practice and persuasion more than on research. A quality textbook notes that critics called him unusually well marketed and charismatic, while crediting him with acting on what he preached.[^s1] Contemporary press coverage reported client savings, such as a chipmaker's cost cuts, as company claims rather than independent evaluations.[^s4] On the elevator speech, the attribution rests on etymology research that dates his 1981 print use; it does not show he coined the phrase in speech, and none of this tests whether elevator pitches work - see the elevator pitch article for the field studies.[^s5][^s7]

## Connections

Crosby introduced the elevator speech that became the elevator pitch, as documented in etymology research on its first print appearance.[^s5] He authored *Quality Is Free*, the book that made his name,[^s1] and *The Art of Getting Your Own Sweet Way*, the career guide where the elevator speech appears.[^s5] His advice anticipates issue selling and managing up - getting a concern onto a senior agenda - and his insistence on plain English for executives links him to plain language, bottom line up front and the executive briefing.[^s3]
