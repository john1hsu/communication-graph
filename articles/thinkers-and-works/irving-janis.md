# Irving Janis

Irving Janis was an American social psychologist, born in Buffalo, New York, in 1918, who took his PhD at Columbia University, spent his career as a professor of psychology at Yale, and died in 1990.[^s1] Communicators know him for two things: his early experiments on persuasion in the Yale communication programme, and the word "groupthink" - his name for the way a close-knit team can talk itself into a bad decision because nobody wants to break the consensus.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Janis began in persuasion research. He co-wrote *Communication and Persuasion* (1953) with Carl Hovland and Harold Kelley, the Yale volume that treated opinion change as something to test experimentally.[^s6] In the same year he and Seymour Feshbach published a study of fear-arousing health messages: secondary-school pupils who heard the mildest warnings about dental hygiene adopted more of the advice, and resisted later counter-arguments better, than those given the most frightening version - a result that set off a long debate about fear appeals.[^s7]

The groupthink idea came out of a Yale seminar on the psychology of small groups.[^s2] Janis first described it in a 1971 magazine article, choosing a deliberately Orwellian-sounding word,[^s5] and developed it in *Victims of Groupthink: A Psychological Study of Foreign-Policy Decisions and Fiascoes* (1972).[^s3] He built the theory from case studies of U.S. policy failures such as Pearl Harbor, the Bay of Pigs and the escalation of the Vietnam War,[^s3] and set them against cases where teams decided well, such as the Marshall Plan and the Cuban missile crisis.[^s4] He later worked on the psychology of decision making more broadly, including sources of error in decision processes.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Janis gave managers a vocabulary for a meeting failure they had all seen: the illusion of unanimity, self-censorship, pressure on the colleague who raises a doubt, and "mindguards" who keep awkward information away from the group.[^s3] Victor Vroom, his Yale colleague, credits him with challenging the management literature's assumption that group decisions were simply better.[^s2] The concept spread into business schools, military colleges and management training, and a 2005 U.S. review of pre-war intelligence on Iraqi weapons cited groupthink when the CIA changed some of its procedures.[^s2]

More practically, Janis turned the diagnosis into a meeting design. His prevention advice, summarised in later reviews, includes having the leader hold back their own preference at the start, assigning one or more devil's advocates at every meeting, splitting the group into subgroups or parallel teams, bringing in outside experts to challenge the core members, and holding a "second-chance" meeting to air remaining doubts before a decision goes public.[^s4] For a workplace example: a project lead who wants an honest review of a launch date can open the meeting by asking for risks before stating a view, name a colleague to argue the case for delay, and schedule a short follow-up the next day before the date is announced to customers. Each step makes dissent a planned part of the process rather than a personal risk.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

Janis's own method was retrospective case analysis, and the empirical record is thin and mixed.[^s5] One review found that only a few of the model's many variables had been adequately tested and that research on the prevention steps themselves was scarce; impartial, non-directive leadership was among the better-supported elements.[^s4] Critics go further. Won-Woo Park's 2000 test of 23 predictions supported only two, and another study found cohesion linked to more dissent, not less - the opposite of what the theory expects.[^s5] Ramon Aldag argues that groupthink's popularity rests more on retrospective sense-making than on evidence,[^s5] and Britannica notes that not every bad decision stems from groupthink, nor does every cohesive team decide badly.[^s3] Read Janis as a source of useful safeguards for high-stakes, high-pressure decisions rather than as a proven causal model.[^s5]

## Connections

Janis introduced the concept of groupthink and set it out in *Victims of Groupthink*.[^s3] He popularised the assigned devil's advocate as a standing safeguard in group decision making, which is why devil's advocacy is attributed to him here; the role itself is centuries older.[^s4] He co-authored *Communication and Persuasion* with Carl Hovland, linking him to the Yale persuasion tradition.[^s6] His model is challenged by Park's 2000 test and the wider critique Aldag summarises.[^s5] He is related to group decision making, facilitation and workplace meetings, where his safeguards are applied, and to psychological safety, which decides whether doubts get voiced at all.[^s4]
