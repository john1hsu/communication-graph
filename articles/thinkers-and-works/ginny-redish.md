# Ginny Redish

Janice "Ginny" Redish is an American linguist and consultant whose work turned "write clearly" from an exhortation into a set of testable design practices for workplace and public documents.[^s4][^s5] She directed the federally funded Document Design Project and the Document Design Center at the American Institutes for Research (AIR), co-wrote the 1981 *Guidelines for Document Designers*, and later helped found usability testing as a profession.[^s1][^s2][^s4] Many conventions now taught as plain-language basics, such as informative headings, active voice, short sentences and breaking parallel items into a list, appear in that 1981 book with the research behind each.[^s1]

## Origin

Redish earned a PhD in linguistics at Harvard and joined AIR in Washington, DC.[^s4] In September 1978 AIR began the Document Design Project, funded by the National Institute of Education with Carnegie-Mellon University and the design firm Siegel & Gale, to make forms, regulations and brochures easier to read and use; Redish directed it.[^s2] Her description of the project in *Applied Psycholinguistics* in 1980 reports studies of how people actually comprehend public documents, including experiments on complex conditional statements in forms and work with Spanish-speaking community members.[^s2] In 1979 she founded the Document Design Center at AIR and led it for about ten years.[^s4][^s5] A 2005 profile in the Usability Professionals' Association newsletter describes how she adapted AIR's instructional systems design method, with its formative and summative evaluation, into a process model for designing documents that resembles today's user-centred design.[^s3]

The project's best-known output was *Guidelines for Document Designers* (1981), written by Daniel Felker, Frances Pickering, Veda Charrow, Melissa Holland and Redish for writers with no formal training.[^s1] It gives 25 guidelines in four groups (organising text, writing sentences, typography and graphics), each with poor and better examples, related principles, qualifications and a summary of what the research says, drawn from cognitive psychology, psycholinguistics, human factors, instructional psychology and typography.[^s1] Guideline B-8 tells writers to break a long sentence full of parallel items into a list set off by bullets or another typographic device, and B-7 recommends stating a rule first and then listing its conditions separately, citing experiments in which people followed complex instructions more accurately when the parts were set out separately.[^s1] When Redish reissued the book in 2014 she judged 24 of the 25 guidelines still valid, updating only the advice on type size and on underlining, which now signals a web link.[^s1]

In the early 1980s IBM asked her to apply the same methods to computer manuals, which led her to test software as well as documents; in 1985 she set up one of the first independent usability laboratories in North America, and in 1992 she left AIR to found Redish & Associates.[^s3][^s4] Her books include *A Practical Guide to Usability Testing*, *User and Task Analysis for Interface Design* and *Letting Go of the Words*, a guide to writing for the web.[^s4][^s5]

## Why it matters

Redish's central idea is that every document, form or screen is one side of a conversation with a real reader, so clarity is judged by whether that reader can find, understand and act on the content, not by how the text looks to the writer.[^s3] For a workplace writer that means three habits. First, design for the task: put the action and the conditions where a scanning reader will see them, using headings, lists and tables rather than one dense paragraph.[^s1] Second, treat guidelines as starting points with stated limits; the 1981 book attaches a "qualifications" note to each rule and marks where the research is thin or indirect.[^s1] Third, check the draft with people like the intended readers, the same formative evaluation she brought from instructional design into documents and then software.[^s3]

A concrete case: a benefits team drafting a leave policy can apply B-7 by stating the rule ("You may carry over up to five days") and then listing the conditions underneath, rather than burying "and" and "or" clauses mid-sentence, and then ask two or three employees to say back what the policy lets them do.[^s1][^s3]

Her influence is recognised in the plain-language field itself. The Plain Language Association International gave her its Christine Mowat Plain Language Achievement Award in 2023, citing more than 40 years of work with U.S. federal agencies and on clear, accessible ballots and voter materials.[^s5]

## Evidence and limits

As a historical figure her importance rests on documented work rather than on a single effect size. The research summarised in the 1981 guidelines was, by the authors' own account, representative rather than exhaustive, and several entries note mixed or indirect findings, so individual rules should be read as well-supported heuristics rather than laws.[^s1] Accounts of her career on her own site and in a professional-association profile are friendly sources; the peer-reviewed 1980 overview and the guidelines themselves are the firmest record of what the Document Design Project did.[^s2][^s3][^s4]

## Connections

Redish co-authored *Guidelines for Document Designers*, the book whose guideline B-8 is an early research-backed instruction to set parallel items as bulleted or numbered lists, which is why she is recorded here as having introduced that technique into workplace writing guidance (with medium confidence, since lists themselves are far older).[^s1] Her own site lists *Letting Go of the Words* and *A Practical Guide to Usability Testing* among her books.[^s4] The 2005 profile traces her process model of document design to AIR's instructional systems design method.[^s3] Her work sits squarely within plain language and audience analysis, shares the active-voice advice of William Strunk Jr., and anticipates later reader-testing methods such as paraphrase testing and usability testing.[^s1][^s3][^s5]
