# The Power of Feedback Revisited

*The Power of Feedback Revisited* is a 2020 meta-analysis by Benedikt Wisniewski and Klaus Zierer of the University of Augsburg and John Hattie of the University of Melbourne. Pooling 994 effect sizes from 435 studies with roughly 61,000 participants, it found that feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average (d = 0.48 after removing outliers) but that its effects vary enormously, and that the amount of useful information in the feedback is one of the clearest predictors of how much it helps.[^s1] For anyone who gives feedback at work, its message is that "give more feedback" is the wrong instruction; "give more informative feedback" is closer to the evidence.[^s2]

## Origin

Hattie's earlier syntheses, above all the 2007 *Review of Educational Research* article with Helen Timperley, had ranked feedback among the strongest influences on learning, with an average effect of 0.79 drawn from 12 meta-analyses and 196 studies.[^s3] Those figures came from averaging other meta-analyses, a "meta-synthesis" approach that statisticians had criticised, notably for treating Cohen's d as a universal yardstick of impact across very different studies.[^s4] The 2020 team went back to the 732 primary studies behind 32 meta-analyses used in the Visible Learning work, kept those with a control group or pre-post comparison, a learning outcome and an educational setting, and re-analysed them with a random-effects model that handles dependent effects.[^s1]

## What the study did and found

The average effect was d = 0.55 with all effects and 0.48 once 35 extreme outliers were removed, lower than the 0.79 from the earlier synthesis.[^s1] Heterogeneity was very high (I² above 80%), and 17% of the effects were negative, so a single average describes feedback poorly.[^s1]

The strongest moderator was information content. Feedback that was only reward or punishment had a small effect (d = 0.24); corrective feedback about right and wrong answers had a moderate one (0.46); and high-information feedback, which adds how to do the task better and how to monitor one's own work, had a large one (0.99).[^s1] Feedback helped cognitive and motor skills more than motivation, and most of the negative effects on motivation came from uninformative rewards and punishments.[^s1] Whether feedback was written, spoken or delivered by computer made no significant difference.[^s1] The authors concluded that feedback is powerful on average, but some kinds are far more powerful than others.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The study translates into workplace habits even though its data come from education. Hattie and Timperley's model, which the meta-analysis builds on, says useful feedback answers three questions: where the person is going, how they are doing, and what to do next. It also holds that praise aimed at the person ("you're a star") carries the least information and is the least effective.[^s3][^s2] Take a manager reviewing a junior analyst's first client deck. "Nice work" is reward-only feedback. "Slide 4 is wrong" is corrective. High-information feedback would add: the recommendation is on slide 9, so move it to slide 1; check each chart title states the takeaway; next time, compare your opening against the client's question before sending.[^s1][^s3] Because channel did not matter much in the data, that information is what to protect when feedback has to go by email or chat rather than face to face.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The core result, a positive but highly variable effect, agrees with Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 meta-analysis of feedback interventions, though this study found a smaller share of harmful effects (17% against about a third).[^s2] Several limits matter. The studies are from schools and universities, not workplaces, so applying them to employees is an extrapolation. Egger's test showed funnel-plot asymmetry among journal articles, a sign of possible publication bias. Few studies measured motor or behavioural outcomes, and some effect sizes had to be reconstructed.[^s1] Some moderators that Kluger and DeNisi identified, such as timing and task complexity, were not tested.[^s2] And the broader critique of pooling Cohen's d across very different designs, aimed at Visible Learning, still applies to any single headline number.[^s4] Because the effect is real but strongly context-dependent, its evidence status here is mixed.

## Connections

The study was **introduced by Benedikt Wisniewski** (`cm:benedikt-wisniewski`), **Klaus Zierer** (`cm:klaus-zierer`) and **John Hattie** (`cm:john-hattie`).[^s1] It was **influenced by the Hattie and Timperley feedback model** (`cm:hattie-timperley-feedback-model`), whose levels of feedback it used to frame information content.[^s3] It **complements Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 meta-analysis** (`cm:kluger-denisi-1996`): the 2020 authors set their results beside it as one of the most comprehensive earlier reviews, and both find a positive average with a substantial share of harmful effects.[^s2] It sits under **feedback** and supports task-focused techniques such as **Situation-Behavior-Impact** and **feedforward**, which aim to give the "what next" information the study links to bigger gains.[^s1][^s3] It belongs to the **Visible Learning** research programme (`cm:visible-learning`), whose methods have drawn statistical criticism.[^s4]
