# De Ira

*De Ira* (*On Anger*) is a Latin treatise in three books by the Roman Stoic philosopher Seneca the Younger, addressed to his brother Novatus.[^s1][^s2] It argues that anger is not an uncontrollable reflex but a response the mind consents to, and so one it can learn to refuse. Most of the work is practical: how to notice anger early, how to buy time before acting on it, and how to build habits that make it rarer.[^s3] For a modern reader in a tense email thread or a heated meeting, it is the classic source for the advice to pause before you reply.

## Origin

Seneca (c. 4 BC - AD 65) wrote the treatise in the first century AD; Margaret Graver treats it as one of his early works, and James Romm notes that it draws many of its cautionary examples from the reign of the emperor Caligula.[^s3][^s4] Seneca built on the earlier Stoic account of emotion: anger, like other passions, rests on assent to an impression - roughly, "he wronged me, and he should pay" - so it is voluntary in the Stoic sense.[^s2] He also engages earlier definitions, quoting Aristotle's description of anger as a desire to repay suffering.[^s1]

The key theoretical move is the distinction, in Book 2, between an involuntary first jolt and anger proper. A flush or start of shock happens to us and no reasoning can stop it; anger begins only when the mind approves that impulse and runs with it.[^s1][^s2] Graver argues that most of Seneca's practical advice is aimed at that window before assent, which fits his theory, while some Book 3 strategies for anger already in full swing sit in tension with it.[^s3]

## Why it matters

The treatise turns that theory into concrete rules that map onto modern workplace practice:[^s1]

- **Delay.** Seneca calls delay the best corrective of anger, and asks anger for time not so that it will pardon but so that it can judge (2.29).[^s1]
- **Check the report.** People readily believe what they hate to hear and become angry before they have formed a judgment, so allow some time, since a day often reveals the truth (2.22); credulity does great harm (2.24).[^s1]
- **Don't act while angry.** He tells of Plato, angry with a slave, handing the punishment to a colleague because he knew he would do more than he ought (3.12). Nothing should be permitted to you while angry.[^s1]
- **Take the other side's view.** Put yourself in the place of the person you are angry with (3.12), and remind yourself that you have done the same thing (2.28).[^s1]
- **Review each evening.** Following the philosopher Sextius, Seneca describes questioning himself every night about which fault he had cured that day (3.36).[^s1][^s2]

In workplace terms these are the holding reply, the fact-check before escalating, the rule never to send discipline or a performance message while upset, perspective-taking before a hard conversation, and a short end-of-day reflection. Romm's edition presents the work explicitly as an ancient guide to anger management and points to its relevance for today's uncivil public discourse.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

*De Ira* is philosophy and rhetoric, not an empirical study, and Seneca's claims rest on argument and anecdote.[^s1][^s3] Modern experiments partly support his central advice. In a 2011 set of experiments in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, Wang and colleagues found that a time delay reduced angry people's costly punishment of others, because the delay lowered negative emotion.[^s5] But the delay worked only for people who were distracted during it; those who ruminated on the provocation punished more, and those told to reappraise were unaffected.[^s5] So a pause spent replaying the grievance is not what Seneca's advice needs to work. The Stoic aim is also stricter than most modern advice: the SEP entry stresses that Seneca wants emotions replaced by rational responses rather than merely moderated, a goal modern emotion-regulation practice does not share.[^s2]
