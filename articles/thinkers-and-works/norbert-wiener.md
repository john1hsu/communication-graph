# Norbert Wiener

Norbert Wiener was an American mathematician who spent 41 years at MIT and founded cybernetics, which he defined as the study of control and communication in the animal and the machine.[^s3][^s5] He did not invent the feedback loop - thermostats and steam governors used it long before him - but he gave it a general mathematical form and argued that people, organisations and machines all steer themselves the same way: by comparing what actually happened with what they were aiming for, and correcting the difference.[^s1][^s3] That idea is the root of the everyday workplace meaning of "feedback", which is why the graph credits him with introducing it to communication.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Wiener was a child prodigy who entered Tufts at ten and finished a Harvard PhD at nineteen.[^s3] During the Second World War he worked on predicting the path of aircraft for anti-aircraft fire, which pushed him to model a pilot and a machine as one self-correcting system.[^s3] In 1943 he, the physiologist Arturo Rosenblueth and the engineer Julian Bigelow published "Behavior, Purpose and Teleology", which argued that all purposeful behaviour depends on negative feedback: action is guided by signals about how far the actor still is from the goal.[^s1] Their example was lifting a glass of water to the mouth; patients with cerebellar damage overshoot and oscillate with growing swings until the water spills, a failure of the feedback that normally damps the correction.[^s1]

He named the new field from the Greek word for steersman, and published *Cybernetics* in 1948 and the more popular *The Human Use of Human Beings* in 1950.[^s3][^s5][^s2] From 1946 to 1953 the Macy Conferences, first titled "Feedback Mechanisms and Circular Causal Systems in Biological and Social Systems", brought Wiener together with social scientists including Gregory Bateson, Margaret Mead and, until his death in 1947, Kurt Lewin.[^s4] The conferences never produced a unified theory, but they are widely seen as the route by which feedback reached the social sciences.[^s4]

## Why it matters

In *The Human Use of Human Beings* Wiener defined feedback as control based on a system's actual performance rather than its expected performance.[^s2] He grouped communication and control together because a command and a message rely on the same process, and he argued that giving an order to a person is not essentially different from giving one to a machine: in both cases what you know is the command sent and the signal of compliance that comes back.[^s2] He went further, claiming that a society can only be understood by studying its messages and communication channels, which he called the cement of social organisation.[^s2] Communication theorists still use this vocabulary of steering, deviation and correction to describe how conversations and organisations regulate themselves.[^s5]

## Applying his ideas at work

Three practical habits follow from Wiener's model. First, judge a message by what came back, not by what you sent: a request is not "communicated" until you have some return signal - a reply, a paraphrase, an action - that shows it landed, which is the logic behind closing the loop and techniques such as teach-back.[^s2] Second, treat feedback as information about the gap between a result and a goal; feedback with no stated goal gives the receiver nothing to correct against.[^s1] Third, avoid overcorrection: like the oscillating hand in the 1943 paper, a team that swings hard on every comment can end up further from target than one that adjusts in proportion.[^s1]

A workplace example. Before: a project lead emails a vendor a revised spec and marks the task done. After: the lead asks the vendor to reply with the two changes they will make first, and only closes the task when that reply matches the spec. Why: Wiener's point is that control depends on the returning signal, so the reply - not the sent email - tells the lead whether the message worked.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

Wiener's contribution is conceptual rather than empirical, so it is rated historical here. The engineering sense of feedback does not map neatly onto everyday speech: Lienhard notes that psychologists adopted "positive feedback" from Wiener's work but usually mean praise or reinforcement, whereas in control theory positive feedback amplifies an error and makes a system unstable.[^s3] His analogy between commanding people and commanding machines is also a simplification; the Macy group itself split in several directions rather than agreeing on how far machine models fit human groups.[^s4]

## Connections

Wiener **introduced feedback** (`cm:feedback`) as a general principle of purposeful behaviour in the 1943 paper **Behavior, Purpose and Teleology** (`cm:behavior-purpose-and-teleology`), co-written with **Arturo Rosenblueth** (`cm:arturo-rosenblueth`).[^s1] He **introduced cybernetics** (`cm:cybernetics`) and **authored The Human Use of Human Beings** (`cm:the-human-use-of-human-beings`), his account of communication and control in society.[^s3][^s2] Later work on workplace feedback, including the **Kluger and DeNisi 1996 meta-analysis** (`cm:kluger-denisi-1996`), **feedforward** (`cm:feedforward`) and **teach-back** (`cm:teach-back`), sits downstream of the goal-gap-correction loop he described; the engineer **Claude Shannon** (`cm:claude-shannon`) attended the Macy Conferences as a guest.[^s2][^s4]
