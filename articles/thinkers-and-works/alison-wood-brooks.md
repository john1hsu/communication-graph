# Alison Wood Brooks

Alison Wood Brooks is a behavioural scientist at Harvard Business School (HBS), where she holds the O'Brien Associate Professorship of Business Administration and studies the psychology of everyday conversation.[^s4][^s7] Her work matters to anyone who talks for a living because it turns familiar advice - ask questions, listen, lighten up - into claims that can be tested, and because she is the researcher most associated with the case for **follow-up questions**: questions that pick up on what the other person has just said.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Brooks earned an A.B. at Princeton in 2008 and a PhD at Wharton in 2013, and joined the HBS faculty the same year.[^s7] Her research covers how people choose and manage conversation topics, ask questions, use humour and listen.[^s7] She teaches an MBA course nicknamed TALK and chairs an HBS executive programme on communicating for impact.[^s4][^s7]

Her early public impact came from a study of performance nerves. Published in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* (online December 2013), it tested whether people facing a speech, a maths test or a karaoke song did better by telling themselves "I am excited" than by trying to calm down.[^s5] Because anxiety and excitement are both high-arousal states, she argued, relabelling the arousal is easier than suppressing it; in her experiments the "excited" groups gave speeches that raters judged more persuasive and confident and scored higher on the other tasks.[^s5] That study is the origin of the technique now called **anxiety reappraisal**.[^s5]

Her best-known line of work is on questions. With Karen Huang, Michael Yeomans, Julia Minson and Francesca Gino, she ran online chats and analysed speed-dating data in which people who asked more questions were liked more by their partners - yet the askers themselves did not expect asking to make them more likeable.[^s3] In the 2018 *Harvard Business Review* article "The Surprising Power of Questions", Brooks and Leslie John summarised the programme for managers: her research sorted conversational questions into introductory, mirror, full-switch and follow-up questions, and singled out follow-ups as especially powerful because they show you were listening.[^s1] That framing is how follow-up questions became a standard, research-backed recommendation in workplace communication advice.[^s1][^s2]

In January 2025 she published *Talk: The Science of Conversation and the Art of Being Ourselves*, organised around four maxims that spell TALK: choose and manage **t**opics, **a**sk more questions, use **l**evity, and practise **k**indness by attending to your partner's needs.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Brooks's findings give managers concrete, low-cost moves. Before an interview, a one-to-one or a networking event, she suggests setting an explicit goal - for example, ask at least five questions and follow up on the answers - because people who do not ask naturally tend to forget.[^s2] She argues that managers who skip questions out of indifference miss what colleagues, including junior ones, can teach them.[^s2] The *HBR* article adds practical guidance on sequencing (tough questions first in tense encounters, light ones first when building a relationship), on when closed questions beat open ones in negotiations, and on keeping a casual tone so people answer candidly.[^s1]

She is also clear about the boundaries. Asking a rapid series of questions without sharing anything about yourself can come across as guarded or invasive, and norms for questioning differ between workplaces.[^s2] In the original studies third-party observers did not prefer the askers, and the authors suspected the benefit could level off or reverse at very high question rates.[^s1][^s3]

## Applying her work

- **In one-to-ones:** listen for a detail in the answer and ask about it before moving to your own agenda.[^s1][^s2]
- **In job interviews:** treat your own questions as evidence of how you think and listen, not an afterthought.[^s1][^s2]
- **Before a talk:** try saying "I'm excited" and focusing on what could go well rather than on threats - as an experiment to try, not a guaranteed fix (see the evidence below).[^s5][^s6]

## Evidence and limits

The question-asking effect rests on short, getting-acquainted text chats and correlational speed-dating data; the authors note the field study cannot show causation and that other mechanisms were not ruled out.[^s3] How far the effect carries into status-laden workplace conversations is largely untested.[^s2][^s3]

The "get excited" finding has a weaker replication record. A 2021 study by Ganley and colleagues, which used Brooks's "try to get excited" prompt before a maths test (with procedural changes, so not a direct replication), found no reliable increase in excitement and no improvement in anxiety or performance compared with controls.[^s6] Treat anxiety reappraisal as promising but contested, which is how the graph rates it.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Brooks **introduced** (popularised) **follow-up questions** (`cm:follow-up-questions`) as a research-backed conversational habit, through the question-asking programme behind Huang et al. 2017 (`cm:huang-et-al-2017`) and her *HBR* synthesis.[^s1][^s3] She **introduced** the **TALK maxims** (`cm:talk-maxims`) and **authored** *Talk* (`cm:talk-the-science-of-conversation`).[^s4] She also introduced **anxiety reappraisal** (`cm:anxiety-reappraisal`) in Brooks 2014 (`cm:brooks-2014`).[^s5] Her work connects to **open-ended questions**, **job interviews**, **small talk**, **reciprocal self-disclosure** and **active listening**, the neighbouring skills her advice draws on.[^s1][^s2]
