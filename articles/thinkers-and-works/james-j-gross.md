# James J. Gross

James J. Gross is the Ernest R. Hilgard Professor of Psychology at Stanford University, where he directs the Stanford Psychophysiology Laboratory and the Stanford Center for Affective Science.[^s1] His research is on emotion regulation: how people influence which emotions they have, when they have them, and how they experience and express them, with a long-running focus on two strategies, reappraisal and suppression.[^s1][^s2] For anyone handling a tense meeting, a blunt client or critical feedback, his work supplies the core evidence that rethinking a situation before you respond tends to serve a conversation better than masking the feeling once it has fired.[^s3][^s5]

## Origin

Gross studied philosophy and psychology at Yale (BA 1987), spent a year as a visiting graduate student at Oxford, and took a PhD in clinical psychology at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1993 before joining Stanford.[^s1] In a 1998 review in *Review of General Psychology* he argued that emotion regulation had grown out of earlier work on psychoanalytic defences and on stress and coping, notably Lazarus and Folkman's coping framework, into a field of its own.[^s2] He separated it from coping by noting that it covers making emotions stronger as well as weaker, does not assume a strategy is good or bad, and looks at specific emotions rather than general stress.[^s2]

The same review set out what became known as the process model. It places regulation at five points as an emotion unfolds: choosing which situations to enter, changing the situation, steering attention, changing what the situation means, and modulating the response once it has started.[^s2] The first four act before the emotion has fully formed (antecedent-focused); the last acts after (response-focused).[^s2] Reappraisal, transforming how you read a situation so that it has a different emotional impact, is his central example of cognitive change.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Gross's 1998 experiment in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* turned the model into a testable contrast. 120 undergraduates watched a disgust-eliciting medical film after being told either to view it objectively (reappraisal), to hide their feelings (suppression) or simply to watch.[^s3] Both strategies reduced visible disgust, but only reappraisal reduced how disgusted people felt; suppression left the feeling intact and raised sympathetic nervous system activation.[^s3]

With Oliver John he then built the 10-item Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, with six reappraisal and four suppression items, and tested it across samples totalling more than 1,400 students in the first study alone.[^s4] Habitual reappraisers reported more positive and less negative emotion and were rated by peers as closer and better liked; habitual suppressors shared less emotion, received less social support and reported lower well-being, partly because suppression felt inauthentic.[^s4]

The finding most directly about communication came from Emily Butler, Gross and colleagues in 2003. When one partner in a conversation about an upsetting topic hid her feelings, the exchange was disrupted, rapport fell and the other partner's blood pressure rose; partners of people who reappraised did not show these costs.[^s5]

## Applying his work at work

Three habits follow from his findings. Intervene early: before a meeting you expect to be difficult, decide how you will read the predictable trigger, because antecedent-focused strategies act before the emotion takes hold.[^s2] Reframe instead of masking: a fixed smile over unchanged irritation still costs you physiologically and can leave the other person tenser.[^s3][^s5] Pick from the whole menu: sometimes the better move is to change the situation, for instance by moving a charged discussion out of a large meeting, not to rethink it.[^s2]

A workplace illustration: a product manager is told in a review that her launch plan is naive. Suppressing the sting keeps her face neutral but leaves her clipped and distracted for the rest of the meeting. Reading the comment as a reviewer worried about a risk she has not yet explained lets her ask which risk, which answers the challenge and keeps the room working.[^s3][^s5]

## Limits

His landmark studies used mostly undergraduate samples, laboratory film tasks and short conversations between strangers, so effect sizes in real workplaces may differ.[^s3][^s4][^s5] The questionnaire findings are correlational and do not show that reappraisal causes better relationships.[^s4] Bonanno and Burton argue that no strategy is uniformly healthy or harmful: reappraisal can backfire when a problem is controllable and should be acted on, and brief suppression can be the right call, so the skill is choosing flexibly for the situation.[^s6] Popular summaries that cast reappraisal as always good and suppression as always bad go beyond what this research shows.[^s6]

## Connections

Gross **introduced** the process model of emotion regulation (`cm:process-model-of-emotion-regulation`) in his 1998 integrative review.[^s2] He **introduced** cognitive reappraisal (`cm:cognitive-reappraisal`) as a defined, experimentally tested regulation strategy contrasted with expressive suppression (`cm:expressive-suppression`).[^s3] He **authored**, with Oliver John, the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (`cm:emotion-regulation-questionnaire`).[^s4] His framing of the field was **influenced by** Richard Lazarus's stress-and-coping research (`cm:richard-lazarus`).[^s2] He co-authored the Butler et al. study on the social costs of suppression (`cm:butler-et-al-2003`), and the regulatory-flexibility critique bears on how far his strategy contrasts generalise, as does Troy and colleagues' work on when reappraisal helps (`cm:troy-shallcross-mauss-2013`).[^s5][^s6]
