# Carl Rogers

Carl Rogers (1902-1987) was an American psychologist who originated the nondirective, or client-centred, approach to counselling, in which an understanding and accepting relationship, rather than the expert's advice, is what helps a person change.[^s1] Most of what workplace training now calls empathic or active listening traces back to him: listening for feelings as well as facts, checking your understanding with the speaker, and holding back judgement while you do it.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Rogers was born in Oak Park, Illinois, left Union Theological Seminary for psychology, and took his MA and PhD at Columbia University's Teachers College in 1928 and 1931.[^s1] He spent the 1930s in child-guidance work in Rochester, New York, became professor of clinical psychology at Ohio State in 1940, and from 1945 to 1957 taught at the University of Chicago, where he helped set up the university's counselling centre; he later taught at Wisconsin and in 1963 helped found the Center for Studies of the Person in La Jolla, California.[^s1] His books include *Counseling and Psychotherapy* (1942), *Client-Centered Therapy* (1951) and *On Becoming a Person* (1961).[^s1]

His listening method did not arrive fully formed. Rogers recalled that a colleague with Rankian training urged him to listen for the feelings behind a client's words and reflect them back, and that later study of recorded interviews showed how much more than a technique this was.[^s2] In his 1957 statement of the conditions for constructive change, he named the helper's genuineness (congruence), unconditional positive regard and empathic understanding, perceived at least minimally by the client, as the active ingredients, and he argued they applied to relationships generally, not only to therapy.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Rogers gave managers a diagnosis and a remedy. The diagnosis, in his essay "Barriers and Gateways to Communication", is that the main block to mutual understanding is our reflex to judge, approve or disapprove of what the other person says, and that this reflex is strongest exactly when feelings run high.[^s4] The remedy, set out with Richard Farson in a 1957 paper for the University of Chicago's Industrial Relations Center, is active listening: grasp the speaker's total meaning, including the feelings, avoid advice, judgement and reassurance, and test your understanding by restating it until the speaker agrees you have it.[^s3] They argued that people who feel listened to become less defensive and more willing to look at their own views, and that a listening manager tends to be listened to in return.[^s3]

A workplace example: a team lead tells her manager the new reporting rules are "pointless busywork". The evaluating reply is "They're required, so let's not debate it." The Rogerian reply is "You're spending hours on reports that don't seem to change anything, and that's eating into client work?" Only once she confirms that summary does the manager explain the rule or ask what would help. Rogers described empathy in this sense as temporarily entering the other person's world without judging it while staying secure in your own, and checking your sense of it with them as you go.[^s2]

## Limits

Rogers complained that his approach was caricatured as parroting the speaker's last words, and he stressed that empathy is an attitude, not a formula; a reflected phrase delivered without real interest reads as a technique.[^s2] His 1957 claim that the core conditions are both necessary and sufficient for change was bold. The best later evidence supports a weaker version: a 2018 meta-analysis of 82 samples and 6,138 clients found therapist empathy a moderately strong predictor of therapy outcome (r = .28), with effects that varied considerably and data its authors described as limited.[^s5] That is good support for empathy mattering, not proof that it is sufficient, and the evidence comes from therapy rather than from offices.[^s5] Rogers himself reported that therapists' self-ratings of their empathy agreed poorly with clients' and observers' ratings, a useful warning for any manager who believes they already listen well.[^s2]

## Connections

Rogers is credited with **introducing empathy** as the central quality of a helping relationship, through the empathic understanding condition of his 1957 model, and with **introducing unconditional positive regard** in the same model.[^s6] With Farson he **introduced active listening** for supervisors and managers.[^s3] He **authored *On Becoming a Person***, his best-known book, and **authored "Barriers and Gateways to Communication"**, which names the judging reflex as the main barrier.[^s1][^s4] He is related to **paraphrasing** and reflective listening, the reflection practice he learned and then warned against mechanising,[^s2] to **defensiveness**, which active listening is meant to lower,[^s3] and to **Elliott et al. 2018**, the meta-analysis that tests his empathy claim.[^s5] He is also related to **Thomas Gordon** and **Weger et al. 2014**, whose own articles in this graph record his influence on their listening work, and to the **listening circle** and **one-on-one meetings**, where his listen-before-you-judge stance is put to use.[^s3]
