# Joseph M. Williams

Joseph M. Williams (1933-2008) was a professor of English and linguistics at the University of Chicago who changed how professionals are taught to revise. Instead of asking what was wrong with a writer, he asked what a reader would need on the page to understand a text, and he argued that even abstract prose hides a story of characters doing actions.[^s1] His book *Style: Ten Lessons in Clarity and Grace* (1981), now *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* with Joseph Bizup, turned that idea into a set of diagnostics that editors, lawyers, scientists and managers still use.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Williams was born in Cleveland, studied at Miami University in Ohio and took a Ph.D. in English and linguistics at the University of Wisconsin-Madison in 1966; he joined the Chicago faculty in 1965 and taught there until he retired in 1999.[^s1] He began as a historian and grammarian of English, but in the 1970s the American Medical Association asked him to build a writing programme for doctors. Reading medical journals, he catalogued the style he called "medicalese" and concluded that the fix lay in how readers process sentences, not in more rules.[^s1] With Gregory Colomb he drew on cognitive science, decision theory and attribution theory to predict how readers respond to features of a text.[^s1]

In the same years he tested what graders actually reward. In an article in the *Journal of Basic Writing* he reported work with Rosemary Hake in which English teachers, given essays that differed only in style, mostly graded the noun-heavy versions higher, even though they said they would not teach that style; memos that administrators rated poorly also carried more nominalizations.[^s4] He used this to argue that the profession's style standards had been drawn from editors and academics rather than from the readers in government and business who had to act on the text.[^s4]

## Main ideas and works

*Style* differed from Fowler's and Strunk and White's guides by speaking to writers already in their careers and by working from the reader's side.[^s1] Its current table of contents shows the method: after a lesson on correctness, the clarity section covers actions, characters, cohesion and coherence, and emphasis; later lessons frame documents and sections, then treat concision, shape and elegance, and the book ends on the ethics of clarity.[^s2] The core diagnostic is to find the characters and the actions in a sentence and make them its subjects and verbs, which is the origin of the "characters as subjects, actions as verbs" technique and of the modern treatment of nominalization as a style fault.[^s1][^s2]

From the late 1970s Williams, Colomb and Frank Kinahan also built what became the Little Red Schoolhouse, a Chicago course launched in 1981 that combined a process approach (the stages skilled writers follow) with a formal one (how features on the page trigger predictable reader responses). More than 10,000 Chicago students took it, and it spread to the business and law schools.[^s1] He co-wrote *The Craft of Research* with Wayne C. Booth and Colomb,[^s3] helped rewrite Turabian's manual for research writers, and co-founded a consultancy that trained law firms, agencies and corporations.[^s1] His colleague Lawrence McEnerney credited him with seeing that experts face writing problems created by their own expertise, which schoolroom rules do not address.[^s1]

## Why it matters

For workplace writers, Williams supplies the "why" behind most clarity advice. A rule such as "avoid nominalizations" is easy to apply mechanically and easy to dismiss; his framing asks instead who the reader thinks the story is about and whether the sentence tells it in that order.[^s1][^s2] That makes his method a fit for the sentence pass of a structured edit, after purpose and structure are settled. A practical example: a project lead rewrites "There was a failure in the implementation of the approval process by the vendor" by asking who did what, and gets "The vendor did not implement the approval process", which names the actor a manager needs to call.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

Williams' own evidence shows that readers and graders often reward the opposite of what they claim to value, but the studies he reported were small and preliminary, and he said so himself.[^s4] Whether his sentence rules improve comprehension has had mixed tests. A 2018 eye-tracking study of 27 Danish students reading English as a second language, which cited *Style* among the guides giving such advice, found no reliable reading benefit for revisions that replaced nominalizations, passives and reduced clauses; sentence length mattered, and the author suggested that cohesion between sentences may matter more.[^s5] That result fits Williams' own emphasis on cohesion and on the whole reader experience rather than single-rule edits.[^s2][^s5] Some Chicago colleagues criticised his formal approach as rigid; he replied that formal patterns mark all good writing and help generate thought.[^s1]

## Connections

- **Authored** *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* (`cm:style-lessons-in-clarity-and-grace`), first published in 1981 and now in its 13th edition with Joseph Bizup.[^s1][^s2]
- **Authored** *The Craft of Research* (`cm:the-craft-of-research`) with Wayne C. Booth and Gregory G. Colomb.[^s3]
- **Introduced** reader-centred **editing for clarity** (`cm:editing-for-clarity`): his question of what a reader needs to understand a text, taught through *Style* and the Little Red Schoolhouse, underlies the sentence-level revision passes taught today.[^s1][^s2]
- **Challenged by** Balling 2018 (`cm:balling-2018`), which found no comprehension benefit for second-language readers from the construction-level advice that *Style* and similar guides give.[^s5]
- **Related:** his diagnostics are the source of characters as subjects, actions as verbs and of the nominalization critique; they serve concise writing and clarity, sit alongside Lanham's paramedic method and plain-language guidance, and target the curse of knowledge that experts bring to their own prose. He positioned *Style* against William Strunk Jr.'s rule-based approach.[^s1][^s2][^s4]
