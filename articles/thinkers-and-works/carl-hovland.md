# Carl Hovland

Carl Iver Hovland (1912-1961) was a Yale experimental psychologist who did more than anyone to turn persuasion from rhetorical lore into a laboratory science.[^s1] Born in Chicago, he studied at Northwestern and took his Yale PhD in 1936 under the learning theorist Clark Hull. He became chair of Yale's psychology department at about 33 and a Sterling Professor in 1947.[^s1] For anyone who has to win support at work, his legacy is a set of tested questions: who is saying this, how is the case ordered, does it admit the other side, and how will its effect change over time?[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Hovland's persuasion research began in the Second World War. On leave from Yale from 1942 to 1945, he ran the Experimental Section of the War Department's Information and Education Division, testing whether training and orientation films actually changed soldiers' knowledge and opinions. The results appeared in *Experiments on Mass Communication* (1949), with Arthur Lumsdaine and Fred Sheffield.[^s1] Back at Yale he founded the Communication and Attitude Change Program. The Rockefeller Foundation funded it for over fifteen years, and more than thirty colleagues and students worked in it. Its main books were *Communication and Persuasion* (1953, with Irving Janis and Harold Kelley), *The Order of Presentation in Persuasion* (1957) and *Personality and Persuasibility* (1959).[^s1] In his last years he moved on to concept learning and early computer simulation of thinking. He died in 1961, aged 48.[^s1]

## Key ideas

The Yale programme broke persuasion into variables that could be tested one at a time. These included the credibility of the source, the order in which arguments are presented, whether a message covers one side or both, the audience's self-esteem, and the use of fear appeals.[^s1]

**Source credibility.** In the best-known study, Hovland and Walter Weiss gave Yale students identical articles credited either to a trusted source (a medical journal, a planning board) or a distrusted one. Students learned the facts equally well from both. But they shifted their opinions much more for the trusted source: a net 23% change against about 7%.[^s2] The Yale group went on to define credibility as two separate perceptions. **Expertise** is whether the source can give valid information. **Trustworthiness** is whether the source seems to be informing rather than persuading for its own gain.[^s4] That split still underlies how communicators think about credibility.

**The sleeper effect.** Four weeks after the Hovland-Weiss study, the trusted source's advantage had faded: its effect fell while the distrusted source's rose.[^s2] The wartime film studies had first shown this delayed persuasion. Hovland and Weiss first suggested that people simply forget the source. When their recall data showed the source was still remembered, they proposed instead that the source becomes dissociated from the message in memory.[^s3]

**Social judgment.** With Muzafer Sherif, Hovland studied how people judge a message against their own position. They described ranges of acceptable and rejected positions, so the same message can move one audience and push another away.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Hovland's findings still apply at work. People can understand a message without believing it, and belief depends heavily on who delivers it.[^s2] A strong analyst whose proposal comes through a vendor with a stake in the outcome can lose trust on motive even when the expertise is real.[^s4] Pairing expertise with visibly disinterested motives makes for the most credible case.[^s4] A practical example: a team lead who wants a new tool approved does better asking a neutral platform engineer to present the evaluation than having the vendor's account manager do it. The facts are the same, but the second messenger is easily dismissed as self-interested.[^s4]

## Evidence and limits

Much of the early Yale work rested on small student samples. In Hovland and Weiss, only 61 of 223 students attended all three sessions.[^s2] The sleeper effect proved fragile. Reviews in the 1970s called it unreliable, and some suggested researchers would do better to accept the null hypothesis. A 2004 meta-analysis of 72 data sets found the effect does occur, but mainly when both the message and the discounting cue make a strong first impression, when people think hard about the message, and when the cue comes after the message.[^s3] Later work by Richard Petty and John Cacioppo broadened the Yale models by adding differences between individual audience members. Recent work also argues that online cues like popularity and repetition now often substitute for expertise and trustworthiness.[^s4] Treat Hovland's results as durable starting points, not fixed laws.

## Connections

Hovland's group gave the modern concept of **credibility** its expertise-and-trustworthiness form,[^s4] demonstrated in the Hovland-Weiss study.[^s2] He introduced the **sleeper effect**[^s3] and, with Sherif, **social judgment theory**.[^s4] He co-wrote *Communication and Persuasion*, the Yale programme's flagship book,[^s1] and his training under Clark Hull shaped its learning-based view of attitude change.[^s1] Petty and Cacioppo's later persuasion models, the source of the **elaboration likelihood model**, extended the Yale agenda.[^s4]
