# Judith A. Hall

Judith A. Hall is an American social psychologist, University Distinguished Professor Emeritus of Psychology at Northeastern University, whose career has centred on nonverbal communication.[^s1] Her best-known contribution is treating the ability to read other people correctly, which she and colleagues call interpersonal accuracy, as something that can be measured, compared across groups and studied for its causes and payoffs.[^s1][^s3] For anyone at work who relies on "reading the room", her research is a reminder that such reads can be tested, and that they are less reliable than people tend to assume.

## Origin

Hall took her BA at Harvard College in 1967 and her PhD at Harvard in 1976, taught at Johns Hopkins University from 1976 to 1980, and joined Northeastern in 1986, retiring in 2020.[^s1] Her faculty profile lists four research threads: accurate perception of others' states and traits (including remembering how people looked and behaved); gender differences in nonverbal accuracy, expressiveness and behaviour; how status and power relate to nonverbal skill; and verbal and nonverbal behaviour in medical visits.[^s1]

## Key ideas

**Decoding is a measurable skill.** Hall's work relies on performance tests rather than self-report: people watch or hear short clips and say what the person was feeling or doing, and their answers are scored against a known answer.[^s4] A typical example is the Profile of Nonverbal Sensitivity (PONS), built by Robert Rosenthal's group in 1979, in which viewers judge situations from two-second clips of face and body or from speech whose words have been masked.[^s4] Rosip and Hall found that knowing more about what cues mean was only weakly linked to how accurately people decoded them, and that people's self-ratings of their nonverbal knowledge predicted little.[^s4]

**Women decode slightly better, on average.** Hall's 1978 *Psychological Bulletin* review of 75 studies was an early meta-analysis in the area. It found that women outperformed men in judging nonverbal cues by an average of about 0.40 standard deviations, with gender explaining under 4% of the variation in scores.[^s2] She called the difference moderate rather than large.[^s2] Her 2004 work with Janelle Rosip found that the gap held even after measuring how much people knew about cues, so better knowledge does not explain it.[^s4]

**Gathering the field.** In 2016 Hall, Marianne Schmid Mast and Tessa West edited *The Social Psychology of Perceiving Others Accurately* for Cambridge University Press, which brings together research on how accurate people are, what accuracy is linked to and whether it can be improved.[^s3] With Mark Knapp and Terrence Horgan she co-wrote *Nonverbal Communication in Human Interaction*, an interdisciplinary textbook covering gesture, touch, the face, eye behaviour, the voice and nonverbal messages in daily life.[^s6]

## Why it matters

Hall's framing turns a vague talent into a skill you can check. Imagine a team lead who reads a new hire's short replies on a video call as disengagement. On Hall's view, that read is a hypothesis to test, and there is evidence about how often hypotheses like it turn out right.[^s4][^s3] Her finding that knowing more about cues does little to improve accuracy also explains why reading a list of "body language tells" rarely helps. A better habit is to check the read directly: ask a question, or offer a tentative label and watch the response.[^s4] Her studies of doctor-patient visits carry the same point into client-facing work, where both what is said and how it is said affect the outcome.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

Hall's own reviews spell out their limits. The 1978 meta-analysis included only published or submitted studies, could not rule out selective reporting, and noted that gender cannot be experimentally assigned, so socialisation or personality could explain the gap.[^s2] Later work suggests the female advantage is real but small and depends on the channel and the task. In a 2018 study of 290 German listeners judging vocal emotions, women were more accurate overall (d about 0.31), but overall differences were no longer significant after correcting for multiple tests, and the pattern varied by emotion and type of stimulus.[^s5] Treat group differences as averages that say little about any one colleague, and treat lab decoding scores as a weaker guide to real conversations than the tests' precision suggests.[^s5][^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced** interpersonal accuracy as an organised research area. She did not invent the study of reading others, but she was a leading contributor to its measurement research and co-edited the 2016 volume that defined the field.[^s3][^s1]
- **Authored** *The Social Psychology of Perceiving Others Accurately* (as co-editor, 2016).[^s3]
- **Authored** *Nonverbal Communication in Human Interaction* with Knapp and Horgan.[^s6]
- **Related:** nonverbal communication, facial expressions and paralanguage, the channels her tests sample; the myth of reading lies from body language, which her knowledge-versus-accuracy findings help explain; medical appointments, where she studied doctor-patient communication; and Michael Argyle, an earlier pioneer of nonverbal research.[^s4][^s1][^s6]
