# Blowing the Whistle on Troubled Software Projects

"Blowing the Whistle on Troubled Software Projects" is a short article by Mark Keil and Daniel Robey, published in *Communications of the ACM* in April 2001 (volume 44, issue 4, pages 87-93).[^s6][^s1] It asks why organisations keep funding software projects that insiders already know are in trouble. Its answer is a two-sided communication failure. The people who see the problem often stay quiet, and when they do speak up, the executives who could act often do not listen.[^s1] Later researchers credit the article with coining the term *deaf effect* for that second failure.[^s3]

## Origin

Keil, a Georgia State University professor whose research covers IT project escalation, risk and status reporting, drew the article from a study of internal and external information-systems (IS) auditors.[^s4][^s1] An earlier 1998 manuscript by Keil describes the fieldwork: 42 telephone interviews with auditors drawn from respondents to a 1995 mail survey, each asked about a troubled project they knew, with about 30 interviews covering whistle-blowing.[^s5] The same interview set fed Keil and Robey's 1999 *Journal of Management Information Systems* paper on how troubled projects are turned around, which found de-escalation was usually triggered by senior managers, internal auditors or outside consultants.[^s2]

The CACM article organises its findings under two headings: the mum effect, a reluctance to blow the whistle, and the deaf effect, a reluctance to hear it.[^s1] The mum effect was already well documented in social psychology; the auditors' accounts added career fear, money and family obligations, and reporting lines that tied them to the project they were judging.[^s5] The 1998 manuscript already used the label "deaf effect" for executives who did not respond to bad news, and gave no outside source for it. It used the London Stock Exchange's Taurus project to show decision makers clinging to a failing system.[^s5][^s1] A later study states plainly that Keil and Robey coined the term in their study of IS auditors.[^s3]

## Why it matters

The article's main communication lesson is that getting people to speak up is necessary but not enough. Overcoming the mum effect does not redirect a failing project unless the receivers are also willing to hear and act on bad news.[^s5][^s1] That framing moved attention from the courage of the messenger to the listening habits of leaders, and opened a line of experiments on why decision makers ignore warnings.[^s3]

It also gives practical, still-usable advice for anyone who reports on risky work:

- **For senior executives:** keep audit and review staff independent of the people running the projects, give them enough size and backing that they cannot easily be ignored, and treat bad news as feedback rather than disloyalty.[^s1]
- **For IS professionals and reviewers:** present yourself as a team player who wants the project to succeed rather than as a policeman, while keeping your independence.[^s1][^s5]
- **On going over someone's head:** the article calls this organisational leapfrogging. It sometimes worked, as when an audit team went past a vice president who dismissed its warnings, but in one case an auditor who escalated as far as possible was let go.[^s1][^s5]

Climates differed sharply between organisations. Receptive ones had larger audit staffs, senior support for audit, good relationships between audit and business units, and a control-conscious culture.[^s5][^s1]

## Evidence and limits

This is an exploratory, interview-based study of one profession, reported in a practitioner magazine. The CACM version gives no counts of how many auditors stayed silent, so its patterns are illustrations rather than measured effects.[^s1] The interviews were retrospective accounts of a single project per auditor.[^s5] The deaf effect was later tested in role-play laboratory experiments, the first with 105 undergraduate students playing project leaders, so how far its findings transfer to real executives is still open.[^s3] Hence `historical`: the article is valued as the origin of a concept rather than as evidence for an effect size.

## Connections

- **Authored by** Mark Keil and Daniel Robey.[^s6]
- **Introduces** the deaf effect, the receiver-side failure to hear, heed or act on a warning; later work credits this article with the term.[^s3][^s5]
- **Influenced by** the mum effect, the reluctance to pass on bad news, which the article takes from earlier social psychology and pairs with the deaf effect.[^s1][^s5]
- **Applied in** status reporting: the article is about how bad news on a project's status reaches, or fails to reach, the executives who can act.[^s1]
- **Broader:** upward communication, the flow of information from staff to decision makers.[^s1]
- **Related:** employee silence and psychological safety describe the climate that makes auditors stay quiet;[^s5] escalation communication and delivering bad news are the sender's practices the article discusses.[^s1][^s2]
