# William Strunk Jr.

William Strunk Jr. (1869-1946) was a professor of English at Cornell University and the author of *The Elements of Style*, a short handbook that set out, in brief form, what he saw as the main requirements of plain English.[^s1] Its numbered rules - including the instruction to use the active voice and to omit needless words - became, through a later revision by his student E. B. White, the most widely known set of writing rules in American offices and classrooms.[^s1][^s2] For workplace writers he matters as the source of habits now repeated in style guides and plain-language programmes, and as a reminder of how far short rules can travel beyond their original nuance.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Strunk earned his Ph.D. at Cornell in 1896 and taught there for his whole career, from 1899 to 1937.[^s2] He first printed *The Elements of Style* privately in 1918 as a 43-page text for his own English classes; Cornell's library still holds a 1918 privately printed copy among his papers, alongside his doctoral thesis.[^s2][^s4] A commercial edition followed from Harcourt, Brace and Howe in 1920.[^s1] The book deliberately covered only a small part of style: a few punctuation rules, the usage errors students most often made, and eleven principles of composition, numbered so that instructors could cite a rule by its number when marking a manuscript.[^s1]

E. B. White, a member of Cornell's Class of 1921, took Strunk's course; in a 1957 *New Yorker* column he praised the "little book" and said he had been trying to omit needless words since 1919.[^s2] Macmillan then asked White to update and expand it, and the Strunk and White edition appeared on 16 April 1959, reached the *New York Times* best-seller list that August and has since sold more than 10 million copies.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Strunk's composition rules read like a checklist for clear business writing. He told writers to make the paragraph the unit of composition and open it with a topic sentence, to put statements in positive form, to prefer definite, specific and concrete language, and to keep related words together.[^s1] His rule on voice argued that the active voice is usually more direct, vigorous and concise than the passive.[^s1] His best-known rule asked that every word do useful work: "Omit needless words."[^s1] Strunk was explicit that this did not mean writing only short sentences or leaving out detail; it meant cutting phrases such as *the question as to whether* down to *whether*.[^s1]

## Using his rules at work

- **Treat the rules as an editing pass, not a drafting method.** Strunk wrote the rules as reference points for correcting drafts, which suits a final review of an email or report.[^s1]
- **Read the caveats.** His active-voice rule says the passive is often convenient and sometimes necessary, and that the choice depends on which word should be the subject; he did not ban it.[^s1]
- **Cut padding, keep substance.** Before sending a status update, strike stock phrases (*the fact that*, *in a hasty manner*) but keep the dates, owners and figures the reader needs.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The rules are one teacher's judgement, not findings from studies of readers, so the status here is `historical`.[^s1] Linguist Geoffrey Pullum, writing for the book's 50th anniversary, accepted much of its general style advice as harmless but called the authors "grammatical incompetents" on grammar.[^s3] He granted that the book itself counsels moderation on the passive, yet argued that in the Strunk and White edition three of the four sentences offered as passives are not passives at all, and that tutors influenced by it came to mark every passive as an error.[^s3] For practitioners, the lesson is to keep Strunk's aim of clear, economical prose while checking any mechanical rule against what the sentence actually needs to do.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

- **Authored** *The Elements of Style*, first privately printed in 1918 and published commercially in 1920.[^s1][^s2]
- **Introduced active voice** as a writing rule: Rule 10 of his handbook, later echoed in plain-language and Army writing guidance (see those articles for their sources).[^s1]
- **Introduced concise writing** in its best-known form, the omit-needless-words commandment that Cornell describes as his.[^s2]
- **Influenced E. B. White,** his former student, who credited Strunk with his habit of cutting words and revised the book in 1959.[^s2]
- **Challenged by** Pullum's "50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice", which faults the book's grammatical analysis and its effect on writing instruction.[^s3]
