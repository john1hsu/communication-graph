# The Network Nation

*The Network Nation: Human Communication via Computer* is a 1978 book by sociologist Starr Roxanne Hiltz and computer scientist Murray Turoff, published by Addison-Wesley in a 588-page first edition and reissued in a revised edition by the MIT Press in 1993.[^s5][^s4] It was one of the earliest books about people using computer networks, then called computer conferencing, to socialise, gather and organise, and it is the work the graph credits with framing digital, computer-mediated communication as a subject of study in its own right.[^s3][^s1]

## Origin

The book came out of a working partnership: Turoff had built EMISARI, the first computer conferencing system, and Hiltz, a sociologist, has said her research depended on his systems.[^s1] Hiltz supplied the social-science lens on how people actually used such systems, and the resulting book combined technical description of networks with what historians describe as the earliest sociology of a community whose members met and collaborated only online.[^s1][^s3] Historians of the field treat it as a founding reference for computer-mediated communication.[^s1][^s2][^s3]

## Why it matters

Most of the problems knowledge workers now manage in email, chat and shared documents were named here, a dozen years before the World Wide Web.[^s3] Historians of computing single out its foresight about flaming, privacy, anonymity, telecommuting and online political organising.[^s1] Its authors imagined everyday life and work running through networked conferencing and messaging, and they took the accompanying risks to privacy and identity seriously from the start.[^s1][^s2][^s3] The revised 1993 edition kept a chapter on the societal impacts of computerized conferencing, signalling that the authors saw the social effects, not the hardware, as the core subject.[^s4]

For a practitioner, the book is a reminder that the trade-offs of digital work are old and well studied rather than new side effects of recent tools. Hiltz and Turoff's own later research on group systems spells out the lessons that grew from this programme: asynchronous discussion lets anyone raise an idea at any time and allows more parallel contributions than a meeting, but latecomers struggle to see how a thread reached its current state.[^s6] Structured project groups online tend toward more equal participation, while open forums show a few voices dominating, and information overload sets in once a discussion grows past roughly a hundred active members.[^s6]

## Using it today

Three habits follow directly from that line of work. First, when a discussion moves to an asynchronous channel, write a short running summary at the top so people joining later can see what has been decided and why.[^s6] Second, give online working groups a defined task and structure, since structure rather than the medium is what spreads participation.[^s6] Third, size channels deliberately: split or moderate a forum before it reaches the scale at which members stop reading.[^s6]

## Limits

The book is a historical work, not a tested set of techniques. Its observations drew on the early users of research conferencing systems, a small and unusual population rather than a typical workforce.[^s1][^s2] Its reputation rests largely on how much of its forecast later came true, which is a retrospective judgment by historians rather than a measure of the original study's rigour.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

*The Network Nation* was **authored by Murray Turoff** (`cm:murray-turoff`) and **Starr Roxanne Hiltz** (`cm:starr-roxanne-hiltz`), as the MIT Press record of the revised edition shows.[^s4] It **introduces digital communication** (`cm:digital-communication`) as a field of social research, because it was the first book-length sociology of a community that existed only through a computer network.[^s1] It sits beside **computer conferencing** (`cm:computer-conferencing`), the technology it studied, and **asynchronous communication** (`cm:asynchronous-communication`), whose benefits and costs its authors' later research described.[^s1][^s6] It relates to **information overload** (`cm:information-overload`) and **unequal participation** (`cm:unequal-participation`), two problems that line of research measured in online groups.[^s6]
