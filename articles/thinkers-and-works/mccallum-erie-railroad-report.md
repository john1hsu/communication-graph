# Erie Railroad Superintendent's Report (1856)

The Erie Railroad Superintendent's Report is the report Daniel C. McCallum wrote as general superintendent of the New York and Erie Railroad, setting out how a large, spread-out company should divide responsibility and how information should travel back to the people in charge.[^s2] An 1857 engineering handbook reprinted its management sections, crediting McCallum's report to stockholders of 25 March 1856, and summarised his six principles: divide responsibilities clearly, give officers authority to match them, have a way to check that duties are done, report failures promptly, run a system of daily reports and checks, and make the whole system show who is at fault.[^s1] Business historian Alfred Chandler, citing it from the Erie's 1855 annual reports, describes it as one of the founding statements of modern American business administration.[^s2]

## Origin

McCallum, a self-taught engineer, became general superintendent of the Erie in 1854, when it was one of the longest railroads in the world at nearly 500 miles.[^s2][^s4] He argued that methods that let one superintendent watch a 50-mile road in person fail on a road ten times as long, so the long road needs a formal system instead of personal oversight.[^s2] He paired the report with a large printed organization diagram, drawn from the company's returns of September 1855, that showed functional offices branching from the general superintendent's office.[^s5] Henry Varnum Poor's *American Railroad Journal* publicised the system, a British parliamentary report and New York state railroad commissioners described it, and the Pennsylvania Railroad's 1857 organization plan built closely on it.[^s2]

## What the report set up

The report's lasting contribution to workplace communication is a schedule of routine reports, each with a defined sender, recipient and content.[^s2]

- **Hourly:** telegraphed reports of each train's position and the cause of any delay, tabulated as they came in and filed as a record.[^s2]
- **Daily:** reports from conductors and station agents on trains and traffic, arranged so two sources could check each other.[^s2]
- **Monthly:** reports from division superintendents and department heads on costs and work done, which fed statistics that let the head office compare divisions.[^s2][^s4]

Subordinates reported to their immediate superior, with the daily reports to the general superintendent as a deliberate exception.[^s2] The handbook's reprint adds standing registers such as weekly engine returns and monthly engine-performance reports, and McCallum's claim that a good system gives the head of operations a complete daily history of details.[^s1] Rosenthal stresses that the problem McCallum faced was not too little data, since the telegraph brought plenty, but turning it into measures such as cost per ton-mile that the board could use.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Communication historian JoAnne Yates shows that railroads were among the first enterprises to need written internal communication, and that routine reports sent up the hierarchy on a fixed schedule, in formats ranging from printed forms to long prose reports with tables, became the chief management tool of the departmentalised company.[^s3] Chandler concludes that the departments, lines of authority and flows of data set up by McCallum and a few other railroad engineers in the 1850s became the base on which later corporate management was built.[^s2] Read today, it is an ancestor of the status report and the monthly management pack, which still answer McCallum's question of how a manager who cannot see the work can still know what is happening.[^s2][^s3]

Three lessons still apply when you design a reporting routine. Decide what decision each report serves, as McCallum did when he built rates and comparisons from operating data.[^s2] Summarise as information rises: Yates found that by the time reports reached the board they were screened down to what policy decisions needed.[^s3] And watch the cost to the people filling them in: plant superintendents at Du Pont later mocked hourly operating reports with hundreds of blanks per form.[^s3]

## Evidence and limits

This is a historical work, not a tested method. Chandler's account rests largely on the Erie's own reports, and he notes McCallum paid little attention to capital accounting and depreciation, and that the Erie later fell to financiers he calls unscrupulous, after which McCallum turned to bridge-building.[^s2] Its link to the modern business report is one of lineage rather than direct invention: Yates traces a gradual shift from occasional letters to regular forms and reports across many firms into the early twentieth century.[^s3]

## Connections

The report was **authored by** Daniel C. McCallum, credited by name in the 1857 reprint.[^s1] It **introduces** systematic upward communication, the routine flow of reports from operating staff to managers that Chandler describes in detail.[^s2] It is an early, low-confidence origin for the **business report**: Chandler credits it with the data flows later management built on, while Yates shows the report genre maturing over the following decades.[^s2][^s3] Project status reporting, escalating a problem and information overload are modern descendants and side effects of the same reporting system.[^s3][^s4]
