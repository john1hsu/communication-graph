# Elizabeth Wolfe Morrison

Elizabeth Wolfe Morrison is a professor of management and organizations at New York University's Stern School of Business whose research asks why employees so often keep quiet about problems they can see, and how organizations can build climates that welcome their input.[^s1] With her Stern colleague Frances Milliken she gave the workplace a name for that pattern, organizational silence, and much of today's practical advice on encouraging people to speak up rests on the research programme that followed.[^s2][^s6]

## Origin

Morrison studied psychology at Brown University, took a PhD in organization behavior at Northwestern University, and joined NYU Stern in 1990; she has since held the ITT Harold Geneen chair, served as vice dean of faculty and of executive programs, and been an associate editor of the *Academy of Management Journal* and *Academy of Management Annals*.[^s1] She was named an Academy of Management Fellow in 2024.[^s1]

In their 2000 *Academy of Management Review* article, Morrison and Milliken argued that employees often know about problems but do not tell those above them, and that this silence can become a collective, organization-wide norm rather than a string of individual choices.[^s2] They traced it to two roots at the top: managers' fear of negative feedback, especially from subordinates, and unspoken managerial beliefs that employees are self-interested, that senior leaders know best, and that agreement is healthy while dissent is a threat.[^s2] Those beliefs lead managers to exclude staff from decisions and bristle at bad news, which in turn makes employees less trusting and committed, seeming to confirm the original assumptions.[^s2] Employees then compare notes and conclude together that speaking up is pointless or dangerous, often from thin evidence such as one colleague's unexplained missed promotion after voicing dissent.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The model explains a failure every manager has met: problems surface late, leaders mistake quiet for consensus, and errors grow because no one brings the bad news.[^s2] Morrison and Milliken argued that silence also costs the people who keep it, through lost sense of control, stress and withdrawal, and that it falls hardest on employees who differ from the majority.[^s2] Their advice was to seek honest feedback regularly, not to punish the messenger, back statements that concerns are welcome with visible action, reward people who raise risky information, and offer anonymous channels.[^s2]

A workplace illustration: a project lead holds a monthly review where the director rebuts every warning about the schedule. After a few months the team stops raising risks, and the director reads the calm as confidence. Applying Morrison's lens, the director first asks herself what her reactions have taught the team, then opens each review by asking for the biggest risk nobody has mentioned and thanks whoever names it before discussing it.[^s2][^s5]

## Evidence

With Milliken and Patricia Hewlin, Morrison tested the model in interviews with 40 employees: 85% recalled feeling unable to raise an important issue with someone above them, the most common reasons were fear of being labelled negatively, fear of damaging relationships and a sense of futility, and 74% of those who had stayed silent believed colleagues felt the same.[^s3] The authors flagged the small, young sample and reliance on recall as limits.[^s3] A large test of the model across 24 countries in one multinational (136,018 respondents) found that formal involvement schemes and a participative climate were each linked to less opinion withholding, though in high power-distance countries formal schemes helped only when the climate was also participative; the data were correlational.[^s4]

Later work by Morrison refined the picture. With Kelly See and Caitlin Pan she reported across three studies that a personal sense of low power drives silence, an effect that weakens when the supervisor is seen as open to input.[^s5] By her 2023 review she noted that hundreds of studies on voice and silence had appeared in a decade while key questions about the construct remained open, a reminder that the field's findings are still developing and largely survey-based.[^s6]

## Connections

Morrison, with Milliken, **introduced employee silence**, under the name organizational silence, in their 2000 model of how silence becomes a collective norm.[^s2] Her model **influenced Huang, Van de Vliert and Van der Vegt (2005)**, who built their cross-national test of structural and climate-based voice mechanisms directly on it.[^s4] She is related to **Milliken, Morrison and Hewlin 2003**, her own interview study of what employees withhold and why,[^s3] to **employee voice**, the behaviour her later reviews pair with silence,[^s6] to **power distance**, which shapes how well participation schemes reduce silence,[^s4] and to **psychological safety**, **upward dissent**, **implicit voice theories** and **managing up**, neighbouring ideas about when it feels safe and worthwhile to speak to those above you.[^s5][^s6] Her work sits within **organizational communication** as the study of upward information flow.[^s2]
