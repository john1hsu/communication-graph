# RFC 1855

RFC 1855, titled "Netiquette Guidelines" and also numbered FYI 28, is an informational document published by the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) in October 1995. Written by Sally Hambridge of Intel, it set out a minimum set of courtesy rules for one-to-one messages (email and the real-time "talk" program), one-to-many forums (mailing lists and Usenet newsgroups) and information services, in short bullets that organisations could adopt and adapt as their own policy.[^s1] It is not an Internet standard; its authority came from being the first etiquette guide produced through the IETF's own consensus process.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The guide was the sole deliverable of the IETF's Responsible Use of the Network (RUN) working group, chaired by Hambridge and Gary Malkin, whose charter recorded a need for an etiquette guide reflecting the Internet community's needs.[^s2] The charter set milestones for a literature bibliography in late 1994, a draft guide in early 1995 and submission as an FYI RFC in mid-1995.[^s2] The finished document says it was written for "newbies" - the wave of people then arriving online without knowing its culture - and closes with a bibliography of about 28 earlier sources it drew on, which is why it reads as a codification of existing Usenet and email custom rather than an invention.[^s1] It also tells readers to check local rules, noting that employers may own the email their staff send.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Many norms that workplace writing guides still repeat appear in RFC 1855 in compact form: all capitals read as shouting; subject lines should say what the message is about; drop people from the cc line once a thread becomes a two-person exchange; be brief without being terse; quote only the relevant part of the message you reply to; and assume a message may be read by people you did not intend, so write nothing you would not put on a postcard.[^s1] It explicitly framed one-to-one text as harder than speech because tone and body language must be inferred, and warned that recipients differ in language, culture and sense of humour, so sarcasm, idioms and date formats travel badly.[^s1] For emotional messages it advised waiting overnight before replying and not answering a flame at all.[^s1]

Its short "talk" section is one of the earliest written etiquettes for real-time one-to-one chat. It noted that a chat request interrupts the other person, so it should be used only when appropriate and not with strangers; that a blank line should signal the end of a turn; that sessions should close with a goodbye; and that typos usually need not be corrected because the meaning comes through.[^s1] The interruption point is the same concern behind modern workplace chat norms about self-contained messages and sparing alerts.[^s1]

The broader idea it formalised - netiquette as a community-enforced code that keeps online spaces pleasant and efficient and reduces conflict, with norms such as lurking before posting, not flaming and not typing in capitals - became general Internet vocabulary.[^s3]

## Using it today

Read RFC 1855 as a historical baseline, not a rulebook. Several rules are tied to 1995 technology: lines under 65 characters, MIME-encoding attachments, signatures of about four lines because connection time cost money, and files under roughly 50 KB.[^s1] The durable core is about the reader: know whom you are sending to, make the subject and content match, keep it short, respect time zones by not assuming silence means a lost message, and stay calm in writing.[^s1] A team writing its own email or chat norms can use the guide's original invitation literally - start from a minimal list and adapt it to local needs.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

RFC 1855 is consensus guidance, not a tested intervention, so it is rated `historical`. Later research gives partial support to its premise that courtesy conventions change how messages land: in a 2017 online survey, Ramon Bartl had 105 participants rate four bad-news business emails from a superior or a subordinate, each in a plain and a netiquette-enhanced version; the courteous versions were rated as clearer, left readers less uncertain and more satisfied, and made the sender seem more likeable.[^s4] That is a single vignette study with self-reported ratings, so it supports the general direction rather than any specific rule in the RFC.[^s4] The guide also reflects one community at one moment - mostly technical, English-speaking users of the mid-1990s - so its sense of what counts as rude may not match every culture or workplace.[^s1]

## Connections

RFC 1855 is **authored by** Sally Hambridge, credited on the document and as co-chair of the working group that produced it.[^s1][^s2] It **introduces** a written etiquette for real-time one-to-one chat in its "talk" guidelines, an early origin for what is now chat etiquette, though workplace chat norms have since grown from many sources.[^s1] It is **applied in** email, its main subject, and in instant messaging through those talk rules.[^s1] Its advice to use smileys sparingly links it to emoji and tone in text and to Scott Fahlman's emoticon, its overnight-wait rule echoes a cooling-off period, and its "know your audience" line connects to audience analysis.[^s1] It sits under digital communication, alongside Ray Tomlinson's invention of network email, the medium it set out to civilise.[^s1][^s3]
