# Michael Argyle

Michael Argyle was a British social psychologist at the University of Oxford who did more than almost anyone to turn eye contact, facial expression, posture and tone of voice into subjects of careful study, and to argue that the way people handle a conversation is a skill they can learn and improve.[^s3][^s2] His obituarist called him one of Britain's best-known and most productive academic psychologists, with 44 books written or edited.[^s1] Much of today's workplace advice on eye contact, listening signals and "reading the room" rests on research questions he framed.[^s7][^s3]

## Origin

Argyle was born in Nottingham in 1925, served as a navigator in the Royal Air Force during the Second World War, and studied moral sciences and experimental psychology at Cambridge.[^s1] He joined Oxford as a lecturer in 1952 and stayed for his whole career, becoming Reader in Social Psychology in 1969 and a founding fellow of Wolfson College; he died in 2002.[^s1] His stated interests were social skills and competence, nonverbal communication, happiness and the psychology of religion.[^s1]

## Key ideas

**Eye contact as regulated intimacy.** In 1965 Argyle and Janet Dean published "Eye-contact, distance and affiliation", later listed as a citation classic.[^s1] It set out what became known as equilibrium theory, or affiliative conflict theory: people feel competing pulls towards closeness and towards privacy, and settle on a comfortable level of intimacy that gaze helps to express.[^s5] Because gaze, distance, smiling and topic all signal intimacy, the theory predicts that when one goes up, people compensate with another, for example by standing further back from someone who holds their gaze.[^s4][^s5]

**Gaze has jobs.** With Mark Cook, Argyle wrote *Gaze and Mutual Gaze* (1976), which a Lancaster University glossary describes as a seminal survey of what mutual gaze does for adults and how its use differs between cultures.[^s7] That functional view (gaze signals attention and attitude, and helps manage conversation) is the basis for treating eye contact as a skill rather than a fixed habit.[^s7][^s3]

**The body speaks alongside words.** His book *Bodily Communication* covered gaze, faces, voice, gesture, posture, touch, space and appearance, plus cultural differences, social skills and persuasion; its 1988 second edition added a chapter on social skills.[^s3] The publisher notes that nonverbal communication had by then become part of professional training in social work, education and management.[^s3]

**Social behaviour is a skill.** Argyle described social behaviour with a social skill model, covering how people send verbal and nonverbal signals, how listeners read them for emotion and personality, the common errors in judging others, and how these skills can be trained.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Argyle's framing gives a practical stance for everyday work conversations: if gaze, distance and tone are signals, you can notice and adjust them. A manager who sits beside, rather than across from, a nervous new hire during a hard one-to-one is using the compensation idea, reducing one intimacy signal so that steady eye contact feels supportive rather than intense.[^s4][^s5] The skill model also explains why communication can be coached: you can observe someone's signals, give feedback and rehearse.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and limits

Equilibrium theory has been tested many times and is grouped with arousal-based and affect-based models as some of the best-known accounts of how people adapt to each other in conversation.[^s5] Support is real but partial. In a 2001 virtual-reality study of 50 students, women kept more distance from a virtual person who made eye contact, as the theory predicts, while men's distance did not clearly follow the agent's gaze.[^s4] Other work found intimacy signals moving together rather than offsetting: in a 1975 study of seven-year-olds sharing jokes, children seated closer made more eye contact and laughed and smiled more, which the author took as evidence against the model's assumption of a fixed intimacy level.[^s6] Later theories of interpersonal adaptation tried to explain when people match each other and when they compensate.[^s5] Read Argyle's ideas as useful starting hypotheses that depend on the relationship, the situation and culture, not as rules.[^s7][^s4]

## Connections

- **Introduced** equilibrium (affiliative conflict) theory, with Janet Dean in 1965, to explain how gaze relates to other signs of intimacy.[^s5][^s1]
- **Introduced** the modern study of eye contact and mutual gaze as a social skill, through his 1965 paper and *Gaze and Mutual Gaze*; he did not discover eye contact, but popularised studying it.[^s7][^s1]
- **Authored** *Bodily Communication*, a broad survey of nonverbal signals that Robert Rosenthal praised as an introduction to the whole field.[^s3]
- **Related:** gaze and eye contact and camera-level gaze, where his functional view applies; proxemics, the distance half of equilibrium theory; nonverbal communication more broadly; and the Mehrabian myth, a reminder that nonverbal findings are often over-generalised.[^s3][^s4]
