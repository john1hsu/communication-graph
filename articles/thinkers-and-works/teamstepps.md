# TeamSTEPPS

TeamSTEPPS, short for Team Strategies and Tools to Enhance Performance and Patient Safety, is a free training curriculum that teaches teams how to communicate under pressure: how to brief before work starts, check that a message landed, and speak up when something looks wrong.[^s1][^s2] It was built for hospitals, but its tools are plain communication routines, which is why they keep turning up in office guidance on escalation, status updates and challenging a senior colleague.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

The U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) and the Department of Defense developed TeamSTEPPS together through a multi-year research and development project and released it around 2006 as a proposed national standard for healthcare team training.[^s1] Its designers drew on aviation crew resource management (CRM), the cockpit training that taught flight crews to share information and challenge errors regardless of rank, which the Department of Defense had adapted for clinical teams.[^s4] AHRQ's own account of the two-challenge rule says human factors experts first created it to help airline captains avoid disasters when skilled decision makers have momentary lapses.[^s3]

## What it contains

The curriculum is organised around four teamwork skills - team leadership, situation monitoring, mutual support and communication - each with a small set of named tools.[^s2][^s4]

- **Leadership:** briefs before a shift or task, huddles to re-establish shared awareness mid-stream, and debriefs afterwards on what went well and what to change.[^s2]
- **Situation monitoring:** structured checklists for scanning status, team members, environment and progress.[^s2]
- **Communication:** SBAR (situation, background, assessment, recommendation), call-outs, check-backs that close the loop, and structured handoffs.[^s2]
- **Mutual support:** task assistance, feedback, advocacy and assertion, the DESC script, CUS phrases ("I am concerned... I am uncomfortable... this is a safety issue") and the two-challenge rule.[^s2]

The two-challenge rule says to voice a concern firmly at least twice so you know it was heard; the person challenged must acknowledge it, and if it is still unresolved you go to a supervisor or further up the chain and tell the team you have asked for more input.[^s3] AHRQ limits it to situations where someone could be seriously harmed, and treats "stopping the line" as a rare, serious step.[^s3]

## Why it matters

For anyone outside healthcare, TeamSTEPPS is the best-documented source of the graded speaking-up scripts that workplace guidance now borrows: CUS and the two-challenge rule give a junior person agreed words that escalate in strength, which lowers the personal cost of challenging someone senior.[^s2][^s3] Its rollout also shows how widely a communication curriculum can travel. By 2017 its developers estimated that tens of thousands of people had been trained worldwide, AHRQ had trained more than 3,500 master trainers between 2008 and 2010, and roughly 35% of U.S. healthcare workers had been exposed to it.[^s1]

**Example.** A project analyst sees that a vendor contract is about to be signed with the wrong liability cap. *Before:* "Sorry, this might be nothing, but should someone maybe look at clause 9?" *After, in TeamSTEPPS style:* "I'm concerned - clause 9 caps liability at $50K, not the $500K we agreed. I'm uncomfortable signing until legal confirms. Can we hold for an hour?" If the deal lead waves it off, the analyst says it a second time and then takes it to the legal director, telling the lead they are doing so.[^s2][^s3] The rewrite names the concern, escalates in agreed steps and keeps the challenge open rather than letting it drop.

## Evidence

The strongest support is for team training in general rather than for TeamSTEPPS specifically. A 2016 meta-analysis in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* found that healthcare team training improved trainee reactions, learning, transfer to the job and organisational results, with effect sizes from about d = .37 to .89, largely regardless of who was trained or the work setting; the data fitted a chain in which learning leads to transfer, which leads to results.[^s5] TeamSTEPPS' own developers acknowledge that this meta-analysis covered team training broadly, and that early on there was little research linking TeamSTEPPS to patient outcomes.[^s1]

Program-specific studies are mostly before-and-after designs. A 2014 review of 16 peer-reviewed TeamSTEPPS studies reported that most found better patient outcomes, such as fewer falls or less perinatal harm, but noted few used simulation and few covered general wards; the same author's own quasi-experiment found positive but non-significant trends.[^s4] A 2021 Norwegian surgical-ward study found improvements in three safety-culture and three teamwork dimensions after 12 months, while its authors cautioned that the small single-site design could not establish cause.[^s6] The developers also report that the hardest part is not teaching the tools but changing and sustaining team culture, and that physician champions mattered for adoption.[^s1] Treat the tools as well-designed practice with encouraging but uncontrolled evidence, not as proven causes of better outcomes.[^s1][^s4][^s6]

## Connections

- **Introduces** graded assertiveness in its best-known workplace form: the CUS phrases, advocacy and assertion, and the two-challenge rule sit in TeamSTEPPS' mutual-support toolkit.[^s2][^s3]
- **Influenced by** crew resource management in aviation, which the Department of Defense adapted into healthcare team training.[^s4]
- **Supported by** Hughes and colleagues' 2016 meta-analysis of healthcare team training, which found gains at every evaluation level.[^s5]
- **Supported by**, more weakly, Aaberg and colleagues' 2021 surgical-ward evaluation.[^s6]
- **Related:** closed-loop communication and checking understanding (its check-back tool); the DESC script (part of its mutual-support module); escalation communication, employee silence and upward dissent (the problem its speaking-up tools target); after-action reviews (its debriefs); and Eduardo Salas, whose team-training research is part of the same tradition.[^s2][^s5]
