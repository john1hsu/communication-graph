# David M. Clark

David M. Clark is a British clinical psychologist and Chair of Experimental Psychology at the University of Oxford, where he founded the Oxford Centre for Anxiety Disorders and Trauma.[^s1] He developed cognitive-behavioural treatments for panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder and health anxiety.[^s2] For communication skills his lasting contribution is an explanation of why the fear of being judged keeps people quiet in meetings, interviews and presentations, and a practical way out of it: drop the self-protective habits and turn attention back to the conversation.[^s3][^s6]

## Origin

Clark's social-anxiety work grew out of a collaborative Oxford research group. In 1995, Adrian Wells, Clark, Paul Salkovskis and colleagues reported a small experiment with eight socially anxious patients: each had one session of plain exposure to a feared situation and one in which they also cut back the "safety behaviours" they used to avoid embarrassment.[^s3] Exposure with fewer safety behaviours produced larger drops in anxiety and in belief that the feared disaster would happen, which the authors offered as a reason why repeated exposure alone often fails socially anxious people.[^s3] The same year Clark and Wells published their cognitive model of social anxiety, which later practitioners summarise as two linked claims: anxious people judge how they come across from how anxious they feel, and their safety behaviours can make them look less engaged to others.[^s6]

## Key ideas

The model became a treatment, cognitive therapy for social anxiety disorder, and its signature exercise is directly usable at work. Early in therapy the person holds two short conversations: one while self-focused and using their usual safety behaviours, and one while focused on the other person with those behaviours dropped.[^s6] Most people feel less anxious and believe they came across better in the second, and video feedback is then used so they compare their felt impression with how they actually looked.[^s6] The practical lessons are that self-monitoring is itself part of the problem, that feelings are an unreliable guide to how you appear, and that protective habits such as scripting, staying quiet or avoiding eye contact need testing rather than tolerating.[^s3][^s6]

## Why it matters

The treatment has strong trial support. In a 2006 randomised trial of 62 patients, 84% of those given cognitive therapy no longer met criteria for social phobia afterwards, compared with 42% given exposure plus applied relaxation and none on the wait-list, with differences holding at one year.[^s4] A 2014 network meta-analysis of 101 trials (13,164 participants), which Clark co-authored, found individual cognitive-behavioural therapy had the largest effect of any psychological treatment and recommended it as the first-line option.[^s5] Beyond the clinic, Clark is described as a principal architect of England's Improving Access to Psychological Therapies programme, now NHS Talking Therapies, which reports treating more than 550,000 people a year and publishing outcomes for nearly all of them.[^s2] His honours include fellowship of the British Academy and an American Psychological Association award for distinguished scientific application of psychology.[^s1]

For a workplace reader, this is why the graph's advice on speaking anxiety does not stop at "practise more": exposure helps most when people also stop hiding, and judge the result by others' reactions or a recording rather than by their own nerves.[^s3][^s6]

## Limits

The founding 1995 experiment was a single-case series of only eight patients, so it showed a promising mechanism rather than settled proof.[^s3] Much of the supporting evidence, including the 2006 trial and the 2014 meta-analysis, comes from teams that include Clark, so independent replication matters when weighing it.[^s4][^s5] The work was designed for a clinical disorder; applying its exercises to everyday nerves before a presentation is a reasonable extension, but persistent, impairing social anxiety calls for a qualified clinician.[^s6] The idea of safety behaviours was also a group product, with Wells and Salkovskis as co-authors, so crediting Clark alone would overstate his role.[^s3]

## Connections

Clark **introduced**, with colleagues, the practice of [[dropping-safety-behaviours]], first tested in the 1995 Wells-Clark experiment.[^s3] He co-created the **clark-wells-cognitive-model** that explains [[social-anxiety]] and the **cognitive-therapy-for-social-anxiety-disorder** built on it.[^s4][^s6] His therapy uses video feedback in the same way as [[self-recording-playback]], to check felt impressions against reality.[^s6] The work bears on [[speaking-anxiety]], [[communication-apprehension]] and the [[spotlight-effect]], and sits beside [[anxiety-reappraisal]] as an evidence-based response to the fear of being judged.[^s3][^s5]
