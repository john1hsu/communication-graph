# Rebecca Hincks

Rebecca Hincks is a speech and language researcher at KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm, where, as of 2009, she taught technical and scientific English as an associate professor in the Unit for Language and Communication.[^s3] Her work asks a practical question every presenter faces: which measurable features of the voice make a talk sound engaging, and can software help a speaker change them? She defined a simple measure of pitch variation, showed that listeners' sense of a speaker's liveliness follows it, and with Jens Edlund tested a live pitch meter for presentation practice.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

Hincks began with speech recognition for pronunciation teaching. In a 2003 *ReCALL* study, 11 immigrant engineers in Sweden used a commercial speech-recognition program at home during a 200-hour technical English course; compared with 15 classmates without it, the program helped those with strong accents but did little for those who already pronounced English well.[^s4] Her 2005 KTH doctoral thesis, *Computer support for learners of spoken English*, then turned to presentation speech, analysing 28 ten-minute student presentations.[^s1] There she defined the pitch variation quotient (PVQ): the standard deviation of a speaker's pitch over 10-second stretches, normalised for the speaker's average pitch so that voices of different heights can be compared.[^s1] PVQ ranged from 6% to 34% across samples, and the thesis closed by proposing a "speech checker" feedback tool for rehearsing presentations, for native and non-native speakers alike.[^s1]

A companion Interspeech paper tested whether the measure matches what listeners hear. Eight judges rated the liveliness of 18 Swedish students presenting in English; ratings tracked pitch variation strongly, and for women also tracked how many syllables they spoke between pauses.[^s2] The thesis reports an overall correlation of .83 between PVQ and perceived liveliness.[^s1]

In 2009 Hincks and KTH speech technologist Jens Edlund built that feedback idea into a meter that lit up as pitch variation rose above a learner's own baseline, with lights that flashed and vanished rather than leaving a graph to study.[^s3] Fourteen Chinese engineering students practised sentences from their own talks; those using the meter raised pitch variation more than a control group who only listened to their recordings, and the gain carried into a later presentation given without feedback.[^s3] The authors placed this within earlier work on visual pitch displays for pronunciation learners, going back at least to de Bot in 1983, so her contribution was to aim the method at presentation delivery rather than to invent visual feedback.[^s3]

## Why it matters

Hincks gave workplace speakers two usable ideas. First, "sounding flat" is measurable: listeners' impressions of liveliness follow how much the pitch moves, which is why acoustic coaching tools now report pitch variation alongside pace and fillers.[^s2][^s1] Second, she framed the tool as a rehearsal aid that compares you with your own baseline, not with someone else's voice, and drills your own sentences.[^s3] She was also candid about limits: more pitch movement does not prove a better presenter, the meter cannot tell whether emphasis lands on the right word, and expert raters in the 2009 study heard only a slight, statistically untested gain in liveliness.[^s3]

Her later work matters for anyone presenting in a second language or to an international audience. In a 2010 *English for Specific Purposes* study, 14 Swedish engineering students gave the same talk in English and Swedish; they spoke about 23% more slowly in English and, for the same amount of speaking time, conveyed less information.[^s5] She drew practical lessons: non-native presenters need more time or less content, native speakers should learn to slow down, and feedback tools could warn speakers who talk too fast.[^s5] The paper won the TESOL 2010 Heinle Award for Distinguished Research.[^s6]

## Connections

Hincks is credited here with introducing acoustic speech-analysis coaching to presentation training, through the 2009 meter study with Edlund (`cm:hincks-edlund-2009`) and the speech-checker proposal in her thesis; confidence is medium because visual pitch feedback for language learners predates her.[^s3][^s1] She introduced the pitch variation quotient and authored the thesis *Computer support for learners of spoken English*.[^s1] Her liveliness study (`cm:hincks-2005`) is evidence for vocal delivery and against monotone delivery in presentations, the delivery problems her tools target, all part of paralanguage.[^s2][^s3] Her lingua franca research links that work to intercultural communication, since speaking rate and content load change when a presenter switches language.[^s5]
