# Edward T. Hall

Edward T. Hall was an American cultural anthropologist who is usually credited with founding intercultural communication as a practical discipline.[^s1][^s6] His central claim was that much of what goes wrong between people from different cultures happens below the level of words: in how close they stand, how they treat time and how much meaning they leave unsaid.[^s1][^s3] For anyone who works with colleagues, clients or suppliers abroad, he is the source of several ideas that still frame cross-cultural advice, and of the habit of training people for those encounters rather than simply briefing them.[^s2][^s6]

## Origin

Hall was born in Webster Groves, Missouri, in 1914, studied at the University of Denver and the University of Arizona, and took a PhD in anthropology at Columbia University; his fieldwork ranged from Pueblo communities in the American Southwest to Europe and the Middle East.[^s4] He served in the U.S. Army in Europe and the Philippines during the Second World War.[^s3] After the Foreign Service Act of 1946 reorganized the diplomatic service and created the Foreign Service Institute (FSI) to train its officials, Hall joined the FSI and helped build its curriculum.[^s2][^s6] In the 1950s he directed the State Department's training of technicians and administrators bound for overseas posts under the Point Four programme.[^s3][^s4] He later taught at institutions including Bennington College, Harvard Business School, the Illinois Institute of Technology and Northwestern University, consulted for architects and for business and government on intercultural relations, and lived in Santa Fe, New Mexico, until his death in 2009.[^s4]

Leeds-Hurwitz's history of the FSI argues that the diplomats' needs changed what was taught: instead of broad profiles of national character, Hall's group analysed everyday interaction, studied nonverbal messages such as space, time, tone of voice and gesture, and borrowed a linguistic model so that cultural patterns could be taught like the patterns of a language.[^s1] Hall used role plays in this training.[^s2] He set these ideas out for a general readership in *The Silent Language* (1959), *The Hidden Dimension* (1966), which developed proxemics, and *Beyond Culture* (1976); later books included *The Dance of Life* and, with Mildred Reed Hall, *Hidden Differences*.[^s3][^s4]

## Why it matters

Hall's practical legacy is the idea that culture can be observed and learned as patterns of behaviour, so people can be prepared for specific encounters instead of being told to "be sensitive".[^s1][^s6] Proxemics, for example, describes intimate, personal, social and public distances; Brown's summary of *The Hidden Dimension* notes that comfortable conversational distance differs between the United States and parts of Europe.[^s3] Today's FSI still offers courses on communicating across cultures, although one diplomat-training advocate argues the field has flourished more in universities than in day-to-day diplomatic preparation.[^s6]

A workplace illustration: a project lead in Chicago is puzzled that a partner team abroad never says no to a deadline in meetings, then misses it. Read through Hall's lens, she would look less at the words and more at context, timing and silences, and test her reading by asking in a private follow-up which parts of the plan feel risky, rather than taking public agreement as commitment.[^s1][^s2] The lesson to keep is the observation-and-rehearsal approach, not a fixed label for the partner's country.[^s5]

## Limits

Hall's categories were built from observation and anecdote, and textbooks note that they invite cultural stereotypes, however useful.[^s2] A 2011 systematic review of research using his high-/low-context concept found that most country classifications rested on less-than-adequate evidence, that findings were mixed and often contradictory, and that the conventional rankings were flawed or at best very limited.[^s5] Treat his ideas as questions to ask about a particular team, not predictions about individuals.[^s2][^s5]

## Connections

Hall **introduced** `cm:cross-cultural-training`: his FSI work answered the mandate to train diplomats for intercultural contact and set the template of pattern-based, practice-heavy preparation.[^s1] He is also credited with founding `cm:intercultural-communication` and naming `cm:proxemics`, `cm:high-and-low-context-cultures` and `cm:monochronic-and-polychronic-time`, all of which point to him in this graph.[^s1][^s3] He **authored** *The Silent Language* (`cm:the-silent-language`), *The Hidden Dimension* (`cm:the-hidden-dimension`), *Beyond Culture* (`cm:beyond-culture`) and *The Dance of Life* (`cm:the-dance-of-life`).[^s3][^s4] His focus on space, time and tone links him to `cm:nonverbal-communication` and `cm:paralanguage`, and his high-/low-context scheme is critically reviewed in `cm:kittler-rygl-mackinnon-2011` and popularised for managers in `cm:the-culture-map`.[^s1][^s5]
