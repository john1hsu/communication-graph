# Improved Tactical Engagement Simulation Training Techniques

*Improved Tactical Engagement Simulation Training Techniques: Two Training Programs for the Conduct of After Action Reviews* is a January 1979 research product of the U.S. Army Research Institute for the Behavioral and Social Sciences (ARI), written by John J. Bosley and Peter W. J. Onoszko of Human Sciences Research, Inc. and Claramae S. Knerr and Robert H. Sulzen of ARI.[^s1] The Army's own history of the method calls it the first ARI document written to guide leaders in conducting after-action reviews (AARs).[^s2] It has two parts: a self-study guide for the person leading a review, and a programme that lets a unit's training officer teach other leaders through classroom sessions, short role-plays, observation of real reviews and co-leading.[^s1]

## Origin

By the early 1970s the Army broadly agreed that its traditional training critique - an umpire lecturing a unit on subjective estimates of how it had done - was ineffective, often negative and resented.[^s2] ARI built "engagement simulation" exercises in which casualties were assessed objectively, first SCOPES for infantry squads and then REALTRAIN for armour and combined arms, demonstrated at Fort Benning in 1973 and adopted by Army training command from 1974.[^s3] The 1975 REALTRAIN report already described a post-exercise review in which each side explained what it saw, insisted it was not a critique, and found most participants rated it valuable.[^s3] Bosley and colleagues watched those REALTRAIN reviews and turned what worked into a teachable method, drawing on the non-punitive, fact-gathering spirit of S. L. A. Marshall's interviews after combat.[^s1][^s2] Later guides - an NTC handbook in 1982 and Training Circular 25-20 in 1993 - carried the method into Army doctrine.[^s2][^s4]

## What it says

The guide casts the leader as a moderator who keeps a low profile, lets participants supply the facts and never hands down "approved solutions".[^s1] Preparation comes first: the leader learns the exercise's objectives, and a controller debrief reconciles conflicting observers' accounts into one accurate record of events before anyone faces the unit.[^s1] The review opens by explaining its purpose, the process and the leader's role, then asks the unit leaders to restate their mission and initial plan, before walking through key events in time order with people from both sides describing what they actually saw.[^s1] It closes with a short recap of turning points, built with the participants rather than delivered as a lecture.[^s1]

Its most durable contribution is a list of eight leader skills: pacing, attending closely to each speaker, keeping reports accurate, holding a no-punishment climate, asking open-ended questions, keeping a low profile, managing the group, and building summaries that link separate events into a pattern of cause and effect.[^s1] It also names the traps: sliding back into a one-way critique, injecting opinions, finishing people's sentences, guessing at others' intentions, letting rebuttals or excuses take over, pulling rank, and dwelling only on mistakes when effective actions deserve discussion too.[^s1]

## Why it matters

Most workplace debriefs, retrospectives and project reviews descend from the Army AAR, and this guide is where its facilitation craft was first written down as skills a leader can learn and practise.[^s1][^s2] Its central move - agreeing the plan and the facts before anyone discusses causes, and making the facilitator a questioner rather than a judge - is what separates a learning conversation from a blame session.[^s1][^s4] A manager running a sprint retrospective can apply it directly: reconcile the timeline with the people who were there before the meeting, open by restating what the team set out to do, ask "what did you see at that point?" rather than "why did you do that?", and redirect excuses back to observations.[^s1]

## Evidence and limits

The guide is practitioner doctrine, not a tested intervention: its skills came from observing reviews, and the REALTRAIN results that inspired it rested on participant opinion and a very small performance sample rather than controlled comparison.[^s3] Later research supports the broader practice - a 2013 meta-analysis of 46 samples (2,136 people) found debriefs improved performance by about a quarter over controls (d = 0.67), with facilitation and structure as possible moderators.[^s5] But a 2007 ARI field study at the Joint Readiness Training Center found that even experienced facilitators who knew the steps often slid back into lecture and critique, asked yes/no questions or answered their own questions, which suggests the facilitation skills are harder to sustain than to describe.[^s4]

## Connections

The work **introduces** the **after-action review** (`cm:after-action-review`) as a taught method: it is the first ARI leader's guide to it, though the practice itself first appeared in the SCOPES and REALTRAIN exercises of 1973-75, so the edge carries medium confidence.[^s2][^s3] It is **authored by** John J. Bosley (`cm:john-j-bosley`), Peter W. J. Onoszko (`cm:peter-w-j-onoszko`), Claramae S. Knerr (`cm:claramae-s-knerr`) and Robert H. Sulzen (`cm:robert-h-sulzen`).[^s1] It is **influenced by** the **interview after combat** (`cm:interview-after-combat`) of **S. L. A. Marshall** (`cm:s-l-a-marshall`), the source of the review's objective, non-punitive character.[^s2] Its leader skills overlap with **facilitation** (`cm:facilitation`), **open-ended questions** (`cm:open-ended-questions`), **summarizing** (`cm:summarizing`) and **active listening** (`cm:active-listening`), and its no-punishment climate anticipates **psychological safety** (`cm:psychological-safety`) and guards against **defensiveness** (`cm:defensiveness`).[^s1] It sits beside the Army's other communication doctrine in this graph, **FM 6-0** (`cm:fm-6-0`) and **Effective Writing for Army Leaders** (`cm:effective-writing-for-army-leaders`), and its rejection of the one-way critique is an early statement of developmental **feedback** (`cm:feedback`).[^s1][^s2]
