# Rob Buckman

Rob Buckman (1948-2011) was an English-born medical oncologist, author and broadcaster who spent most of his career in Toronto, at Princess Margaret Hospital and the University of Toronto, with a cross-appointment at the M. D. Anderson Cancer Center in Texas.[^s1][^s4][^s3] Communicators remember him for one idea: that telling someone unwelcome news is not a gift some people have and others lack, but a skill with steps that can be taught, practised and checked.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Buckman qualified in medicine at Cambridge in 1972, where he also performed comedy with the Footlights, and trained in oncology at the Royal Marsden Hospital in London before moving to Canada in 1985.[^s4] Alongside clinical work he wrote more than a dozen books, presented television series and made dozens of humorous medical-information videos with John Cleese.[^s4][^s3]

His landmark book, *How to Break Bad News: A Guide for Health Care Professionals* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1992, with contributions from Yvonne Kason), grew out of his award-winning training videos.[^s1] Its starting point was that medical education did almost nothing to prepare people for these conversations, and it offered a set of basic communication principles plus a six-step protocol that could be learned quickly.[^s1] The approach centred on finding out what the listener already knows and wants to know, understanding their agenda, and responding to their feelings, and it also covered the reactions of family and colleagues.[^s1]

Working with psychiatrist Walter Baile at M. D. Anderson, Buckman refined the steps into the mnemonic SPIKES - Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Emotions (Empathy), Strategy and summary - which he presented at an American Society of Clinical Oncology education session in 1998 and which the pair published in 2000.[^s5][^s2] They also built a CD-ROM series of simulated interviews for teaching the method, still used in M. D. Anderson's communication training after his death.[^s3][^s5]

## Key ideas

**Bad news is measured by the gap.** Buckman defined bad news as anything that seriously and adversely changes a person's view of their future, so how bad it feels depends on the distance between what they expected and what is true.[^s2] That is why his core rule was "Before you tell, ask": find out what the person already believes before delivering anything.[^s2][^s5]

**Deliver in small pieces.** Warn that difficult news is coming, avoid jargon, give the information in chunks and check understanding as you go.[^s2]

**Respond to the emotion before moving on.** Identify the feeling, link it to its cause and acknowledge it; in his 1998 talk he advised addressing the strongest emotion in the room, including the messenger's own.[^s2][^s5]

**End with a plan.** Every conversation should close with a summary, agreed next steps and a chance for questions.[^s2][^s5] He presented SPIKES as a strategy to adapt, not a script to recite.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Buckman's framing travels well beyond the clinic. A manager announcing a cancelled project, a consultant telling a client the budget will overrun, or an HR partner explaining a restructuring faces the same problems he named: the urge to soften or conceal news to escape one's own discomfort, uncertainty about how much detail to give, and feeling helpless when nothing can be fixed.[^s2] Before cancelling a vendor contract, for example, a project lead can apply his rule by first asking the account manager how they think the pilot went, then stating the decision plainly, acknowledging the frustration, and ending with the wind-down timeline.[^s2]

He also argued that experience alone does not fix poor delivery, citing a randomized trial showing that senior oncologists still benefited from structured communication training.[^s2] At the 1998 session, fewer than 5% of about 400 polled oncologists reported any training in breaking bad news.[^s2]

## Evidence and limits

The teaching approach Buckman championed has measurable effects on the messenger. A 2018 meta-analysis of 17 studies (1,322 participants in the main analysis) found that bad-news training produced a large improvement in observer-rated delivery skill and a moderate gain in confidence.[^s6] Evidence that recipients end up better off is much thinner: only one included study reported effects on patients' depression or anxiety, and the authors call for research on outcomes, ideal components and length.[^s6] Workplace versions of his method are adaptations of clinical work rather than separately tested programmes, so treat the steps as well-reasoned practice rather than proven ROI.[^s6][^s2]

## Connections

- **Introduced** the modern teachable approach to delivering bad news, through his 1992 book and training videos.[^s1]
- **Introduced** (with Walter Baile) the SPIKES protocol, the best-known structure for these conversations.[^s2][^s5]
- **Authored** *How to Break Bad News* (1992).[^s1]
- **Related:** difficult conversations generally; active listening, which his perception and empathy steps depend on; and the MUM effect, the reluctance to pass on bad news that his methods are meant to overcome.[^s2]
