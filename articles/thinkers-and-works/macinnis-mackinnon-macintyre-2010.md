# The Illusion of Transparency and Normative Beliefs About Anxiety During Public Speaking

This 2010 paper by Cara MacInnis, Sean Mackinnon and Peter MacIntyre took a well-known laboratory finding, that nervous speakers think their anxiety shows far more than it does, and tested it in a real public-speaking class with real audiences.[^s1] The effect held: audiences rated speakers as markedly calmer than the speakers felt or expected to be seen. The paper's second contribution is a twist that matters for anyone coaching presenters: many speakers assume the audience sees *more* anxiety than they actually feel, because people expect public speaking to make everyone nervous.[^s1]

## Origin

The authors, then at Brock University, Wilfrid Laurier University and Cape Breton University, built on Gilovich, Savitsky and Medvec's 1998 studies, which showed that people overestimate how readable their lies and emotions are and traced this to anchoring on their own feelings and adjusting too little.[^s3][^s1] Savitsky and Gilovich had extended the idea to speech anxiety in 2003: in an experiment with Cornell students, speakers judged their nervousness as more visible than listeners did, and speakers told about the illusion beforehand gave talks that observers rated as more composed and better.[^s4] That 2003 study also left an anomaly, since speakers' guesses of how anxious they looked slightly exceeded how anxious they said they felt, which pure "leakage" of feelings cannot explain.[^s1] The 2010 paper set out to replicate the effect outside the lab and to explain the anomaly.[^s1] It appeared in *Current Research in Social Psychology*, a peer-reviewed open journal run by the University of Iowa's Center for the Study of Group Processes.[^s2]

## What the study did and found

In Study 1, students in an introductory public-speaking course gave short speeches to classmates, with audiences of about 15 people.[^s1] Speakers rated their own anxiety and how anxious they thought they appeared, using an adapted six-item speaking-anxiety scale, and every audience member rated each speaker on the same items.[^s1] Speakers' felt anxiety (mean 24.0) and their predicted audience ratings (23.3) were both well above the audience's actual ratings (17.3), and the more anxious a student was in general, the larger the gap (r = .37).[^s1] About half of speakers thought they hid some of their anxiety, but 36% believed the audience saw more than they felt, replicating the 2003 anomaly.[^s1]

Study 2 surveyed 183 students. They estimated that about half of people their age are extremely anxious when speaking publicly and fewer than a quarter feel no anxiety, and they rated the typical person as more anxious than themselves.[^s1] The authors propose that this shared "script", that speaking anxiety is normal and expected, feeds the starting anchor speakers use when guessing what the audience sees.[^s1]

## Why it matters

For a team member about to present to leadership, the practical message is that nerves feel louder from the inside than they look from the seats: peer audiences rated speakers nearly 30% less anxious than the speakers rated themselves.[^s1] Telling speakers about this gap before they present improved their performance in Savitsky and Gilovich's experiment, so it is a cheap, honest piece of reassurance for a manager or coach to give.[^s4] The normative-belief finding adds a second lever: if speakers assume everyone is visibly nervous, they may project anxiety onto the audience's view of them, and the authors warn this can become a self-reinforcing cycle in which feeling exposed raises anxiety further.[^s1]

A workplace example: before a quarterly review, an analyst says, "Everyone will see my hands shaking." A coach who replies, "Audiences consistently notice much less than speakers feel, so focus on your first slide's message," is applying the study accurately; promising "nobody will notice anything" overstates it.[^s1][^s4]

## Evidence and limits

The core finding replicates the direction of the earlier laboratory work in a more realistic setting, which strengthens confidence in the illusion of transparency for speaking anxiety.[^s1][^s3] It is rated mixed here rather than robust because the evidence is a single classroom study with modest, partly overlapping samples (66 students completed both key measures), no large multi-lab replication has been found, and the samples were mostly young university students.[^s1] The normative-script explanation is correlational and the authors call it preliminary; the design cannot show that the script causes the inflated predictions.[^s1] Study 2 also contains a reporting inconsistency, describing a non-parametric test but reporting a t statistic.[^s1]

## Connections

The study is **influenced by Savitsky and Gilovich's 2003 speech-anxiety experiment** (`cm:savitsky-gilovich-2003`), whose paradigm and anomaly it takes up, and by **Gilovich, Savitsky and Medvec's 1998 illusion-of-transparency studies** (`cm:gilovich-savitsky-medvec-1998`), which defined the effect.[^s1][^s3] Its results are **explained by anchoring and adjustment** (`cm:anchoring-and-adjustment`), with the authors adding that cultural beliefs about anxiety shape the anchor.[^s1] It is **studied in social psychology** (`cm:social-psychology`), the field of the journal that published it.[^s2] It sits under the **illusion of transparency** and **public speaking anxiety**, and connects to **Thomas Gilovich**, the **spotlight effect**, **anxiety reappraisal** as a complementary way to manage nerves, and **communication apprehension**, the broader trait its anxiety scale measures.[^s1][^s4]
