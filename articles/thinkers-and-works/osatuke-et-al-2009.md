# Civility, Respect, Engagement in the Workforce: Nationwide Organization Development Intervention at Veterans Health Administration

This 2009 article by Katerine Osatuke, Scott Moore, Christopher Ward, Sue Dyrenforth and Linda Belton, published in *The Journal of Applied Behavioral Science*, is where CREW first appeared in the research literature. It describes the background and rollout of the Veterans Health Administration's programme for raising civility inside workgroups and gives a first evaluation of whether it worked, using employees' ratings of the interpersonal climate in their own group as the yardstick.[^s1]

## Origin

CREW started in 2005, when top Department of Veterans Affairs leaders committed to improving the everyday tone of workplace interactions after several internal studies flagged civility as a problem; the VA's internal consulting office, the National Center for Organization Development (NCOD), designed the programme and ran it in the VHA.[^s2] The first pilot covered eight units at eight facilities, and by early 2010 more than 750 units at 150 facilities had taken part.[^s5] The 2009 paper documents the model the authors built: national leaders endorse it, hospital leaders adopt it, and local coordinators trained and supported by NCOD run it in their own workgroups.[^s1] Units were assessed with a short Civility Scale covering respect, cooperation, conflict resolution and discrimination, and trained unit "champions" led regular CREW meetings where staff could raise problems candidly.[^s5]

## What it found

The article reports two rounds of CREW across 23 sites. Civility rose significantly from before to after the intervention at the participating sites, while comparison sites showed no significant change in either round.[^s1] A later account by the same programme team summarised this first test as CREW groups improving markedly while matched control groups stayed flat over 12 months.[^s2]

## Why it matters

The paper reframed incivility as something a work group can change itself rather than a matter of individual discipline. CREW deliberately builds positive, civil behaviour inside a group and leaves individual problem cases, such as bullying, to normal human-resources processes.[^s5] Its designers later stressed that it works best when teams volunteer, and that it is not suited to highly dysfunctional groups or to problems rooted in poor leadership.[^s2] Those lessons carry over to any team trying to fix how people talk to each other: agree norms together, meet regularly, and measure the climate before and after.[^s1][^s2]

The paper also seeded independent work. Michael Leiter's team applied a modified version of the process in five Canadian hospitals, which produced the controlled evaluations most often cited for CREW today.[^s4] By 2014 the programme had run in more than 1,000 workplaces.[^s6]

## Limits

This is a preliminary evaluation by the programme's own designers, reported in an abstract without effect sizes, and the authors themselves noted that evaluation data on climate programmes are scarce and that the mechanisms of change still needed study.[^s1][^s2] Its outcome measure has also been questioned: a 2012 paper developing a brief civility-norms questionnaire described the eight-item Civility Scale as useful for evaluating CREW but noted that some items go beyond civility, into teamwork, acceptance of diversity and tolerance of discrimination, and that items mix workgroup and organisation levels.[^s3] A 2021 evidence summary found no meta-analysis of incivility interventions and noted that most incivility research is correlational, so CREW's support rests on a handful of primary studies.[^s6]

## Connections

The article **introduces** CREW (`cm:crew-civility-intervention`), giving the first published account of the programme and its evaluation, and it is **authored by** Katerine Osatuke (`cm:katerine-osatuke`) with VHA colleagues.[^s1] Its claim that the programme improves civility is **supported by** Leiter et al. 2011 (`cm:leiter-et-al-2011`), the Canadian adaptation that tested a modified CREW against comparison units,[^s4] and its measurement is **challenged by** Walsh et al. 2012 (`cm:walsh-et-al-2012`), which questioned what the Civility Scale actually captures.[^s3] It sits under **workplace incivility**, the problem it targets, and relates to **team communication norms**, **facilitation** and **organizational communication**, since CREW works through facilitated meetings in which a group agrees how its members will treat one another.[^s1][^s5]
